# 0027: The Museum — documents as 3D objects built from their records

**Context.** The archive's documents are physical things: victory steles, clay tablets and their envelopes, coins, papyri, sealed charters, newspapers, telegrams. On a page they are only text. A model you can turn, under a light that skims the surface, makes the "primary source" idea concrete, and is the most shareable picture of a single document. The danger is invention: a model must not show anything the record would contradict.

**Decision.**
- **Every model is generated from the artifact's page** (`atlas/src/three/museum/record.ts` → `ObjectSpec`), deterministically (a hash of the id, never `Math.random`):
  - The **medium** decides the kind of object and its material: basalt/diorite/limestone steles, rock reliefs, bricks, foundation tablets, plaster and statue-base graffiti, bronze/silver tablets, clay tablets (single, sets, in an envelope), wax diptychs, potsherds, coins, papyrus rolls, sealed charters, codices and printed books, sheets, newspapers and telegrams.
  - The **era** decides style: a Bronze Age stele has a rounded top, later ones a gable or a flat top; manuscripts get a red initial and two columns, printed books a title page, typescripts a monospaced face, newsprint its yellowed stock.
  - The **condition and the description** decide the damage. "The lower part of the stone is broken away" breaks the stone (it then stands on a steel mount); "a fracture runs through the final register" splits it in two; "the last lines are worn" softens them; "the final lines are lost" and every `[...]` lacuna spall the surface; fire chars paper edges (transparent) and blackens clay; water leaves tide-lines; a coin's wear, corrosion and an off-centre strike follow its grade.
  - **Record numbers decide size**: a coin's diameter comes from its weight, its fineness and the alloy's density; a stele's letters grow with the size of its boast; a tablet's size and a newspaper's format follow the length of their text.
  - **Colour from the state**: a stele's pigment, a book's dyed leather and a charter's wax seals take the colour of the issuer's or party's arms (read from `heraldry.ts`'s blazon); the seals carry the parties' arms, a clay envelope the author's seal rolled across it, an emblem coin the state's arms.
- **The text on an object is the record's own text**: a stone or bronze inscription carries its transliteration, as recorded (so the stele's boast — "260200" — can be read on the stone); a coin its legend, portrait type and reverse type; paper, parchment and metal the document's words in translation. Clay is written in wedges: one sign per syllable of the record's text, the same sign for the same syllable within a script (and numbers as numeral wedges), so a tablet's layout, lines and lacunae follow its text. The viewer says which of these it shows.
- **Surfaces are painted, not modelled**: each face is a canvas (albedo and a height map) turned into a normal map, on a low-poly mesh. A **raking light** toggle swings the key light down to skim the faces, so carved and stamped text stands out, as in an epigrapher's photograph.
- **Two ways in**: "View in 3D" on every artifact page with a physical object (`#/p/art:N?3d=1`, shareable; works "known only from quotations" have no object and no button), and **the Museum** (`#/museum`, `#/museum/art:N`): a rotunda with 26 celebrated documents on plinths, oldest first, each era painted on the wall above its objects. Visitors turn from one object to the next (drag, arrows, tap), step back for a cutaway overview, and open any object in the turntable viewer.
- **The selection is a rule over the record** (`engine/src/export/museum.ts` → `museum.json`, 17 KB gzip): the oldest dated document, the oldest inscription, letter and treaty, the first victory monument, the stele that invents the most enemy dead, the oldest letter still in its envelope, the first graffito, the oldest treaty in metal, a school exercise on a potsherd, the oldest papyrus, codex and paper book, the first coin and first gold coin, the most debased coin beside its own first issue, a wax tablet, the treaty that ended the deadliest war, the first sealed charter, the first patent, newspaper, printed book, telegram and typed treaty, and the newest document. Each item carries a trimmed copy of its page and the arms and seal it shows, so the hall needs no page shards.

**Why.**
- **Evidence first**: nothing on an object is free invention; where the record is silent (a script's sign shapes, a portrait's features) the choice is a pure function of the record's own values and the same everywhere.
- **Shareability and habit**: the hall gives a reason to return (it re-curates itself when the record changes), and the viewer turns any of 18,000 documents into an image worth posting.
- **Phones**: everything is lazy (the button is 0.6 KB in the page bundle; the viewer chunk is 34 KB gzip and the hall 7 KB on top of the shared three.js); the hall builds its objects one at a time after the room appears, at low texture resolution, and re-carves the one in front of the visitor sharper once they stop (at most three at a time); render on demand; frames stay well under 16 ms at 390×844.
