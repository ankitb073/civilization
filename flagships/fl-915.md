> I pray the gods to keep him, every god in his due place.
> I only pray the gods to teach him that smoke is not a fire.

In AY 3712 someone nailed ten stanzas of verse to the door of the priests' house at [[set:264|Ölye]], and someone else copied them before the steward's men tore them down. The copy, PAL·PO·00035, "Verses on Bright Smoke", mocks [[per:34201|Sitlyitlilyian]], emperor of [[pol:1371|Tomzel]], who had come to the throne at fourteen in 3687. It charges him with burning the house of writings at Bakh, with sending other men's sons to die at Likhtli while he stayed at home, and with letting his steward take a tenth of the harvest for the war, a tenth for the new hall, a tenth for the victory feast and a tenth for the painter. A translator learns to distrust poets who speak for ploughmen, but this one can be checked. Bakh was sacked in 3694 and its archive burned. At Likhtli in 3701 Tomzel put 97,739 men into the field against 30,016 and lost 20,347 of them: the poet's "twenty thousand", and his "more than three to every Tsey man".

His distinction between smoke and fire describes the years AY 3500 to 3750 better than any I could invent. The states that blazed brightest were often the nearest to going out. [[pol:1177|Iethithi]] broke apart six years after its greatest victory. [[pol:1279|Dagafu]] reached its largest size in 3680 and collapsed in 3681. Tomzel touched its peak in 3730 and was gone by 3739. Meanwhile slower fires burned under the ash: a faith of one god that spread by sword and by sermon, a better way of working the fields, and a population that ended the period larger than it began, despite a long cold and four great plagues. People living under states numbered 178.2 million in 3500, 173.2 million in 3620 and 184.9 million in 3740. The number of states went from 36 to 34 and then, as the empires shattered, to 43. And it was cold: 0.48 °C below the long-run mean over the period, and nearly 0.9 °C below it in the 3620s.

## The victor's six years (AY 3500–3530)

The period opens with a lesson the next two centuries would repeat. Iethithi, ruled from [[set:104|Yiethithi]], was the most populous state in the world, with some 26 million people, and its queen [[per:31931|Lamfin Li]], called the Victorious, had reigned since 3456. When she died in 3502 the throne went to a usurper, [[per:32415|Ish'o Maf]], and three years later he led the empire into the [[evt:32746|Iethithi–Yidha War]] against the kingdom of [[pol:1324|Yidha]], to take the land of [[reg:15|Baan]].

We see this war from below. "The Remembrance of a Lance of Iethithi in the War for Bani" (PAL·CH·08437) was written in 3536 by an old man who had been "the least of the thirty" lances his lord owed the crown. He is frank about the first field, Zhiyiyzhikhti in 3507, where his comrades "divided the cattle of Bani at the fire before we had seen them" and were broken, though he will not write the number of the dead. The record supplies it: 12,062 of Iethithi's men against 4,112 of Yidha's. His "five to their one" at Nivirathyirshi in 3509 is also close: 118,124 against 25,754. At the second battle there, in 3511, Yidha lost 16,589 men and the war. By the Treaty of Nivirathyirshi (PAL·TR·02124) it gave up ten districts and a town. The treaty was sworn "before Kievi and Veshethmekh", the gods of both parties: in 3511 the faith of the One was still one faith among several.

Victory did not save the victor. In 3506 [[evt:32668|the Yellow Fever]] broke out at Nodhinge and ran along the trade roads to twelve more cities, killing about 800,000 people, roughly one in six of those it reached. Iethithi peaked in 3510 at 26.5 million. Then its politics failed. In 3516 a second usurper, [[per:32563|Ma'shi Mu'a]], took the throne, and in 3517 came the [[evt:32890|Collapse of the Iethithi State]]. The record lists the fever, the defeat of 3507 and both usurpations among its causes, and no shortage of grain. The empire went to six successors. One, the kingdom of [[pol:1398|Imshi]], would last until 4050; another, the [[pol:1397|Ayshi]] empire, would itself fall apart in 3702.

The loser survived its defeat by changing its name. Yidha's last king, [[per:32233|Baldun II]], reigned until 3526. In that year Yidha broke into three, and the heir that held [[set:0|Taulin]] took the name of [[pol:1405|Yidhe]]. Under the queen [[per:32745|Shathnadhuth]] (3531–3564) and then [[per:33088|Nifpulin]] the Great (3564–3623), it became an empire and grew from 9.1 million people in 3550 to 20.1 million by 3700. Of all the great powers in this chapter, the one born of defeat outlived every other.

## The sword of the One (AY 3530–3600)

The faith named beside Kievi in that treaty taught that time runs in one line from the making of the world to its judgement; that there is one divine power, and the old gods are its servants or shadows; that the good life is compassion and release from suffering; and that the message is for all peoples. A universal faith with a judgement at the end of time can be carried on a spear, and in this period it was.

In 3532 the emperor [[per:32684|Matafvafen]] of [[pol:1323|Lienunushal]] opened the [[evt:33116|Holy War of Lienunushal against Pienpedhzhe]], into the province of [[reg:7|Tunnge]]. It swung back and forth for fifteen years. The "Lament for Those Who Fell in the Holy War" (PAL·PO·00012) counts four battles at Virnadhu, "and thrice the field was taken from us, and once we took it back", exactly as the record has it. The strangest turn came in 3539: the [[evt:33179|Conversion of Pienpedhzhe to Veshethmekh]], in the year [[per:32814|Natdapuefnen]], called the Devout, came to the throne of [[pol:1328|Pienpedhzhe]]. The kingdom of [[pol:1389|Tlo]] converted the same year. By the logic of a holy war the fighting should have stopped. It went on for eight more years, and the Lament explains why: the enemy "took the Name into their mouths and not into their hearts, and they would not give up the land". The land had been the point. Lienunushal lost 97,135 men, Pienpedhzhe 76,764, and at Munu in 3547 the attacker was broken and settled for a bare truce. The victors' own account (PAL·CH·05938) survives water-stained and partly illegible; here we hear the losers more clearly.

The defeat began Lienunushal's unravelling. Provinces seceded in 3570 and 3601, and in 3602 the empire broke into three. Its heart went on as a smaller kingdom of [[pol:1430|Lienunushal]] at [[set:18|Linnshal]], which would outlive this chapter by three centuries.

Most conversions were quieter; kings chose gods much as they chose allies. [[pol:1390|Miuhitl]] took up [[mov:84|Kebali]] in 3519; [[pol:1400|Dueminnga]], a fragment of Iethithi, adopted [[mov:67|Dankhüdhil]] in 3534; [[pol:1356|Kavu]] adopted [[mov:96|Setlietlpovi]] in 3537; [[pol:1417|Bene]], a fragment of a fragment born of the [[evt:33502|Collapse of the Yiviyina State]] in 3567, chose Dankhüdhil in 3583. By 3620 [[mov:58|Veshethmekh]] held 15 per cent of the land, against 17 per cent each for the old cults of Uthungala and Mie'to'ieshio.

Success bred dissent from within. In 3581 Tlamfin Tlyi, keeper of the lamp at Ma'itli in the Muvush empire, sent out an open letter "Cutting Off the Lukewarm" (PAL·LT·01698). He accused the priests of lighting lamps before the old gods "lest the merchants be offended" and of counting the tithe while the wounded lay in the fields. This was [[evt:33712|the Myituwize Schism]]: it denied no doctrine, but demanded that doctrine be practised. [[mov:129|Myituwize]] never counted more than about 290,000 adherents, but its charge that the faith had grown comfortable would be made again in each of the next two chapters.

## The cold decades (AY 3600–3680)

In 3600 Dagafu was the largest state in the world: 27.7 million people and 411 districts, ruled from the city of [[set:9|Dagafu]]. [[per:32478|Navavun]] the Great (3516–3550) had built it up, and [[per:33011|Nagule]] the Conqueror (3557–3620) had added to it, most expensively in the [[evt:33618|Dagafu–Tambulu War]] of 3579–3587 against the kingdom of [[pol:1386|Tambulu]], fought for land in [[reg:21|Daadüdhih]]. Dagafu lost 55,393 men, Tambulu 19,957, and Dagafu won: the Treaty of Jyijinjivji gave it 41 districts, five towns and 56,232 kilograms of silver. Gains were counted in land and costs in men, and few conquerors in this chapter paid more men than the side they beat.

Then came the cold. Daadüdhih entered a run of droughts: [[evt:33826|a great drought in 3603]] over 194 districts, [[evt:33944|another in 3614]] over 398, [[evt:34067|a third in 3621]] over 385. A cold spell set in from 3620 to 3679, and the eruptions of Mount Nvideyo in 3624 and Mount I'mikhil in 3637 dimmed the sky further. In 3624 famine killed some 365,000 people in Daadüdhih. A measure of grain, normally worth about 8 grams of silver, cost 13.5 grams at Taulin in the 3620s and 16.5 grams at Dagafu in the 3630s.

Into this cold came [[evt:34069|the Black Fever]]. It broke out in 3623 at [[set:164|Pinpidhzhi]] and spread along the trade roads to 48 more cities, killing about 2.9 million people, nearly one in five where it struck. For once we have a witness at the first door. The "Daybook of a Physician of Pienpedhzhe in the Year of the Black Fever" (PAL·DI·00001) begins with a rope-maker in the harbour quarter, "his tongue dry as bark". The physician, sworn to the household of [[per:33383|Bienalen I]], stayed when a colleague fled. He notes that "bread is cheap, for no one buys it ... Coffins are dear." On the twenty-second day his servant falls ill and he moves him into his own bed. The daybook survives whole, but it ends three days later with a prayer, and we do not know whether the writer lived.

Dagafu's crisis was political before it was natural. In 3620, the year Nagule died, a man named Nakathash seized the throne; a revolt broke out at Tadudüvi in 3627 and a province seceded at Kazhini in 3628. At the end of that decade Dagafu held 237 districts and 19.2 million people. It recovered fast, to a peak of 31.2 million people in 3680 under the queen Nashinzhè. But the record keeps another measure of a state, its stability, and on that measure Dagafu in 3680 was close to nothing. In 3681 came the [[evt:34645|Collapse of the Dagafu State]], into six successors. Its recorded causes reach back sixty years, to the usurpation of 3620: a state can grow for half a century on a cracked foundation. The heir at the capital, the kingdom of [[pol:1464|Dagafu]], lasted until 4141; another, [[pol:1465|Kambive]], will appear in the next chapter as the cradle of a new faith.

Readers in my own country will ask about Khishkhis. The answer is a warning against reading the present into the past. The kingdom of [[pol:1243|Khishkhis]] fought the [[evt:34263|Holy War of Khishkhis against Puvu]] (3638–3655) into [[reg:20|Khishkhih]] and won it. The "Hymn for the Peace of Agni" (PAL·PO·00015) says the One spoke to [[pol:1277|Puvu]] first "in the famine of Daadüdhih" and then "with the sword of His servants". The treaty took 40 districts and 115,569 kilograms of silver. Puvu collapsed in 3665, and Khishkhis the next year: the [[evt:34512|Collapse of the Khishkhis State]] followed defeats by Dagafu in 3644–3646 and a lost battle at Dyigini in 3651, inside the very war the hymn celebrates. The hymn does not mention Dyigini. The heir, a new [[pol:1454|Khishkhis]] empire, lasted until 4067, but it is not the state that rules from Nambi today. That story belongs to chapter 17.

Against all this stands one quiet novelty. In 3662 the farmers of Pienpedhzhe began to rotate their crops, a practice the record credits to one Khakienuth, who died two years later at about thirty-two. The idea travelled slowly; Shudhunki had to find it again for itself in 3827. Nor could it stop a drought. The "Record of the dry years from AY 3668" (PAL·RE·00141), kept at Kabaf, says that "the wells ran dry and the river shrank to a stream"; grain there cost 19.5 grams of silver in the 3660s, nearly two and a half times its usual price. In 3679 [[evt:34536|the Shaking Cough]] killed another 900,000.

## The great war and the burning of Bakh (AY 3680–3739)

The war the record ranks second in the period, after the holy war of 3532, was the [[evt:34671|Yidhe–Pienpedhzhe War]] (3683–3700), fought for the province of [[reg:8|Diebeguvebenga]]. Pienpedhzhe had just lost a war to the kingdom of [[pol:1406|Yaaril]], Yidhe's ally, and 23,024 men in a single day at Khaadhange in 3670. Yidhe's empress Makhkaalith, called the Fair, took the field in person.

Three witnesses can be set side by side: Makhkaalith's letter from the field of Rurin in 3690 (PAL·LT·01668), the official "Yidhe account of the Yidhe–Pienpedhzhe War" of 3701 (PAL·CH·06205), and the record. The letter says the enemy dead at Rurin "were beyond counting" and Yidhe's losses "light". The chronicle counts 5,250 enemy dead and 346 of its own. The record gives 1,940 for Pienpedhzhe and 1,707 for Yidhe: Rurin was a draw that Yidhe was left holding. Over the whole war the chronicle's tallies of Yidhe's dead come to about 11,000; the record puts them at 55,288, against 69,517 for Pienpedhzhe. Yet the letter admits a defeat at Linidhagshal, blaming the rain and "certain vassals who came late with half their due", and it shows an empress taxing her houses of prayer for "a holy labour" against a people who had worshipped the same One since 3539.

The war ended at Pininnushal in 3700, where Pienpedhzhe's queen Kafvafupuefen, barely twenty, was killed and her army lost 13,377 men. The Treaty of Pinpidhzhi gave Yidhe 37 districts, five towns, trading rights and 130,630 kilograms of silver, the largest indemnity of the period so far. Pienpedhzhe collapsed four years later.

Tomzel, meanwhile, grew. Under [[per:32252|Nitllu Mov II]] (3487–3542) it had held about 11 million people; under [[per:32887|Shoe Tluie]] the Conqueror (3571–3628) it neared 20 million. Two usurpers broke the line between 3628 and 3666, but expansion went on. In the [[evt:35140|Tomzel–Yidhe War]] of 3725–3728 Tomzel won twice at Nivirathyirshi, the ford where Iethithi had beaten Yidha two centuries before, and in the second battle Yidhe lost 27,001 men. The Treaty of Taulin, sworn in Yidhe's own capital in 3728, gave Tomzel 33 districts. Yidhe's signatory was its new emperor [[per:34426|Tukulven]], who had come to the throne the year before at the age of nine.

Tomzel peaked two years later, at 29.8 million people and 532 districts. Sitlyitlilyian died in 3730. His son Minasilyian, the three-year-old prince of the satire whom the poet hoped would be "a little flame, and not a little smoke", succeeded at about twenty-one, and in 3739 the [[evt:35280|Collapse of the Tomzel State]] split the empire six ways. Its recorded causes read like the satirist's charge-sheet: Likhtli in 3701, a revolt in 3713, defeat by Yidhe at Yirantudhu in 3726. From the wreck rose the [[pol:1497|Ölie]] empire, which kept Ölye as its capital, and the kingdom of [[pol:1498|Ane]].

## The red year (AY 3739–3750)

Tukulven grew up. In 3745 he attacked the [[pol:1373|Ishuiithie]] empire, and in 3746 he won the field at Lininadhe. That spring [[evt:35300|the Red Sickness]] came out of Nodhinge. It reached 56 more cities and killed about 4.6 million people, close to three in ten where it struck: the worst single disaster of the chapter. Tukulven wrote to the merchant houses of Taulin "with our own hand, because our secretary is dead" (PAL·LT·01700). Grain, he told them, was "less than a zhiflun the measure, because the mouths that should eat it are being carried out on the carts; but do not imagine it will stay cheap when the reapers die in the fields." The prices agree: the zhiflun weighed 12.6 grams of silver, and grain at Taulin in the 3740s cost about 9.6. A temple record at Taulin (PAL·RE·00146) adds the view from the street: "Whole streets stood empty."

That same season, at Kivunge, a seeker named Nitllo Simin stood before the elders of Kievi, whose teaching was justice and moderation, and asked: "When the house burns, does the steward go moderately for water?" The "Record of the Disputation at Kivunge" (PAL·CH·08445) marks the birth of the Oze movement, a creed of duty and sacrifice made for a year of plague.

A word on how we know all this. Besides eighteen literary works, some 800 documents survive from the period: 415 inscriptions, 107 chronicles, 68 treaties, 54 coins, 27 muster rolls and only 25 ledgers. Inscriptions are mostly kings speaking; ledgers, where ordinary prices hide, are rare. Court annals choose what to remember: the Annals of Nini for 3600–3699 (PAL·AN·00625) note the Black Fever but none of the ten battles Nini fought. And chance shapes the rest. Makhkaalith's letter, Tukulven's letter and the Kivunge disputation all come from a single excavation at Taulin in AY 4948. Our sense of Yidhe's voice in this age rests on the luck of one dig.

Beneath the courts, speech and money changed too. Kushies, Em, Khungangu and Khundangji emerged as distinct languages about 3599, Kuteni and Oyanim about 3699, and in Tasemem, about 3554, an s between vowels became h. Some 147 merchant houses were founded, 159 closed, 55 new coinages were struck and 14 weight standards introduced.

By 3750 the map belonged to the heirs of three falls: Yidhe under a young conqueror who had survived both defeat and plague; Ölie, risen from Tomzel's ruin; and Imshi, from Iethithi's. [[evt:35299|A new drought in Daadüdhih]], begun in 3745, was still parching the fields. The next chapter takes up the story there, with Tukulven's long reign and a world that was about to grow warmer.
