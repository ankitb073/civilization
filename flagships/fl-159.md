*Delivered before the academy at [[set:114|Tamesitsiyi]] by [[per:22782|Niloshfish]], master of works, in the year 2127 of the Mietseyeshio, and afterwards written out at the request of its members.*

Gentlemen of the academy, you have asked me to speak of the new works, and I obey; though I warn you that a mason who is asked to make a speech is like a horse asked to sing. I shall be brief where I can, and plain where I cannot be brief.

Eleven years have passed since the arms of our king, [[per:22537|Sosho]], prevailed at [[evt:22843|Sase]] and at [[evt:22875|Athtathe]], and he set his seal to the [[evt:22876|Treaty of Athtathe]], which enlarged the kingdom by lands and by a river. In those eleven years the cities of [[pol:924|Yiotl]] have grown, and their thirst has grown with them. A city may be fed by carts, for grain does not spoil on the road; but a city cannot be watered by carts. Water must come of itself, or it does not come at all. The question I set myself was therefore simple: how shall water be persuaded to walk to the city, across valleys that lie in its way, without anyone carrying it?

The answer is two things, and the first is the arch.

Consider a row of wedge-shaped stones, each narrower at the bottom than at the top, set upon a curved frame of timber. While the frame stands, the stones rest upon it. Then the last stone is driven in at the crown, and the frame is struck away; and the stones, which should by every instinct fall, do not fall. Each presses upon its neighbour, and by pressing, holds it up. The weight that would have brought each stone down by itself is passed from stone to stone, outward and downward, into the piers, and so into the earth. I have stood under such an arch while the timbers were drawn out, and I confess that I prayed; but it stood.

Permit me a philosopher's moment, since I am among philosophers. The arch is a city. No stone in it is strong enough to stand alone across the gap; every stone is held by the others and holds them in turn. The keystone is praised because it is placed last and highest, but it is not stronger than the rest, and without them it is merely a stone in the air. I commend this to any citizen, of whatever rank, who imagines that he stands by himself.

The second thing is the fall. Water will go anywhere, provided it is always going down. We therefore lead it from the springs in the hills along a channel of stone that falls, by careful measurement, a finger's breadth in every hundred paces: steep enough that it moves, gentle enough that it does not tear the channel apart. Where the ground drops away into a valley, we do not let the water drop with it. We carry the channel across on arches, one row upon another if the valley is deep, so that the water passes over the heads of the shepherds below at the same gentle slope it kept upon the hill.

A word, finally, on the distribution, for engineering without justice is merely cleverness. When the water arrives, it goes first to the public fountains, where any woman of the city may fill her jar; then to the baths; and only last, and for a fee paid in [[cur:513|sima]] to the treasury, to private houses. Our teachers of the [[mov:25|Mietseyeshio]] say that justice in dealings and moderation in all things are the measure of a good life, and that the wise withdraw from appetite. I have tried to build a channel on those principles. The rich man's garden may drink, but after the poor man's children.

That is the whole of it: stones that lean on one another, and water that is always going gently down. You will hear it called the [[evt:22976|Invention of Arch & aqueduct engineering in Yiotl]], and my name attached to it. Do not believe more than half of that. The masons cut every stone, and the labourers carried them, and the city paid for both. I only did the arithmetic, and held my breath when the timbers came out.
