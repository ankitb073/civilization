title: Here Mash Shimin Slept
---
The sieve was a frame of planks with wire mesh nailed across it, and by the third week Yi Ivye could read it the way her mother had read the sky over the uplands. Earth went through. What stayed was stones, and charcoal, and the bones of small animals, and now and then a thing a hand had made. Those she laid on a board by her knee for the foreigner's boy to collect. The rest she tipped onto the heap, and the dust came up and settled on her arms, in the creases of her knuckles, in her teeth.

The trench ran across the lower town of Yinishma like a knife across a loaf. Near the bottom there was a band of black as thick as a hand: burnt beams, the foreigner said, and the points of arrows. A city had been taken here and set on fire, thirty-three hundred years ago and more. Above the black lay a long pale nothing, centuries of it, and above that, near the top where the barrows ran, the walls of houses that people's grandfathers might have known.

It was the second summer that the rain had failed to come up into the hills. The record calls what was happening to the sky the [[cli:251|Industrial Warming]] and has it beginning more than a century before; on the Asum farms above the city it had no name at all. It was only that the wheat came up thin and went white before it filled, and the springs the old people drank from turned to mud and then to cracked mud, and the beech leaves came down in the middle of summer as if someone had told them it was autumn.

Her daughter slept under the standing wall at the end of the trench, where the shade lasted until noon. Nuizashimin was seven. She had been born in a spring storm, in the year the rain came down so hard it took the footbridge at the ford, and she had been named for it. Now she slept most of the day, because sleep was the one thing hunger left alone. From the sieve Yi Ivye could see the girl's feet, the soles black, one ankle turned out. Every few shakes she looked, the way you look at a pot.

The foreigner was called Nambili. He was an old man, sixty-one that summer, and he had dug, his boy said, in more cities than Yi Ivye had heard of. He wore a hat with a cloth hanging down the back of the neck, and he walked the lip of the trench all day with a notebook, and he paid at sunset, in coin, without arguing. Every morning more people waited at the rope than he had work for, and the boy chose. He had chosen Yi Ivye on the first morning because she said she could read, and because, he told her later, she had looked as if she would not fall down.

* * *

At sunset she took her coins up through the town to the grain market, with Nuizashimin on her hip, because the girl's legs would not do the hill.

The market had grain. That was the thing she could not get used to, the thing she kept turning over as if it were a riddle with an answer. There were sacks of it in the stalls and more in the depot by the railway, and the board above the scales said what it had said the year before and the year before that: less than one [[cur:1112|Ime]] the measure. When her mother was a girl it had cost more, and her mother had called that robbery. The grain was not dear. In a good year the uplands sold their wheat down here and bought what they lacked. Then there was no wheat, and they sold the cart, and the second pig, and the copper pot that had been her grandmother's, and after that they sold nothing, because there was nothing, and the number on the board stayed exactly where it was. Of everything that summer, that was what she could least forgive.

Ie Yuiv had gone down to [[set:30|Namosash]] on the railway in the first winter, to the port, where there was said to be work on the quays. He had stood at the carriage window with his hand flat on the glass until the train took the curve. There had been one letter, at the thaw, saying he had a bed and a cough. Since then nothing, and she did not know what nothing meant, and she had mostly stopped trying to decide.

On the steps of the grain hall a priest of the [[mov:25|Myitsiyishio]], the republic's own cult, was preaching moderation in all things. The wise, he said, withdraw from appetite. Nobody laughed. It would have taken more than anyone had.

She bought what the coins would buy and carried it and the girl back down to the lower town, where the people from the hills slept in the roofless rooms the foreigner had not yet dug, and she made the porridge thin enough to last.

* * *

The wall came out of the ground on a morning in the fourth week, among the high houses near the top of the trench. At first it was only plaster, a grey face under the rubble, and the men clearing it would have put a pick through it if the boy had not shouted. Nambili came down the ladder with his pencil in his teeth.

Somebody had written on it. Not painted: scratched, with a nail or the point of a knife, letters the size of a thumb, crooked where the plaster was rough.

Nambili looked at it for a long time. He could read the scripts of kings, the boy said, half the scripts in the world; but this was a hand, not a script, and it was in the language of the hills.

"Nineteen," said the boy. That was her number on his board. "You said you read."

Yi Ivye climbed down. Close to, the letters were easy: the same letters she had learned in three winters at the valley school, only older in the shape of some of them, the way her grandmother had made them.

"Here Mash Shimin slept," she read, "and dreamed of Winyim."

The men at the top of the trench laughed, the first laughing she had heard in that place all summer. Then everyone had an opinion. Winyim was a girl: what else did anybody dream of, scratching a wall in the dark? Winyim was a village, somewhere the writer had walked from. Winyim was a god. Old Khuishtli, who had been a carter before the carts were sold, said it was a horse, that he had once known a horse with a name very like it, and they laughed at him too.

"Mash Shimin," Nambili said. "A man or a woman?"

"I don't know. It could be either."

He had her read it again, slowly, a letter at a time, and he copied the letters into the notebook, and then the words, and under the words what they meant.

"How old?" she asked.

He laid his palm flat on the plaster, as if it might still be warm. "These houses? A hundred years. A little more."

* * *

That evening, when the coins had been handed out, he asked her to come to the shed where the finds were kept. She went with Nuizashimin asleep on her shoulder, because she did not leave her anywhere.

The shed smelled of wet sacking and lamp oil. Along one wall stood the stones the summer had given up, upright in a row like people waiting at the rope. Nine victories, he said, all won at this one city; five of them here, and four more carved into the living rock where the trench had laid it bare. Somebody's victory at Yinishma, again and again, over more than two thousand years. He lifted the lamp to the tallest, a black stone with the writing cut deep and clean.

"Can you read that?"

She could not. The letters were not hers.

"It says a king beat his enemies here." He moved his finger along a line and read it out: "He killed 10,500 soldiers." He moved the lamp to the next, a paler stone, worn soft. "This one says eighteen thousand eight hundred. The one carved in the rock down at the bottom of the trench says thirty-nine thousand eight hundred. Every king who stood on this hill, and one queen, tells me exactly how many they killed, and not one of them is telling the truth. Kings round up." He looked down at the notebook, at the crooked letters he had copied off the plaster. "In the whole city, this is the only one who tells me he slept."

"Or she," said Yi Ivye.

"Or she."

At the door the boy was counting what was left in the cashbox. The paper from Namosash had come up on the evening train, he said, and the foreigner's country had won another battle against the king of Baga. A holy war, the paper called it.

Nambili did not look round. "Not against you," he said, to nobody in particular. "I only dig."

* * *

Nuizashimin woke in the night as hot as a stone in the sun and asked for water, and when she had drunk she asked about the wall.

"Was Mash Shimin hungry?"

"I don't know."

"Was it a lady?"

"Nobody knows."

"What's Winyim?"

Yi Ivye lay on her back in the roofless room with the girl's head on her arm and looked at the stars, which were the only thing that summer that had not changed. She might have said a girl, or a god, or a horse. She said it was a place up in the hills, higher than their farm, where the storms still came in spring and the springs ran and the wheat stood up yellow and heavy, and that people who slept in the lower town sometimes dreamed of it, and that this was all right. It was allowed.

"Is Father there?"

"Go to sleep."

The girl slept. Yi Ivye did not. She lay and listened to the lower town breathing round her, the coughing in the next room, a man somewhere saying one name over and over, and under all of them, under the rubble, Mash Shimin's wall, a hundred years of dark pressed against its face, waiting for morning to tell everyone again that somebody had been here, and had slept.

* * *

The season ended in the sixth week. Nambili had what he had come for: sixteen inscribed finds, the boy said, counting the plaster. On the last morning the boy pasted wet paper over the wall and beat it with a brush until the letters stood up in it, and peeled it off and laid it in the sun to dry, Mash Shimin's sentence pressed into it backwards like a footprint.

At the pay table that evening Nambili asked her name.

"Not nineteen," he said. "Your name."

She told him. He wrote it in the notebook under the copy of the wall, and the girl's name too when she asked him to, and spelled them back to her to be sure. Then he paid her, and something over for the reading, and shook her hand as if they had closed a sale, and in the morning he went down to the station with his crates.

His report was printed two years later, in his own language. It describes one burnt layer, and trenches cut across the palace quarter and the lower town; it lists the stones by the names of the kings who raised them, and the queen; and the last of its sixteen finds is the plaster, entered as a graffito. It names no one living but himself.

The famine went on into the next year, the year the Republic of [[pol:1952|Yisamashtsi]] elected [[per:46743|Nuizaziyuive]], and then it was over. When the record came to count the [[evt:47755|Famine in Yizamashti]], it set down 4,956,536 people of [[reg:1|Yisamashtsi]] who had died or fled, and it does not say which. It has no column for a man with a bed and a cough.

On the morning the foreigner left, Nuizashimin woke in the shade of the last standing wall of the lower town and said she had dreamed.

"Of what?"

"Father. He was on the up train." She frowned, holding on to it. "He had his hand on the window."

Yi Ivye looked at the wall beside them. The plaster there was soft; a nail would do it, or the edge of a coin. *Here Nuizashimin slept, and dreamed of Ie Yuiv.* She had seen, that summer, what walls kept, and for whom, and for how long. She did not write it. She put her hand on her daughter's back, where she could feel it rise and fall, and kept it there.
