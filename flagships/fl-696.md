## Rain among the prophets

For a man who served fifty-nine years as a priest of the state, [[per:55137|Nazhige]] left almost nothing of himself. The record gives his birth in AY 4534, among the Khumbivi people of the [[pol:1670|Dagafu]] Empire; his priesthood from 4554, at twenty; and his natural death in 4613, at seventy-nine. It names no parent, wife or child, no temple, not even the rite he served. The Empire's faith was the [[mov:24|Uthungala]], which taught that time runs in a line toward a final judgement and that prosperity is a sign of divine favour. Whether Nazhige kept its altars, the record does not say.

What it keeps is three collections of other men's words, all complete, all in the state archive of Dagafu. No other writer in the catalogue is credited with more than two works of scripture or teaching. His name means rain; the names of the masters he wrote down mean flood, honey and harvest. He lived under five rulers, three of whom seized the throne, in an empire that grew from 423 districts at the end of the 4530s to 475 at the end of the 4610s.

## Gathered by those who heard him

The *Teachings of Navavun* (PAL·PH·00042) were compiled at Dagambi in 4565. Navavun had founded his school there in 4539, when Nazhige was five, and had died three years before the compiling. His sayings are brisk and worldly: "Believe nothing because it is old or because it is written. Test it." Another runs: "If the gods exist, they have better things to do than to punish us." The record closes the Bange school in the year it opens it, 4539, and no other document names the school or this Navavun. Without Nazhige's register he would be a date.

It was a year of civil war. In the [[evt:44141|Third Dagafu Civil War]] the secessionist Nilu Empire held Ajü and Mbilu, and a Nilu muster roll for the Battle of Khayagu in 4564 lists 16,310 archers from Mbilu (PAL·MU·01434). In 4567, the year after Ajü returned to the Empire, Nazhige compiled there the *Sayings of Nambave* (PAL·SC·00096). Nambave had founded the Binenduve at Ajü in 4509 and died in 4516, eighteen years before Nazhige was born; these are words "as his disciples remembered them", half a century on. "Do not believe a thing because a priest has said it," one runs. "Test it as you test silver." Another: "This is the way of our fathers; let others keep the ways of theirs."

That same year the Treaty of Ndulamlol ended the [[evt:44126|Holy War of Dagafu against Kankush]], binding Kankush to cede seven districts, pay an indemnity and take up the Uthungala. The Dagafu chronicler later claimed twelve districts and wrote that "Thanks were given in the houses of the Uthungala" (PAL·CH·07781). Whatever the other priests of Dagafu were doing that year, one sat at Ajü writing down a prophet who asked to be left to his own ways.

## The turning of the wheel

Thirty-seven years pass before the third collection. The Empire fought five wars in them, among them the [[evt:44316|First Khishkhis–Dagafu War]], my own country's failed attempt on its lands; on Nazhige the record is silent. In 4604, aged seventy, he compiled the *Sayings of Navande* at Mbilu (PAL·SC·00098) as a printed broadsheet. With a newspaper and a patent of that year, it is among the first three printed things the archive holds from the Empire. Navande had broken from the Vimekhulu at Mbilu in 4560 and died in 4586; the city's district passed to his Bangekhulu in 4569.

Where Nambave's world "has a beginning and it will have an end", the broadsheet opens: "What rises will fall and what falls will rise; do not grieve at the turning of the wheel." Where Nambave kept to his fathers' ways, this teaching is "not for one people but for every nation". And where Navavun saw "no shame in wealth honestly got", both prophets say "Wealth is a chain of gold; the wise go without." Nazhige reconciled none of it.

## A common stock

Nor need he have, for these are not transcripts. Both prophets also say, word for word, "Be just in the market and gentle in the house", and that line is far older than either. The *Sayings of Nakavzi* (PAL·SC·00001), the founding text of the Uthungala, compiled by a priest in AY 106 on a clay tablet now fragmentary and excavated at Nilu only in 4901, already holds it, beside the line I have taken for my title and Navande's "There is one above all the gods". The words Nazhige gave two reformers were already the oldest words of the Empire's own faith. The record does not say whether he knew it; proverbs can outlive their tablets. A collection of sayings is a selection from a common stock, and the compiler's craft lies in the choosing.

Did he choose faithfully? Navande can answer. His *Defence* of 4561 (PAL·SC·00151) asks for "justice in our dealings and moderation in all things", holds that "the wise withdraw from wealth and appetite", and declares "We send our word to every nation". Where the broadsheet can be tested against its prophet, it holds.

One caution. The patent for movable type (PAL·PT·00002), dated 4604 at [[set:9|Dagafu]], names another Nazhige, born in 4568. Nambavi has warned (PAL·SP·00060) that among namesakes merging "is the likeliest thing to have happened." The record keeps a priest of seventy and an inventor of thirty-six apart, and so should we.

Nazhige died in 4613; his broadsheet was catalogued in the state archive in 4634, the year the Empire broke into six successor states. I write in Khishkhis, whose faith is the Vimekhulu that Navande left, and I have tested these texts as they ask. They ring true: a priest's work that tells you not to trust a priest, and leaves the testing to you.
