title: Fifteen Sticks and Three Cuts
---
From the ninth rank the battle was a sound, then a wall of dust, and for most of the morning it was the back of the man in front.

He had been given a spear at [[set:277|Lishe]] in the spring and had not yet lowered it at anyone. Ahead of him stood eight ranks of spearmen, and somewhere ahead of them the crossbowmen, and somewhere off to the left the king's horse, which he had seen once at a river crossing and not since. Behind him there were more ranks than he could count, and he was a man who counted for his living. [[per:30579|Natezho]] had brought more than thirty-four thousand men into the country of [[pol:1297|Shetiy]]. The scouts said the men of Shetiy had fewer than nine thousand. It was the kind of arithmetic that makes a man in the ninth rank feel safe and a little foolish, like someone who has carried a sack to market to bring home one onion.

The noise ahead rose in the first hour, and word came back down the ranks the way word does, passed over shoulders and losing a little at each one. The horsemen of Shetiy had come at the front. [[per:30600|Khashsinish]] himself had led them. The front had broken. The front had not broken; it had bent and come straight again. He never learned which was true, and later he thought that probably both were, a hundred paces apart.

The ranks went forward a few steps and stopped, and went forward again. The steppe grass had gone to seed, and the seed came up off the trodden stalks and stuck to his sweat. At midday the word that came back was the one that means the other side is leaving. By the middle of the afternoon the dust had drawn off along the river road, slowly, the way dust moves when the men under it are walking and not running and are carrying other men.

He had not lowered his spear once. Neither had anyone near him. They looked at each other the way people look after a hailstorm has gone over somebody else's village.

* * *

Before evening the man who had charge of his hundred came down the line asking who could count past his fingers, and the men around him pointed at him, because they all knew what he had been.

For eleven years he had stood at the public scales in the grain market at Lishe, where a measure of grain went for a little under one [[cur:657|Mithnatl]] and the difference of a handful was somebody's supper. Each new year the priests of the [[mov:25|Mieteyeshio]] came to bless the weights, and told the measurers what priests had always told them: that the gods ask justice in dealings and moderation in all things, and that a measure is a promise made where the gods can see it. He had believed it in the plain way a man believes a thing that is also good for business.

"The king wants their dead," said the hundred-man. "Theirs, not ours. Ours have their own counters." He handed over a knife and pointed at the willows along the river. "A notch for a man. Every tenth notch, cut it across, deep. A new stick for each hundred. Don't count anyone twice, and don't count anyone breathing."

He cut a bundle of willow in the last light, peeled the ends, and went out.

The dead of Shetiy lay mostly where the front had been, in a long uneven drift, as if a wave had come up the field and set them down and gone back. Crossbow bolts stood in the ground among them like reeds at the edge of a pond. He went along the drift as he would have gone along a row of sacks, touching each man on the shoulder so as not to count him twice, and cutting the notch afterwards with his thumb against the blade. He found at first that if he touched them he did not need to look at their faces. Then he found that he had to look, because some of them were not dead. Those he did not notch. He called for the bearers, and sometimes the bearers came.

He worked until he could not see the knife, slept with the sticks under his arm, and began again when the sky went grey.

* * *

In the morning there was mist on the river, and there were women on the field.

They had come in the night from the villages along the water, and in the grey light the pickets let them walk among the dead, because no one had told the pickets not to. Most of them went in twos and threes. One came alone. She was older than his mother would have been, and she had a walking stick of her own. She watched him notch a man, and then she followed him, three paces behind, the way a woman at the market follows a measurer she does not trust.

When he stopped, she spoke to him. Her speech was his own speech heard through a wall: the words were the right shape and the wrong sound, and he caught one in three. She said a name and laid the flat of her hand on her chest. "Pitselobi." She raised the hand above her own head, a long way above. A tall boy. The name had a heart in it, he thought, or a brother, or both. The rest of it was wall.

"I don't know names," he said in his own tongue, and held up the sticks. "Only how many."

She looked at the sticks for some time. Then she said something with a question at the end of it, and he understood it as well as if she had been born in his own street: Is he in there?

There was only one thing he could think to do. He walked her back to the beginning of the drift, to the first man he had notched the night before, and knelt, and turned the face up to the light.

She looked at it the way you look for a dropped coin, not with grief but with attention, as if grief would use up time she needed. She said a short word that he took to mean no. He laid the head down and went to the next.

They went along the whole drift like that. It took the morning. He turned the faces and she looked, and at each she said the short word, and then a few words more, the same few every time, and he understood after a while that she was praying. Among her words was one he did know, though she bent it: Myitsiyishio. His own faith, the one whose priests blessed the scales at home, said with its vowels thinned the way soup is thinned in a bad year. She was asking the gods to take each man into his proper place, for every god has a place and every place its due. He had heard the same prayer said over his father, in the same order, by a priest who had learned it from a priest. On the morning of the battle, he supposed, both armies had stood under the same sky and asked it for the same thing. One army had brought more men.

Once she knelt beside a boy and looked for a long time, and his own breath stopped. Then she said the word and stood up, and he found that his hands were shaking and that he was not sure whose relief it was.

The sun burned off the mist. The flies came. The bearers of Lishe went past with the last of their own wounded and looked at the two of them and said nothing. Near the river end of the drift, where the bolts had come thickest, the dead lay two and three together and he had to pull them apart to see. She helped him. Neither of them said anything about that.

Then there were no more.

She looked up the field and down it, as if the drift might go on somewhere else. She pointed at the sticks and asked again, a different question this time; he could tell from the way her hand moved that she wanted the number. He showed her. Fifteen sticks of a hundred, and a sixteenth with three deep cuts across it and nothing after.

He said it in his own tongue: one thousand, five hundred and thirty. She said it back in hers, and it came out almost the same, and he nodded, because it was near enough.

Then she pointed down the river road, where the dust had gone the day before, and lifted her eyebrows.

"Most of them," he said. "Most of them walked."

He did not tell her about the river, how a man pressed back against the bank might go into the water and come up a long way off, where nobody was counting. He did not know her word for river. He was glad not to.

She put her fingers on the bundle of sticks, briefly, the way people at home touched the blessed weights for luck. Then she touched the back of his hand. She said something that ended in the name again, and turned, and went off down the river road, leaning on her stick, a small figure going the way some seven thousand men had gone the day before.

He had told her his name at some point in the morning, and she had told him hers, and he had lost it at once among the strange vowels. He never lost the other one.

* * *

He gave the sticks to the hundred-man, and the hundred-man carried them up to the king's tents, and he followed, because nobody told him not to.

The king was standing outside with his officers, looking at the field. He was a man of thirty-five, three years on the throne of old Mieeshimen, and the soldiers did not say aloud how he had come by it. He listened to the number. He looked at the bundle of willow in the hundred-man's hands the way a man looks at a fish that has come up smaller than it felt on the line. Then he nodded and turned back to the field, and an officer took the sticks, and that was the last the measurer saw of them.

That night at the fires the number began to grow. At the first fire he passed it was two thousand. By the time the pot was scraped it was four. The next night men who had stood behind him in the twentieth rank, who had never seen a man of Shetiy alive or dead, said six thousand, and the king's household men said more. The other counters had come in too, with two hundred and eighty-eight, the dead of [[pol:1214|Lishe]], and that number did not grow at all. It went round the fires quietly and was set down, and nobody wanted it any bigger.

He said nothing. A measurer does not argue in the market with a man who is telling a story about his harvest. That evening he watched the cooks feeding willow into the fires, peeled willow cut from the bank, and could not tell in the dark whether any of it was notched.

* * *

Two years later [[per:30579|Natezho]] set up a stone near [[set:200|Shetiy]], a limestone stele with letters cut into it by men who were paid to cut them straight. The lower part has since broken away. What survives says: "Natezho, king of Lishe, defeated Shetiy in Shetiy. He killed 10,700 soldiers. He erected this stele for Etlie, his lord."

That is more men than Shetiy brought to the river that day, by some nineteen hundred. By the stone's arithmetic every man who stood for [[per:30600|Khashsinish]] on that field is dead: the tall son, and the men who walked home carrying other men, and nineteen hundred more besides who were never born at all.

The stone has none of their names on it. Neither did the sticks. The measurer went home to the scales at [[set:277|Lishe]] and gave, people said, a very exact measure. He kept the name. It was the only thing he brought home from Shetiy that nobody ever asked him to make bigger.

**Author's note.** The stone at the end is PAL·IN·02326, set up in AY 3348; his rock relief for [[evt:30973|Yinishme]], PAL·IN·02325, claims a victory the record credits to Mashe Muee. The single surviving line of the Shetiy chronicle, PAL·CH·05582, has Khashsinish breaking a line "at the first charge" without naming the field; my measurer hears it only as rumour, and the mother's prayer borrows that chronicle's spelling of the faith. What the story leaves out, the record keeps: five years later Natezho died at Eol, in a battle Khashsinish won, and Lishe still won the [[evt:30953|Second Lishe–Shityi War]].
