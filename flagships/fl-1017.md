title: Written at Runiy

The order for treaty clay came down from the king's rooms at midday, and by evening the whole palace knew the war was over, because Tamaksit had gone to the jar.

She kept it in the shade of the north wall of the scribes' yard, with three wet cloths over its mouth and a flat stone on the cloths, and she had not lifted the stone since the gates were shut. Twice in her life the gate of the city had been broken and four times it had been shut against an army, and this was the fourth. The jar held the last of the good clay. She had dug it from the bank below the ford before the war came up to the walls, and soaked it, and let the sand go down, and poured off the fine part and let it settle again, and again, until there was nothing left in it but a grey silk that smelled of rain. Since the siege began, the grain scribes had been writing their lists on the yellow stuff from under the cellar floors, which took a sign the way sand takes a footprint and cracked as it dried. Good clay was not for lists.

She lifted the cloths and pushed her fist in to the wrist. Cold, and close as flesh. She pinched off a piece the size of a bean, set it between her front teeth and ground it gently, listening, the way her father had taught her when she was smaller than the jar. Nothing. Not one grain of sand.

Beyond the wall the cooking smoke of [[pol:184|Egu]] stood up from the hill in long grey stalks. In the evenings she could hear the soldiers singing over there, and they sang the songs of her own street, in her own words, because the men of Egu and the men of Runiy were one people, and had been one kingdom until the year Tamaksit was born. Runiy had stood on its own for exactly as long as she had been alive, which was forty-five years. Before the gates were shut, a measure of grain had cost an [[cur:52|ushut]] and a third of silver in the market. Now there was no market.

Her son was asleep in their room by the potters' gate, his right hand bound in a strip of her best wool. Nupdzeyshan had gone out with the old general Siburaman to meet the army of Egu below the city, some seven hundred men against three times as many, and more than two hundred of them had not come back. He had come back. He had come through the gate at dusk with the others, the old general walking last, and since then he had slept as if sleep were a post he had been ordered to hold.

* * *

This would be the sixth.

She had been eight the first time, the year the soldiers of Duegowinga broke the gate and went through the city like water through a basket, and afterwards sat down in the palace and made the king, who was a boy then, swear to their terms. Her father carried a jar like this one up to the palace that morning, and came home, and said nothing at supper. That treaty brought new gods into the palace.

The second and the third were written on silver, because the queen of Egu and the king of Yinnga wanted silver, and her clay was not needed. Her husband was dead by then. Ruban had gone out with Siburaman in the year of the first silver tablet, under these walls, and had not come in again with the rest. The fourth was clay, hers, for Duegowinga again. The fifth was not even Runiy's. Three years ago the armies of Egu and Duegowinga had fought each other in the fields below the city and then sworn their peace in the queen's camp under the walls, while Runiy watched from its own roofs like a man whose yard two strangers have borrowed to settle a quarrel.

The four that were Runiy's stood on the shelf in the tablet room behind her, in a row, the silver ones wrapped in wool. Tamaksit could not read. But the scribes always wrote the place last, at the foot, and she knew the signs for [[set:121|Runiy]] the way she knew the shape of her son's ears. On the tablets of Duegowinga and Yinnga the name was written in strangers' signs, and she had had to take the scribes' word that it was there at all. Only on Egu's silver was it written in the signs of her own people.

* * *

The king came to the yard after the lamps were lit.

[[per:4781|Kuttlomin]] had been king for twenty-four years, and he walked now the way men walk who have carried a load too long and been told to put it down. He had no one with him. He stood over the jar and looked into the clay as if it were a well.

— Is it good?

— It's the best I have, lord. It's the last I have.

He put one finger into it, like a child testing porridge, and looked at the hole he had made.

— They bring their own scribe, he said. They always bring their own scribe, and their own gods. Nobody ever brings clay.

She did not know what to say to that, so she laid the cloths back.

— You made the last one, he said. Duegowinga's.

— Yes, lord.

— And your father the first.

— Yes, lord.

— Not the silver.

— No, lord.

He went past her into the tablet room, and she took up the lamp and followed, because he plainly could not see in there and he did not tell her not to. He found the silver by touch. He unwrapped the first plate and held it to the flame, silver the breadth of two hands with the signs cut into it in rows, and the light ran along the rows like water along a ditch.

— This is Egu's, he said. Eighteen years. Sixteen talents of silver to her palace every year, for twenty years. They tell me there is no silver left in Runiy. They are wrong. There is this.

He weighed it on his palm.

— How many ushut would you say?

— I wouldn't know, lord. I've never held that much of anything.

— No, he said. Nor have I. Not for long.

He tilted the plate until the lamp found the last lines.

— Do you know what it says at the end? "Whoever changes the words of this tablet, may the river refuse him water and the field refuse him grain." He laughed, not much. If I melt it, I change the words. Then the river refuses me water. That would be like her.

— Like who, lord?

— The river.

In the palace the river was a woman. Her name was [[dei:141|Nabidin]]; the moon was a woman too, [[dei:138|Tubel]]; and the one who kept the dead below was a man, [[dei:137|Shuyedarsi]]. They were the gods of [[mov:21|Kunelmu]], and they had come in with the soldiers of Duegowinga in the year of the first treaty. That tablet stood on the shelf at the king's elbow, and every scribe in the palace could recite its clause about the king: "its ruler shall make offering at its altar." So the kings of Runiy had made it, every new moon, for thirty-seven years. But in the street where Tamaksit lived it was all the other way about. The river was Nabudzin, and he was a man; the moon was a man; and the dead went down to a woman, as any grandmother would tell you. Only the storm was a man on both sides of the palace door, and the grain a woman on both. The men singing on the hill prayed to the gods of the street. They always had.

— Twenty-four years I have made her offering, the king said. Barley and oil, and a lamb when there was a lamb. And she has given me one victory, under this wall, three years ago. Were you here?

— I heard it, lord. From the roof.

— More than eight hundred of theirs. I had the scribes count them twice, to be sure of it. One. And the queen of Egu has beaten me twice in the field with her own hand.

The second time had been two years ago, at the [[evt:4646|Battle of Yishdhagshal]]. Nupdzeyshan had been there too. He did not talk about it.

The king wrapped the silver again, carefully, the way a woman swaddles a child that has finally stopped crying, and set it back in its place beside the other.

— Keep the cloths wet, he said, and went out.

She noticed only later, lying awake, that he had not taken it with him.

* * *

They opened the gate at first light.

The queen of Egu came up to the palace on foot, with her guard behind her and her scribe at her side, and Tamaksit, standing against the wall of the yard beside the jar, knew her at once. She had seen her once before, eighteen years ago, from a roof, the year of the silver: a young woman then, in the first year of her reign. Betkulupat was forty-six now. She had a broad, tired face and a soldier's way of looking at a doorway before she went through it.

The scribe came straight to the jar. He was a thin man about Tamaksit's own age, with a reed case at his belt, and he did not look at her. He pressed his thumb into the clay. Then he pinched off a piece the size of a bean, put it between his front teeth and ground it, listening.

He looked at her then.

— Where is it from?

— Below the ford.

— Ours at home is half sand, he said, and wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. Make it for me. You know it better than I do.

So she made it. She took the clay out in two handfuls and beat the air out of it on the board, and folded it and beat it again, and shaped the tablet the way her father had shaped the first one: a pillow two hands broad, a little thicker at the middle, smoothed with a wet palm until the lamp could have seen itself in it. Her hands were steady. That surprised her.

They did it in the hall, in front of the palace gods. Tamaksit stood at the back with the wet cloth over her arm in case the clay began to dry. The queen spoke and the scribe wrote, and a man of Egu read the words back aloud in a herald's voice, and she understood every one of them, because they were in her own tongue. It was short, shorter than any tablet on the shelf. Runiy and its nine districts, its fields and its people, the city and everything in it, were Egu's. There was nothing about tribute and nothing about boundary stones, because there was no boundary left to set them on.

The king pressed his seal into the clay she had made, and then the queen pressed hers. Then the scribe bent over the foot of the tablet and wrote the last signs, and Tamaksit, at the back of the hall, could read them. That was the end of the kingdom of [[pol:192|Runiy]]. It had taken less time than kneading the clay.

Afterwards, when the queen had gone out to look at the walls, the king crossed the yard toward the small door of the shrine, as he must have crossed it every new moon for twenty-four years, and stopped halfway, like a man who has come into a room and forgotten why. He stood there a long while. Then he went in anyway.

* * *

In the afternoon she went out through the gate for the first time since it had been shut, with her digging stick and her basket, and in her fist the scraps the scribe had trimmed from the edges of the tablet, rolled into a ball.

The road down to the ford passed under the queen's stone. It had been cut into the rock the year Ruban died, and everyone in Runiy knew what it said, because a scribe had once read it out in the market and the children had made a skipping song of it: "Betkulupat, queen of Egu, defeated Runiy in Runiy. She killed 5,500 soldiers." Nearly seven hundred men of Runiy had been lost below the walls that day. The stone had counted each of them about eight times. Somewhere in it, Tamaksit had always thought, was Ruban, eight times over, which was more than he had ever been counted in his life.

At the ford the soldiers of [[pol:184|Egu]] were watering oxen. A boy younger than Nupdzeyshan crouched at the edge of the water with a pinch of meal in his fingers, and as she came down the bank he let it go on the current and said the god's name under his breath, Nabudzin, with the same flat vowels her mother had used. He saw her watching and ducked his head, as if he had been caught at something.

She knelt a little way downstream and opened her fist. The ball of clay had been in the hall when [[set:121|Runiy]] stopped being a kingdom. She did not want it in her house. She held it out over the water and let it go, and the river took it back without reading it.

Then she took up the digging stick and cut into the bank. Tomorrow the queen's people would want everything counted: the grain, the oil, the men, the silver on the shelf. Every count would want clay. And at the foot of every tablet, last of all, some scribe of Egu would write the only word she could read.
