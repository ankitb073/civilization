title: Unfired
---
Kupadiyat trod the clay barefoot in the long trough behind the house of tablets, and the boy sat on the step above her and spoiled whatever she gave him.

That was how she put it, and he took it as it was meant. She gave him the scraps, the ends of the day's tablets and the lumps too gritty for the scribes, and he pressed signs into them with a reed he had split himself, badly, so that every sign came out with a little tail. Sipabukin was ten. He was the youngest of the boys learning signs in the house, and the only one who came out to the trough when the teacher let them go. Writing had come to the kingdom only in the queen's first year; the teacher had learned his own signs as a grown man, and he held the boys to them as if they had been handed down from the gods.

"Look," he said.

He had written the year: *in the twentieth year of Mimrit*. The teacher wanted the year-name at the head of every exercise, and every morning Sipabukin found a new way to get the twentieth wrong.

"It looks like a sheep stood on it."

"It's right."

"Then the sheep was right." She stepped out of the trough and the clay came off her shins in grey cuffs. She looked at his tablet, at the tail on every sign, and gave it back. "Again. Then it goes in the water."

Everything unfired went back in the water in the end. Accounts of oil, tallies of fleeces, a boy's year: she soaked them in the trough and trod them, and they came up clay again, ready to be told something else. Only what went into the kiln stayed. The scribes decided what went into the kiln. Kupadiyat decided how long it stayed there and how hot, and she was proud of that, quietly, the way a woman is proud of knowing which stair creaks.

Before the levy marched she had put other things into the kiln. Sling-stones, a basketful, pinched out of tablet clay between her thumb and two fingers, each the size of a dove's egg, fired along with the month's accounts of oil. Lonatbin had laughed at them. He was nineteen, and the slingers of the city chose their own stones from the stream beds one by one and boasted of them. But he had tipped her basket into his bag all the same, because he was kind, and because he was going with Liltalin to Dzebampiba, where last year Egathan had fallen and the city had counted its dead for a month.

She had named him Lonatbin, bear spear, when he was a red thing in a cloth. He had grown up to throw stones.

* * *

The first of them came in by the inland road in the heat of the afternoon, and the city knew before they reached the gate, because they were walking in ones and twos and nobody was singing.

Kupadiyat went down with clay to her elbows. There was a crowd of women at the gate already, doing what she did: looking past each face to the one behind it. A man she knew from the fleece market sat in the shade of the wall with his arm strapped to his chest in somebody else's kilt.

"The slingers," she said. "The city's slingers."

"On the left." He shut his eyes. "There was no water. Three days, no water. Their king was there himself. You could see where he stood from anywhere on the field. They made sure we could."

"Lonatbin. Kupadiyat's son. Tall, he has—" She did not know what he had. A face. "He had a bag of clay stones. Mine."

The man opened his eyes and looked at her for some time, and she understood that he was trying to be kind and could not find the way in. "I saw slingers," he said.

Liltalin had taken three thousand five hundred and eighty-seven men to Dzebampiba. One thousand four hundred and eighty-four of them were lost there. [[set:94|Ushutnga]] learned that number one door at a time over the days that followed, and never as a number. People said: the dyer's two boys. They said: the whole lane behind the oil-pressers. They said that Liltalin had come home alive, and they said it in a particular voice.

Then the gates were shut and the flocks were driven in off the pasture, so that every lane was loud with sheep and slick with them. The old people said the city's name meant *place of sheep*, and now it was nothing else. The priests of the [[mov:5|Lelmi]] went up to the altar and gave the gods their due, and taught, as they always taught, that all things turn on the great wheel, that what ends will begin again. Kupadiyat thought it was a better thing to say over a harvest than over a son.

She went back to the trough, because the clay did not care, and the boy sat on the step and wrote the year.

* * *

Nobody in the house of tablets knew afterwards how the gate was lost. By evening there were three stories, and by morning five. What everyone agreed on was the noise: bronze on bronze down by the gate, and then the sheep, every flock in the city crying at once as the lanes filled with men who were not from Ushutnga.

There was no moon. [[dei:34|Sevni]] had turned his face from the city, the boy said, and Kupadiyat said that Sevni turned his face away every month and nobody sacked anything for it. She took Sipabukin into the back room where the baskets of tablets stood in rows, put out the lamp, and sat with him against the wall, between the fired tablets and the jars of unfired ones, under the niche where the scribes left a pinch of clay each morning for [[dei:37|Novku]], who is wise. The boy had his tablet in his fist. She let him keep it.

They heard the granary go: not fire, but the long dry hiss of grain being poured, the shouting of men who had found it, the squeal of cart axles. The bread of [[dei:36|Nidziktti]], which the queen had given to the priests, went down the hill on other people's wheels. Later they heard the fire. The wind was off the land that night, and it laid the fire down over the harbour quarter, over the rope sheds and the timber yards and the boats drawn up on the shingle, and the light of it came under the door the colour of a kiln's mouth.

The men of [[pol:373|Buneynga]] came in with a torch. There were three of them. They spoke to each other in their own tongue, quickly, the way men speak when they are tired and afraid of missing something, and they tipped the baskets out. Fired tablets rang on the floor like crockery. Unfired ones fell with a sound like dough. There was no silver in any of them, and when the men understood that, the oldest of them laughed, not unkindly, at the whole room.

Kupadiyat stood up. She held out her hands, grey to the wrist, open, so that they could see there was nothing in them. *Clay*, she said in her own tongue. *Only clay.*

The youngest of them took the tablet out of the boy's fist. He turned it to the torch. He could not read it; nobody could have read it who had not sat on that step for a year. He weighed it in his palm the way a man weighs silver, and found it was not silver. Then he took her bronze knife from the bench, the one she cut the tablets with, and looked at the boy for longer than she liked, and went out after the others through the yard. As he passed the trough he dropped the tablet in. It went under with a small cold sound.

Sipabukin was at the trough before she could stop him. He fished it up. It was already soft at the edges, the year going vague under his thumbs, the twentieth year of Mimrit running back into the water with all the little tails of his signs.

"Leave it," she said. "It's clay. It knows how."

Ash came down all night through the smoke-hole and lay on the spilled tablets like flour.

* * *

The peace was cried at the gate some days later, by a man with a voice like a temple horn and nobody behind him. It had been sworn in the city, by the queen and by the king of Buneynga, and none of the women at the gate had seen it. The crier gave the terms. Runiy and the land about it, fifteen districts, to Buneynga. Silver to the palace of Buneynga every year for thirty years. The merchants of Buneynga to pass through the land, and no toll taken of them. And for ten years no man of the one land to take up arms against the other.

"Ten years," said a woman beside Kupadiyat. "That's something."

"Thirty years," the boy said. He was doing it on his fingers. "I'll be forty."

"I'll be with [[dei:38|Shedarsi]]," Kupadiyat said, "and glad of the quiet."

Barley had been a [[cur:195|Kalu]] and a quarter of silver the measure before the levy marched. Now there was no barley to put a weight of silver to. The sheep were gone, driven off up the inland road, and the city that was named for them stood in its own smell of wet ash.

That evening, at the trough, she asked him to write a name.

"Not the year," she said. "Just him."

He wrote it slowly, without the tails, the way the teacher always asked and never got: *Lonatbin, son of Kupadiyat*. She looked at it for a long time, holding it flat on her palm the way you hold a bird you do not want to frighten.

"Shall I put it in the kiln?" He knew what the kiln was for.

"No," she said. "When he comes home, I'll put it in the water myself."

She set it on the sill of the clay-room, unfired, facing the inland road.

* * *

In the twenty-fourth year of [[per:9112|Sibubunen]], Sipabukin, scribe of the house of tablets, sat down to set out the annals of the kingdom from the sixth year of Lathutuin to the year he was in. He was forty, as he had said he would be. The queen was long dead, and Kupadiyat longer. The tablet had stood on the sill through the dry months, and when the rains came it softened at the corners first and then at the signs, and by the end of the rains it was a grey lump with a thumbprint in it. She had never moved it. The rain had.

He came to the nineteenth year of Mimrit and wrote it as the court had always told it:

> In the 19th year of Mimrit. There was a battle at Dzebampiba; it went ill.

For the twentieth year there had been another battle at Dzebampiba, and he wrote that too, and looked at it lying under the first like an echo, and smoothed it out with his thumb while the clay was wet. The court had never wanted two of them. He found that he could give that one back to the clay, the way she would have.

> In the 20th year of Mimrit. [[set:94|Ushutnga]] was sacked by the enemy.

The court wanted no more than that, and he gave it no more. Fourteen years further down the clay, he had to write it again, word for word, because it had happened again:

> In the 8th year of Sibubunen. Ushutnga was sacked by the enemy.

What ends will begin again, the priests said. He had never liked them for it. He read the two lines once more, the same words a hand's width apart on the clay, and then he carried the tablet out to the kiln behind the house, where the trough had stood, and this one he fired.
