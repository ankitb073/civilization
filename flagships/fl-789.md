*To the son of my daughter, at the school in the city, from his grandfather at the dry pastures, greeting. In the year 862 of the [[mov:62|Nidunde]].*

You write that your teacher is making a book of the long drought, and wants the old men to set down what they remember before they forget it or die, whichever comes first. I have dictated this to the priest's boy, who spells better than I do and argues less. Tell your teacher he may cut what he likes.

I was a boy of eight, keeping my father's goats, when the rains first failed. I was past fifty when they came back. Forty-four summers, child. A man's whole life between, marriage, children, his father's grave, all of it under a white sky. The learned now call it the [[evt:27776|Great Drought in Daadüdhih]], as if it were one event, like a battle. To us it was not an event. It was the weather. It was the world.

The first years were the worst, because we did not yet know how to live in it. There was the great hunger, and the dead were buried in the stony ground because nobody could dig deeper. Whole families of the hill people put their pots on their donkeys and went away down the long road towards Khishkhih, and we never heard of them again. A measure of grain in the market at [[set:82|Yad]] went to four [[cur:534|Fyiu]] and more. My mother sold her earrings, then her loom.

After that we learned. We gave up the cattle, which drink like lords, and kept goats, which drink like priests. We dug the wells deeper every year, a man's height at a time, and roped the boys down to clear them. We learned to move before the grass was gone, and not after. Here is a philosophy, if your teacher wants one: a man owns nothing he cannot carry, and no ground he cannot water.

The kings of [[pol:980|Yad]] were still kings when the drought began. In its middle years they ended, and their coin went with them. I still reckon in Fyiu, as old men do. By that reckoning grain is two-thirds of a Fyiu the measure now, and the young men laugh at me for saying so.

The teachers of the Nidunde came out to the pastures in those years, when nobody else did. They said that the good life is compassion, and that there is a release from suffering. I did not understand the release. I understood the compassion. They carried water to the widows' tents with their own shoulders, and asked for nothing, not even to be believed. Many in the pastures who had only bowed to the teaching took it to heart for that alone. A doctrine is argued in the cities. In the hills it is carried, or it is not.

When the rain came back, the first true rain, I stood in it with my mouth open like a fool, and so did my wife, and so did every grown person in the camp. Only the children ran inside. They had never seen it, and they were afraid.

The [[cli:158|Great Drought]] is over, they tell me, and has been for twenty-five years. But come and look at the pastures, child. The grass came back; the trees did not. The old springs are holes full of stones. The wells are still deep, and the boys still go down on the rope. We still say "a dry year" when we mean an ordinary one.

Study well. Be kind to your teacher, and kinder to his book. Write to your grandmother; she cannot read it, but she likes to hold the letter.
