title: Elegy for Those Who Fell for Yiotl

Not for the living is this song, although the living must hear it;  
not for the victors, for we were not the victors; but for the fallen.  
Fourteen years have turned since the oath was sworn at [[set:229|Ivo]],  
and still the mothers of [[set:211|Ool]] set out a cup that no one drinks.  

Let no one say they went out greedy, or for plunder, or in folly.  
Our fathers had driven the spear of [[pol:922|Ana]] from our fields  
in the [[evt:24015|First Ana–Yisamashtsi War]], and taught them the price of coming;  
the sons believed the gods would give a second lesson in their turn.  
They went out for [[reg:5|Yiotl]], which our songs had always called our own,  
and so began the [[evt:24529|Yisamashtsi–Ana War]]. Judge them by their courage, not their fortune.  

Remember [[per:24087|Ea Tluee]] at the [[evt:24558|Battle of Ivu]],  
when thirty thousand of [[pol:957|Yisamashtsi]] stood against more than seventy thousand,  
and the field was ours, and the enemy counted his dead by the cartload.  
Remember [[per:24386|Yoni Sima]] at the [[evt:24599|Battle of Nyinsitlasa]],  
where the host of Ana broke on our line as surf upon a mole;  
and how that same year our standards stood before the walls of [[set:7|Ana]]  
in the [[evt:24600|Third Siege of Ane]], and the enemy's women watched from the parapet.  
Remember [[per:24430|Yi Lieze]] at the [[evt:24640|Battle of Pivithie]],  
in sight of the towers of Pibetie, where six thousand of Ana lay down and did not rise.  

These were our days. A lament may count its glories, for it has little else to count.  

But the gods weigh with a scale we do not see,  
and the field that was given on one day was taken on another.  
At Nyinsitlasa we lost what we had won at Nyinsitlasa; at Ivu we lost what we had won at Ivu.  
Our present king, [[per:23898|Yi Luesha]], stood in the front rank at the [[evt:24656|Battle of Nyinsitlasa]],  
and no one who stood beside him says he turned his back.  
At the last [[evt:24669|Battle of Ivu]] the fortune of the city went down,  
and after three more years of grief the king went to Ivo, and swore the oath, and opened our markets to Ana.  

Here the teachers of [[mov:25|Mietseyeshio]] would have me pause, and I will pause.  
They say: justice in dealings, moderation in all things;  
the wise man withdraws from appetite, and a city is only many men.  
Was it appetite that sent our sons to Yiotl? Perhaps it was.  
But the fallen did not choose the war; they chose only not to run from it.  
The fault of a city is the city's; the courage of a soldier is his own.  
Let the elders carry the one, and let the dead keep the other.  

The scribes have counted our dead: fifty thousand and more, from the first year to the last.  
They have counted the dead of Ana also: more than eighty thousand.  
I set the numbers down, not as a boast, for what is a boast over a grave?  
I set them down so that the sons of Ana, when they read their own tablets,  
will know what it cost them to keep a field that our fathers had held.  

Now the grief is not old, and a new grief is laid upon it,  
for at Faamni our sons fell again, and the [[evt:24915|Treaty of Faamni]] was sworn,  
and silver went out of the treasury of Ool that will not come back.  
Even so, citizens, do not stop your ears to the old dead for the sake of the new.  
Each has a name. Each had a mother. Each was a citizen of Ool.  

Pour, then. Pour for those who lie at Nyinsitlasa and at Ivu and at Pivithie.  
Pour for those who came home with no wound you could see.  
Pour moderately, as the teachers bid, but do not cease to pour.  

*Written in the year 1503 from the founding of Ool.*
