title: Weighed Twice
---
The quartermaster of [[pol:1795|Tomzel]] weighed every coin twice. Once on his own scales, which folded into a box of dark wood, and once on Nakhnen's, which were a horn beam and two leather pans that had weighed wool on the plateau above [[set:240|Tyiush]] since before Nakhnen's grandfather was born. He did it, he said, because he was a man of the [[mov:25|Myitiyishyu]], and the Myitiyishyu asked for justice in dealings, and a herder who could not read a receipt was owed a weight he could see.

Tsivutun sat on the edge of the felt with his chin on his knees and watched the silver go down into the pan. The Tomzel coins were broad and dull. The quartermaster, who came from [[set:264|Ölye]], called them [[cur:995|Sozhne]] and said the word as if it were a prayer. Each one, on either set of scales, weighed exactly what two of the Watlshi coins weighed. Tsivutun's mother, who kept a little purse of Watlshi coins sewn inside her coat, said nothing about this for the whole of the bargaining. Afterwards she bit one of the heavy ones at the edge, looked at the mark her teeth had made, and put it away with the face she kept for a ewe that had twinned.

Thirty wethers. The quartermaster's boys drove them off toward the western rim, where the smoke of the camp lay over the grass like a second weather.

"Tomorrow," the quartermaster said, "you will want to be somewhere else."

"We know," said Nakhnen.

"How many of you are there?" Tsivutun asked.

His father put a hand on the back of his neck. But the quartermaster did not seem to mind. He opened his book first, as if the number might change on its way from the paper to his mouth, and read it out. "Sixty-three thousand six hundred and seventy-eight. And every one of them eats." He shut the book. "More men than live in that town."

Tsivutun looked down at Tyiush: its roofs the colour of dust, its stream bright between the gardens, the market where the Mienibe sold their wool and bought their grain. He tried to think of the town twice over, all of it walking. He could not.

"And theirs?" said Nakhnen.

"A third of that. [[per:43909|Myimanyime]] has them."

Nakhnen laughed, once, through his nose. "Him again."

Two summers before, the armies had come to the upper grass to the north, where the flocks went in the hottest weeks, and fought there, and the same Watlshi general had lost. Tsivutun had been ten. He remembered the ravens most, and that for a year afterwards the sheep would not graze one stretch of slope, and nobody could make them.

"Tlamfin Ash beat him there," the quartermaster said, nodding, the way a man nods at an old horse whose paces he knows. "This time it's [[per:43932|Isyi Mof]]."

He said the name the way you say a new one.

* * *

They went down into the ravine in the dark: the flock, the dogs, the tents struck and rolled, Tvunan's household and theirs. The stream ran shallow over stones there and made a sound like someone talking in the next tent. At first light Tsivutun's mother walked along the packed backs of the sheep with her hands out, touching, and he knew she was counting, because her lips moved, and because she always did.

The sun came over the rim. For a long time nothing happened. A lark went up. Tvunan said there would be no battle at all, the Watlshi would think better of it, and his wife told him to be quiet.

Then the guns.

The sound did not come with the smoke. Tsivutun, on his belly at the lip of the ravine, saw a white puff bloom on the far side of the plateau, and then another, and counted three heartbeats before the noise came up through the ground into his chest; and by then there were twenty puffs, and the noise was one noise. The smoke did not rise. There was no wind on the plateau that day. It lay on the grass, white, and the lines of men went into it and came out of it changed: shorter, or broken in the middle, or not at all.

He could tell the armies apart only by where they had started. The Tomzel came from the west in blocks, so many blocks that at first he thought the ones behind were the same ones seen again. The Watlshi stood between them and the town, with Tyiush at their backs, as if the town were holding them up.

In the ravine the sheep pressed against one another and would not eat. The dogs lay flat. His mother stood all morning with her hand on the bellwether's neck.

Toward the middle of the afternoon the Watlshi line came apart the way an old felt comes apart when you pull it: not all at once, but in a dozen places, and then all at once. Men ran for the town and along its edge and past it. Horsemen came out of the smoke after them.

That was when the mare came up the ravine.

She came at a stumbling trot with her saddle slipped under her belly and her reins trailing, a bay with a cut on her shoulder that had run blood down the foreleg to the hoof. The sheep broke from her. Tsivutun went down the bank without asking anyone, slowly, with his hand flat and low, the way his father had taught him with the young horses, saying the nonsense you say to horses, and she stopped in the stream and stood shaking and let him take the rein.

His name meant horse fire. It was the first time he had felt he deserved it.

* * *

Next day the field belonged to Tomzel.

The Mienibe brought the flock back up because there was nowhere else for a flock to go, and kept it to the northern edge, away from the long trenches the Tomzel were digging. The smell came anyway. The dogs had to be tied.

The quartermaster came in the afternoon for twenty more wethers. He looked tired, and he was as careful with the scales as before. Nakhnen asked him how it had gone, and the quartermaster opened his book.

"Six hundred and eighty-four of ours. Two thousand three hundred and forty-one of theirs." He shut it. "The clerks had it by noon. Write it down while it's true, my father used to say. It doesn't stay true for long."

He did not ask about horses. Tomzel soldiers had been through the ravines at dawn with ropes, gathering up what had run, and Tsivutun had stood the bay behind the rocks where the stream came out of the hill, with her nose in a bag of the grain they had been saving and his heart going like the guns.

That evening Nakhnen looked at her for a long time.

"An army horse in a Mienibe tent," he said, "is a rope for somebody's neck." He meant his own. "Tvunan heard it in the town. Envoys are coming from [[pol:1867|Watlshi]], to swear the peace at Tyiush. We'll take her down and give her back to her own people, and maybe they'll give us something for her, and maybe they won't."

"She's mine," said Tsivutun.

"She's the king's," said his father. "Everything with a saddle on it is the king's."

* * *

They kept her in the ravine until the envoys came. On the day of the swearing Tyiush was so full of strangers that the stream through the market ran brown. There were Tomzel standards at one end of the town and Watlshi at the other, and priests in the street between, and in the yard of the big house by the old wall, where the swearing was to be done, two rows of horses so fine that the bay looked like what she was: a mare from the ranks with a mended shoulder.

The Watlshi grooms did not want her. A horse that came back was a horse somebody had to account for. They argued over Tsivutun's head in the court speech, which he only half followed, and while they argued he learned three things he had not known that morning.

The king of Watlshi was twelve years old.

The king's mother had been queen before him, and four summers ago she had commanded in the field herself, and been killed there, by a town in the north.

And that town was one of the things being given to Tomzel today, inside the house, with the rest.

"Twelve," said one of the grooms, as if it were the boy's own fault, and spat. "It was the priests made him do it."

Tsivutun was twelve. He thought about it with the mare's rein wound round his fist. He tried to think of himself giving away the place where his mother had died, and found that he could not think of his mother dying, and stopped.

The door of the house opened and a boy came out into the yard.

He was smaller than Tsivutun, and his coat was too heavy for the day, and two men in long dark clothes came behind him and stopped in the doorway as if they had been told to. The grooms went down on their knees. Tsivutun, a little late, did the same, and the mare put her nose into his hair and blew.

The king walked along the row of fine horses without looking at them and stopped at the bay.

"Whose is this?"

He spoke the town speech carefully, like a lesson. The head groom began to explain. The king did not look at him. He looked at Tsivutun.

"She came up the ravine," Tsivutun said, "on the day. I caught her."

"You saw it?"

"From the ravine."

The king put out a hand and touched the cut on the mare's shoulder, which had closed, and took the hand away again. "Where do you live?"

Tsivutun pointed with his chin, as the Mienibe did, at the plateau above the town: the long grey edge of it against the sky, where, if you knew where to look, you could see the smoke of the Tomzel camp and, smaller, to one side, the smoke of his mother's fire.

The king looked up at it for longer than Tsivutun had ever seen anyone look at anything.

"Keep her," he said.

The head groom said something about the horse-roll. The king turned and went back into the house, and the men in dark clothes closed the door behind him.

* * *

Nakhnen heard the rest in the market and told it on the climb home, with the bay walking between them. The town stayed Watlshi: the walls, the stream, the priests, the market where the Mienibe sold their wool. The grass went to Tomzel, to a king called [[per:43712|Yinumamshi]] whom none of them had seen: the plateau and the upper pasture and the slopes between, everything above the town where the flocks went in summer. Twenty years of truce were sworn on top of it.

"Since this morning," Nakhnen said. "While you were on your knees in that yard."

Tsivutun walked a while.

"He knew," he said.

His father did not ask who.

At the top the mare put her head down at once and began to eat, tearing the short grass with the sound horses make, which is the most peaceful sound there is.

The quartermaster came in the morning for the last of the wethers. The army was going home before the harvest. He saw the bay at once, the way a man who buys horses sees one.

"That's an army mare."

"She was given me," said Tsivutun.

"Given you." The quartermaster had his book open on his knee. He looked at her teeth, and at her shoulder, and then at the boy, with the face of a just man who has found an error in a sum. "By whom?"

"The king."

"Which king?"

Tsivutun looked down at the town, which was Watlshi, and round at the grass, which was Tomzel, and at the mare, who was eating it.

"The small one," he said.

The quartermaster considered this for some time. Then, because he was a just man, he wrote nothing down.
