To the elders and magistrates of [[set:52|Ihamiw]], who sit in judgement at the gate of the market, a merchant of the city, a dealer in cloth and oil these thirty years, sends greeting and asks a hearing.

I do not come before you as one who curses the king. Let no man say it. I have prayed for the house of [[per:21154|Khikh]] at every new moon since he took the seat of his father, and I will pray for it at the next. I come as one who has kept honest weights all my life, and who finds that the city has not.

You know the matter; it is in every mouth. This year the mint has struck the new [[cur:491|If]], and the new If is not the old If. Lay one of each upon the balance, as I have done before witnesses, and you will see what the herald did not proclaim: of every ten parts of silver in the coin of our fathers, the coin of our sons holds scarcely seven. The face of the king is as fair upon it as ever. Only the silver has gone out of it, as a guest leaves a feast before the reckoning.

What does this [[evt:21607|debasement of the If]] mean to a man who buys and sells? Hear it plainly. The grain-sellers have already learned to weigh what they are paid, and they ask two If and more for a measure, and the measure is no larger. The caravan masters will not take the new coin at the old reckoning; they ask the old coin, or silver by weight. The man who owed me a hundred If in the spring now pays me a hundred If in the autumn and is quit, though he has given me seventy in substance. Is this justice? It is a law that rewards the debtor and punishes the lender, and calls the theft by the name of the king.

I am told the cause, and I do not doubt it. The treasury has bled for a generation. It began to bleed at the [[evt:21376|battle of Kivunge]], when the host of the empire fell on us like a flood on a cornfield, and it bled again at the [[evt:21395|Treaty of Iazhash]], when four districts and their revenues went out of our hands. Then came the [[evt:21559|Ihamiw–Toyinmiwa War]], and the soldiers had to be paid, and the levies fed, five years together. I honour those who fought at the [[evt:21577|battle of Ashmath]] and the [[evt:21630|battle of Listlitl]]. By the [[evt:21631|Treaty of Listlitl]], seventeen districts came to [[pol:911|Ihamiw]], and the merchants of Ihamiw were given privileges in the markets of Toyinmiwa. We rejoiced at it.

But consider the jest that fortune has made of us. The sword opened the markets of Toyinmiwa to our trade, and the mint has closed them again; for what merchant in those towns will take a coin which its own makers have confessed to be light? A victory bought with silver is not repaid by silver drawn out of every purse in the city. The treasury has not paid its debts; it has merely made all of us pay them, by stealth, and in proportion to our honesty.

The priests of [[mov:72|Ishiu]] teach that the wise withdraw from wealth and appetite, and I do not dispute it. I am not wise; I am a merchant. But it seems to me that a teaching meant for the soul has been applied to the coinage, and that the If has withdrawn from its silver more piously than any of us has withdrawn from his appetite. Time runs in a line to the judgement, the priests say; then let the mint remember that it too will be weighed.

I ask three things of the elders. First, that the old coin be received at the tax office at its true weight, and not called equal to the new. Second, that contracts made before the new striking be paid in the old coin, or in silver by weight. Third, that the elders carry to the king the petition of the market: that this be the last time, and not the first of many.

A city is a promise kept between strangers. The coin is the seal on that promise. When the seal is false, every bargain becomes a quarrel.

Written in the year 2528 from the founding of Ihamiw, in the month after the new striking.
