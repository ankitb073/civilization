## A name and a city

The record keeps very little of [[per:2192|Piscualosh'a]]. He was a man of the Nishmam people, born in AY 486, who died a natural death in AY 529, at about forty-three. His name meant *bear stone*. No parent, wife or child is entered against him, and he held one office for a single year: inventor, in AY 525, tied to one event, the [[evt:1846|invention of scribal bureaucracy in Tilcui]]. Of his face, his earlier trade, his house and his grave the record says nothing, and I will not fill the silence.

It does keep his city. [[pol:28|Tilcui]] was a Bronze Age city-state ruled from the harbour town of [[set:131|Shiüzhi]]. The survey of the 490s counts 309,148 people in ten districts and an army of 1,855 men. Its silver was the [[cur:20|Sazhon]] of 8.40 grams. Its faith, [[mov:17|Nishmam]], held that the good life was duty, order and right sacrifice, and that prosperity was a sign of the gods' favour. He was eleven when I'a Lu seized the throne from the old queen I'cuil Losh'a in AY 497. I'a Lu's daughter [[per:2079|Sha Sim'am]] ruled from 506 until her death in 525, and the city grew steadier in each survey of her reign.

## The tablet at the market gate

Nearly everything else comes from one text in his own voice, the "Tidings of the House of Tablets" (PAL·NP·01078), dated AY 526, "the 2nd year of Pizha". The clay original is lost. A medieval copy, with several leaves missing, survives in the university library at Shiüzhi. It is a proclamation, set up "at the gate of the market … so that every man who passes may have it read to him."

Piscualosh'a tells the king: "My lord, your storehouses are counted by the memory of old men. The old men forget. The old men die." The king says, "Do it." Scribes sit "in rows, ten and ten", each with a charge: the grain, the silver, the boats, the levy ("Each house, how many sons; each son, how many years"). Every tablet is sealed, and every seal has a man who answers for it. One copy goes to the palace and one to the house of the gods, and "If the two do not agree, the scribe is beaten."

Neither writing nor the argument was new. A praise-poem of AY 3 from Ane (PAL·PO·00030) already recalls the time when "The steward counted the barley, and the count flew away." Shiüzhi had a scribal school long before him: a king list copied by a pupil in AY 369 (PAL·SX·00042) still bears the teacher's note, "Write it again, ten times." What was new was the machine around the writing: records divided by office, every tablet with a twin, every scribe answerable for his seal. I have checked every state in the record, and none had a scribal bureaucracy before Tilcui. Tantöti and one other state had it by 541; by AY 600 twelve states besides Tilcui had it.

## What the tablet claims

The tablet is advocacy. The record dates the invention to AY 525, the year Sha Sim'am died and her younger brother Pizha took the throne, and does not say in which reign the scribes first sat down. The tablet has no doubt: "In the first year of Pizha the king". The queen who had ruled for nineteen years is not named in what survives. Perhaps the lost leaves named her; perhaps a new king's servant knew whom to thank.

"Not one measure went out at the back door" cannot be tested. The price boast can. Barley, the tablet says, "costs less than one sazhon", and the record's prices at Shiüzhi put grain near 0.85 sazhon in the 520s. But it had been below one sazhon in every decade since the 480s, and in the 530s it rose to about 1.04. The treasury grew from about 16,600 kg of silver at the end of the 520s to 17,200 at the end of the 530s, under 4 per cent. Stability had been climbing since the 500s survey, before any house of tablets.

What rings true is the man's temper. He sharpened the priests' doctrine of favour: "I say: the gods favour the house that counts." He spoke past the court to "men of the harbour, men of the fields, men of the potters' street", and promised learners that "A scribe eats bread every day, in the drought year and in the flood year." He even quoted his mockers: "Piscualosh'a plays with mud."

## After the counting

He died in 529 and did not see what the counting would serve. In 543 Tantöti made war on Tilcui for tribute. The city's general won at Shiüzhi in 545, losing 125 men to the attackers' 1,092; in 547 Pizha led 589 men against 6,036 at Imshi and was killed. That year Shiüzhi was sacked for the second time, and the record notes that its archives burned. Piscualosh'a had prayed that the storm god would "spare the house of tablets from fire". Whether his house was among the archives lost, the record does not say.

The peace of 548 took two districts, made Tilcui a vassal and imposed 6.4 talents of silver a year for 39 years. By the end of the 540s the state counted 233,253 people, against 317,986 in the 530s survey. The treaty was written at Shiüzhi by Nisli'agw, a scribe of Tantöti (PAL·TR·00106). Whether Tantöti learned the craft from Tilcui the record does not say, but the conqueror counted too.

"May the tablet outlast the man who wrote it," he prayed. His clay is gone; his words live because a later copyist thought them worth copying. What outlasted him was a habit: rows of scribes, sealed tablets, two copies that had to agree, where once the only ledger had been the memory of old men.
