**On the King's Stamped Silver: What It Is, How It Was Made, and Why It Serves the City.**  
*Posted at the market gate of [[set:205|Pivish]] and read aloud by the criers, in the year 321 of Pivish.*

Citizens of [[pol:740|Pivish]], you will by now have handled the new silver, and many of you, I am told, have bitten it. You are right to bite it. A thing that asks for your trust should be tested; and since I am the one who made it, it falls to me to answer for it. I am [[per:20386|I'ayof]], assayer of the king's treasury, and what follows is the plain account of the [[evt:20545|invention of coinage in Pivish]], which took place last year under the reign of our lord [[per:20359|Nish'i]], and with his leave.

**What it is.** Each piece is a lump of silver of one weight, no more and no less, cast in a mould, and struck while warm between two dies, so that it bears on its face the mark of the king. That is all. There is no magic in it. Yet consider what follows from so small a thing.

**How it was before.** Until last year, when a fisherman sold his catch to a ship's master from across the water, the two of them would stand at the scales for half the morning, quarrelling over the weight of a rough bar and the purity of its metal, and calling on [[dei:6|Nili]] of the river to witness that the other was a thief. The fish meanwhile spoiled. Every bargain was a lawsuit, and the man who owned the best scales, or the most convincing face, grew rich, while the honest man grew tired.

**How it was made.** I learned assaying in the treasury from the old masters, who weighed the tribute bar by bar. It came to me, as I watched them, that the treasury was weighing the same silver again and again, each time it changed hands, and that this labour could be done once, at the source, and certified for ever. The king heard me. He gave me a furnace and four workmen. We made our offering to [[dei:7|Yof]] of the fire, who governs the melting of metals, before we lit it. The first pieces were ugly; the dies cracked. By the end of the summer we had a mould that did not leak and a die that did not split, and the king's mark came up clean.

**Why it serves the city.** This is not merely a matter of convenience. Our faith, the faith of [[mov:1|Ya'kam]], teaches that prosperity is a sign of divine favour, and that the good life is one of duty and right order. But what is a market without order? A crowd shouting. And what is prosperity that every man must defend at the scales with his fists? It is a quarrel with a profit at the end. The stamped piece puts order into the market. It says: the king has weighed this, and the king answers for it. Thus every buyer and every seller in Pivish now leans, a little, on the honesty of the crown; and the crown, if it is wise, will take care to deserve it.

Some of the older merchants object that a piece of silver is worth what it weighs and no more, and that the king's mark adds nothing. They are half right. The mark adds no silver. It adds trust, which is rarer. Others say that the ship-masters speak of stamped silver in lands across the water, and that I have merely copied it. I have never held such a piece; if such makers exist, I wish them well, and I note that their silver is not ours.

Others ask: what if a king should one day put less silver in the piece and keep the difference? I answer: then he would be stealing from every citizen at once, and [[dei:8|Nish'il]] of the sky, who sees all dealings, would see that too. I would not make the dies for him if he did.

**A request.** Do not clip the pieces. Do not shave them. A clipped piece is a broken promise, and the treasury will take it only by weight.

The wheel turns, citizens. What our grandfathers did with scales, our grandchildren will do with a handful of pieces and a nod. Let us be the generation that began it well.
