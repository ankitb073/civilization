*Extracts from the diary of a secretary of the presidential household, printed by permission.*

**7 Ushuth.** Rain since dawn. The Assembly sat until after ten. I carried three sets of papers across the square and back, and each time the deputies were shouting the same things at one another. The President did not attend. He sat in the green room with the ministers of war and of the interior and sent out for coffee twice. The cook says that when men send out for coffee twice after nine they are planning something. The cook is usually right.

Tonight the President said to me, as I was gathering the day's telegrams: "Do you remember [[evt:45876|Viradhuthpi]]?" I said I was a child then. He said: "I was a young man. I have never forgotten it. Two battles lost in two years, and then [[evt:45877|a treaty]] signed by men in frock coats. That is what parliaments do with defeats. They sign them." I did not know what to answer, and so I said nothing, which is the first duty of a secretary.

**8 Ushuth.** Soldiers in the square at first light, in good order, with their rifles slung. No shots. The telegraph office on the Avenue was closed "for repairs" before nine. The trams stopped at noon. The newspapers of the opposition did not appear in the afternoon; the newspapers of the government appeared with blank columns where the parliamentary report should have been.

At the palace everything was strangely calm. The President received the commanders of the garrison one after another. The commander of the guard, an old soldier who was at [[evt:46071|Khash]] and at Linririnu, came out of the green room looking ten years younger.

**9 Ushuth.** The decree was read on the steps at eleven. I stood behind the ministers and heard it twice, once from the steps and once from the echo off the Treasury. The Assembly is suspended. The parties are dissolved, all but one. "The Republic of [[pol:1850|Bene]] has been a house divided against itself for nearly fifty years," the decree says. "From today it will be one house, with one door and one master of the house."

The crowd in the square was larger than I expected, and quieter. Some cheered. Many simply stood. A woman near the railings said loudly that she did not care who governed so long as bread stayed at a [[cur:1000|Khürew]] the measure. A policeman told her to go home. She did not go home, and he did not make her.

**10 Ushuth.** Priests of [[mov:57|Gigishyu]] came to the palace in the morning, in their grey, by the side door. I do not know what was said. Afterwards one of the younger ones told me, as I walked him to the gate, that the wheel turns, that what ends will begin again, and that compassion is owed to rulers as to everyone else, "because they suffer more than they know." I wrote it down because I did not understand it, and I think I was meant not to.

In the evening the deputies who had refused to leave the Assembly hall were escorted out. No one was hurt. Their names were taken.

**11 Ushuth.** It is done. At noon the President came out on the balcony of the palace at [[set:100|Khuele]], and the chief of the party announced that he would no longer be called President but Chairman. [[per:45479|Ebuthnilin]] spoke for a quarter of an hour. He spoke of order. He spoke of the victories at Khash and at [[evt:46084|Linririnu]], twelve years ago, which proved, he said, that the nation could win when it acted as one man. He did not speak of the Assembly at all. He spoke of bread, and promised it would not rise.

When he came in from the balcony his hands were shaking, and he asked me for a glass of water, and stood holding it without drinking it.

I record, for myself, that I have served three presidents in this house and have now begun to serve a chairman, and that the furniture is the same, and the papers are carried across the same square. I record also that I am a man of Bene, and that whatever this [[evt:46207|revolution]] becomes, I will be a man of Bene when it is over. I pray the wheel turns gently.

The telegraph office has reopened. The repairs, I am told, were successful.
