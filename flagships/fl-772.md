title: Lament of a farming village outside Kielish in the year of its conquest
---
We who till the olive terraces outside [[set:137|Kielish]] have no orator, and so we must make our own lament, as a man who has no carpenter must mend his own door, badly but with feeling.

In the market they used to say that a free man is one who knows whose law he lives under. By that measure we were free this spring and are something else by autumn. We are not slaves. Nobody has put a collar on us. We still own our trees, our oxen and our debts. But the law we live under was written in Nambe, by men who have never seen our terraces, and that is a new kind of thing to us.

Two years ago the men of [[pol:793|Nambe]] came against us. Our king, [[per:18679|Naguf]], who had reigned over [[pol:797|Kielish]] for seven-and-twenty years, went out against them, and they beat him. We told each other it was one battle, that a city is not lost in one battle. This year they came again, and there was a second [[evt:19188|Battle of Yilish]], and they beat our army again, and then they sat down before the walls. During the [[evt:19189|Second Siege of Yilish]] we in the villages could see the smoke of their cooking-fires from our roofs every evening, and we could count them, and we stopped counting.

They did not burn our trees. Let it be written that they did not burn our trees. They took our barley for their horses, and our young wine for their officers, and our sons' cloaks for their sentries in the cold nights, but they did not cut the olives. One of their captains told my neighbour that a man does not burn a field he means to tax. That is the whole of their philosophy, and it has the merit of being true.

Then the gates opened, and the two kings met within the walls, and old Naguf set his seal beside the seal of [[per:18782|Nalage]], and Kielish was no longer a city but a district. The criers called it the [[evt:19190|Conquest of Kielish]]. They read it out in our village square, standing on the well-head. My wife wept. My father, who is seventy, did not weep; he said he had now outlived his country, and went in to his supper.

What has changed? Less than you might think, and more. The price of grain in the market is about a [[cur:409|Kam]] and a quarter the measure. The olives will ripen when they ripen. The priests of [[mov:16|Ninzhe]] still teach in the porch of the shrine, and they still say, as they always said, that teachers are to be honoured but that no one owns the truth. We used to think this was a saying about philosophers. Now we hear it as a saying about kings.

What has changed is the end of the road. When we had a grievance, we walked to Kielish, a morning's walk, and stood in the court, and someone who knew our fathers heard us. Now the end of the road is in Nambe, which none of us has seen. A man may walk a morning to his own city; he cannot walk a morning to an idea.

So we ask the gods for small things, since great things are no longer ours to ask. To [[dei:104|Shilim]], lady of wisdom, we pray that the new masters be wise enough to be idle. To [[dei:106|Kumbe]] of the moon, who sees the terraces at night when the tax-men are asleep, we pray for a heavy harvest that they will not count. To Nizhinande of war we pray nothing at all. We have had enough of his attention.

Here ends the lament of the village, in the tenth year of Nalage, which we must learn to say.
