In the seventh year of King Shuesh'ayueve of Ana, about AY 6, a royal servant named Kha'eshi had a notice read out at the gate of Ane. Until then, he explained, the clay had only counted: a mark for a sheep, a mark for a jar of oil, a mark for a measure of barley. Now he had learned to join sign to sign "as a man joins word to word", so that a man's words could go where he did not go and stay after he was gone. He laid the first tablet before the god of wisdom, and the priest found the lamb's liver "without spot". He ended with a sentence every historian of Yüvinzhi might keep pinned above the desk:

> This is the making of writing in Ana. Kha'eshi made it. The clay has said so. The clay does not lie.

The tablet survives as a medieval copy from the palace archive at Ane (PAL·NP·01077, *The Clay That Speaks: A Notice to the People of Ana*). So does something three years older, a hymn the king himself spoke in AY 3, the earliest dated text we have (PAL·PO·00030, *Praise of the Signs, Spoken by Shuesh'ayueve*). "Before the signs, a word was a bird," it begins. "Now the word is a bird in a cage of clay." Both texts are proud, and both take sides. Kha'eshi insists that "no one say another made it". The king's poem warns, three times over, that no one should doubt his own part in the work. Five thousand years of written history thus open with a quarrel over credit. This book tells what happened in those five thousand years. This introduction explains how we know it, and the many ways in which the clay does lie.

## Eighteen thousand witnesses

Every chapter that follows rests on one archive: 18,398 surviving documents, running from Kha'eshi's notice to chronicles finished this year. Only 561 of them are literary works or modern essays. The rest are the everyday paperwork of power and trade: royal inscriptions, palace chronicles, peace treaties, letters, merchants' ledgers, coins, and in the last millennium newspapers and telegrams.

They are very unevenly spread. The first thousand years, AY 0 to 1000, have left us 1,474 documents, fewer than two a year, and 611 of them are inscriptions. The second millennium left 3,276, the third 3,376 and the fourth 3,235. The fifth left 7,037, more than the second and third together. The past grows louder as it comes closer, and a reader should keep that in mind. A quiet century in the early chapters may have been a busy one whose voices have not survived.

Behind the documents stands what this book calls *the record*, or the documentary consensus. It is the reconstruction of states, rulers, battles, prices and populations that scholars have built by testing each source against the others. It counts 2,021 states, 5,013 wars and 2,960 peace treaties. A population, a death toll or the price of a measure of grain in grams of silver in these pages comes from the record. When we quote a king, we quote him as a witness, which means as someone with something to hide.

## What survives, and why

Clay and stone survive; parchment and paper mostly do not. The commonest media in the archive are the clay tablet (2,075 documents), stone (1,775) and the clay copy of an older tablet (1,683). Then come newsprint (1,031), the printed book (893), the rock relief (718), and limestone and diorite steles (682 and 680). This is the first and largest bias in the archive. The early record is royal and monumental, because kings carved in stone, while the people who wrote on hide, wood and cloth have mostly gone back to the earth. The late record is commercial and popular. In the fifth millennium the commonest kinds of document are chronicles (1,167), newspapers (1,037), ledgers (752), inscriptions (625) and telegrams (445). In the middle three millennia the inscription led every count, with more than 1,200 in each.

The second filter was violence. The record knows 1,330 sacks of cities, and in 297 of them the archives burned. A scribe of Zhinontunu watched the soldiers of Tallin set fire to the house of tablets in AY 206. "The lists of the kings broke," the scribe wrote. "The oaths of the fathers broke. The counting of the barley broke." All that could be saved was "two baskets. Two baskets only" (PAL·CH·08467, *What the Scribe Saw When the House of Tablets Burned*). A soldier on the winning side, describing the burning of Poynge in AY 63, drew the lesson that victors always draw: "Their words are gone. Ours remain" (PAL·CH·08457, *What My Eyes Saw at Nin and Poynge*). He was right. Of the archive's works, 453 survive only as fragments quoted in later texts.

The Poyonga account of that same war is one of them (PAL·CH·00002). It is a single sentence, kept because a Navnga chronicler quoted it 239 years later: "by treachery, the host fell back to the walls." It is a stock excuse, used by the scribes of a dozen kingdoms whenever their side lost. Of a whole people's memory of its war, only the excuse remains.

The third filter is the spade, and here I must declare an interest, because it is my trade. The first piece came out of the ground by chance, in AY 4854: a song about an empty granary in Sanelu (PAL·PO·00095). Systematic digging began in AY 4880. The catalogue now records 316 excavations, almost all credited to two names, Nambili and Nanjil, and most of the material came out of the ground between AY 4880 and 4930. We dig where the famous cities were, which means where the kings were. A single season at Taulin in AY 4948 yielded 222 documents, and the next chapter leans on them heavily. A village that never had a palace usually has no archive at all. When this book says that the early states were ruled by kings who boasted, it is partly describing what we chose to dig.

## Kings who counted the dead

Of all the genres in the archive, the victory monument is the least trustworthy, and it is also one of the commonest. I have checked 2,712 of them against the record. Every one overstates the enemy's dead, and the middle value is an exaggeration of nearly six times. In AY 202 Dolulrinin of Chinontonu beat an army of Tallin at Puthnimdu and carved a stele claiming 19,900 enemy dead (PAL·IN·00007, *Victory stele of Dolulrinin I: Battle of Puthnimdu (AY 202)*). The record counts about 2,660.

Chronicles are subtler, and for that reason more dangerous. For most wars we have an account from each side, and they disagree with each other as well as with the record. Our method is the obvious one. We set the rival accounts side by side, then test both against the documents nobody wrote to be admired. Take the battle at Puthnimdu the following year, AY 203. The palace chronicle of Tallin says the king broke 4,815 enemy troops at the first charge, killing 1,239 for the loss of 333 (PAL·CH·00013, *Tallin account of the First Tallin–Chinontonu War*). An old spearman of the same army, setting down his memories twenty years later, says the Tallin host numbered 13,467, that it faced about two thousand men and that 604 of them died (PAL·CH·08458, *The Tablet of the Six Years of the Spear*). The record accepts the spearman's figures. He had no king to flatter, and he had every reason to remember. One modern study of the Talenonoy–Shue'ethie War found that each court's scribe had scaled the true figures by his own fixed ratio, battle after battle. "When a chronicle is wrong by the same proportion in every battle," it concluded, "the error is policy" (PAL·SP·00018, *Scaled by Rule*).

What keeps the historian honest is the dull document. Treaties had to be exact, because both parties kept a copy. The Treaty of Taulin of AY 87 records that Tallin paid 446 talents of silver to its enemy (PAL·TR·00002), a defeat its king's letters never admit. Prices are better still. The same Zhinontunu scribe who mourned the burned tablets noted that a measure of barley cost "a Yong and a quarter", about 9.5 grams of silver. The record's price for that decade is 9.4. A king of Talenonoy, writing from a war he was losing, priced the grain for his army at "one tyektsi and a little more", a little over 9.3 grams. The record gives 10.5. Men lie about the dead, but they seldom lie about the price of bread, and that is why this book reckons in grams of silver.

## Tongues, scripts and calendars

The record knows 126 languages in six families. Three are great families: [[lan:0|Tasemem]] with 41 languages, [[lan:1|Ngano]] with 42 and [[lan:4|Shengnan]] with 37. The Tasemem tongues put the verb first. The Shengnan tongues put it last and mark their nouns with a rich set of cases. The Ngano tongues, Khubunji among them, the language in which this book is written, use no cases at all. The other three families, [[lan:2|Telnu]], [[lan:3|Brikimo]] and [[lan:5|Haalood]], are each known only from a reconstructed ancestor and a single daughter. Across five millennia scholars have dated 1,844 sound changes. They matter to the historian because names drift. In the tablets the city of Taulin is often written Tallin, like the kingdom it ruled, and Chinontonu, Jinontonu and Zhinontunu are one kingdom and its city, written by different hands in different centuries. This book standardises the names and warns the reader where the sources disagree.

The record also knows 42 writing systems. Six are logosyllabic like Ana's first signs, seventeen are syllabaries, one is an abjad and eighteen are alphabets. And every people kept its own time. Scribes dated by the regnal year of their king, by the founding of a city or by a religious era. A treaty dated "in the 2nd year of Venululath" means AY 87, but only because generations of scholars have joined the king lists of rival courts into a single chronology. Modern scholarship converts every date to Archive Years, counted from AY 0, where the record begins. This book does the same.

## How to read this book

What follows is the work of scholars of [[pol:1638|Khishkhis]], each chapter covering two hundred and fifty years. We have tried to write the history we would want to read. We give the likely case before the extremes: what most people paid for bread, how most wars ended, how long most states lasted, and then the famines, the massacres and the miracles. Where chronicles disagree, we say so and say which one the record supports. We cite our sources by catalogue number, so that the reader can go and argue with them. And we have tried to look past the steles to the people the steles never mention, like the farmer's wife of Mana who recorded the hungry years of the AY 30s: "Three baskets. Last year, nine baskets." (PAL·DI·00023).

The story begins at AY 0, when the record first sees states. There were eight of them, with about 2.6 million people living under their kings, and a great many more who lived under none. Bronze was already being worked, canals were already being dug, and in a palace at Ane the scribes were sitting down with clay and reed. Within six years they would teach the clay to speak. It has been talking, and lying, and telling the truth in spite of itself, ever since.
