title: Its Fields and Its People
---
The herald read the queen's edict at the granary door every morning, and every morning there were more people at the door to hear it, and the words were the same. Ta'eli'an came to know them the way she knew the path over the pass above her village: by the places where they turned.

"Thus says Im'i, queen of Azhe," he began, and the crowd went quiet. Then, in the flat voice of a man reading from clay, he told them that "no man in Azhe shall sell grain above 4.97 [[cur:386|Khi'e]] the measure," and somebody at the back always laughed, because nobody in that line had seen grain at that price in years. The grain-sellers behind the temple had answered the edict by barring their doors. In their back rooms, people said, a measure still went for seven Khi'e of silver, weighed out on a hanging balance by a man who would not look at you. Then the herald lifted his voice for the last line, and the line lifted with it, and the bar came off the door: "The granaries of the palace shall be opened to the poor."

The poor were everyone now. The hill people had been coming down into the city since the hills went hungry. Then the queen's army had come back from [[evt:17335|Niuzafammas]] with a little more than half its men, and the host of [[pol:742|Wakha]] had come behind it, as it had [[evt:17331|the year before]], and sat down in the stubble outside the walls. For the [[evt:17336|second time]] in two years the gates of [[set:176|Azhe]] were shut.

Ta'eli'an stood in the line with a bowl held in both hands against her chest. She held it there because of the milk.

She had carried I'e Nim down out of the hills for six days. I'e Nim: Two Flower, for the day she was born. On the seventh day, under the last of the pines, where the road begins to drop toward the river, Ta'eli'an had stopped carrying her. She had built a little house of stones over her, the way it was done at home, and come on with the others, and her body had not understood. It went on making milk for a child who was not there. It woke her in the night with its aching. In the line, among strangers, it soaked through her dress in two dark rounds, and so she held the bowl high.

A woman came down the line from the door. She was grey, with a ring of keys at her belt and the look of someone who had been sent to find a thing and would not go back without it. She was not looking at faces. She was looking lower.

She stopped in front of Ta'eli'an and moved the bowl aside with one finger.

"How old?"

"Forty days. She was."

"How long since?"

Ta'eli'an counted. "Eleven days."

"And still coming." It was not a question. "No cough? Show me your hands." She turned them over, palms and backs. "Come with me."

"My grain—"

The woman looked at her as though she were simple. "You'll eat in the palace," she said. "Better than this."

* * *

Her name was Wa'alimin, and she had kept the queen's children since the queen's first, the boy who died. The new prince had been born that year. His first nurse, a woman of the city, had lost her milk in the hungry months, and there had been two since, and neither had suited him.

He suited Ta'eli'an at once, which she had not wanted. He was heavier than I'e Nim had ever been, with a full face and a frown, and he took her as though he had been waiting for her in particular. The milk let down so hard it hurt. She wept without any sound, and Wa'alimin, who was folding cloths by the lamp, said without turning round, "Not on his head. They'll say you salted him."

The room was on the river side of the palace, with a window too high to see out of and a lamp of olive oil that was never let go dark. They brought her barley bread with oil to dip it in, and lentils, twice a day: more than her family had eaten in a week since the summer. She ate it all. The milk wanted it. She thought about the line at the granary door and ate it anyway, and was ashamed, and was hungry again by evening.

On the fourth night the queen came.

Ta'eli'an did not know her at first. A woman in a dark wool cloak, smelling of smoke and horses, sat down on the floor inside the door, the way a tired person sits, and watched the child feed. Then Wa'alimin got up so fast her keys rang, and Ta'eli'an understood.

[[per:17128|Im'i]] was thirty-seven years old that year, and it was the ninth year of her reign over [[pol:743|Azhe]]. Her hair was pulled back hard and there was a cut healing on her knuckles.

"Don't stop," she said. "He'll shout the house down." She watched a while. "Where are you from?"

Ta'eli'an told her. The village, the hill road, the pass.

The queen nodded slowly, as if Ta'eli'an had named a debt. "I came back that way. After Niuzafammas." She rubbed the cut on her hand with her thumb. "There was a wind off the hills that day. You couldn't see the man beside you for dust. When it dropped, near half of them were down." She said it the way Ta'eli'an's father used to say how many sheep the snow had taken: as a number that would have to be lived with.

"The priests come every morning," the queen went on. "They are very patient. They say the wheel has turned, and [[dei:10|Masto]] has turned his face with it, and what ends will begin again. And in the meantime I should give the king of Wakha what he asks."

Ta'eli'an did not know whether she was supposed to speak. The prince had gone slack against her, still sucking now and then in his sleep. "What does he ask, lady?"

"The hills. The fields and the people in them." The queen looked at the wall as though she could see through it. "And silver. Twenty-one talents a year."

"I don't know what a talent is."

"Neither did I, at your age. It is about what a strong man can carry on his back from the gate to the river without stopping. So: twenty-one men, bent double, on the road to Wakha. Every year. For thirty-six years." She got up, using the doorframe. "Their king is six. His father came against me at Nisshudi and did not go home. His mother holds the kingdom for him. The boy will put his hand on their tablet and I will put mine on it beside his." She stopped in the doorway. "Is there anyone left up there? In your village?"

"The ones who couldn't walk."

The queen stood with her hand on the frame a long moment. Then she went out, and Ta'eli'an heard her on the stair, slowly, like a woman much older.

* * *

They swore the [[evt:17337|oath]] six days later in the court before the palace, under a white sky.

Nobody had said the nurses could not watch, so Wa'alimin took her up onto the roof of the women's court, and Ta'eli'an bound the prince against her in a shawl and stood at the parapet with the other women of the household, and the whole of it lay below them like a game laid out on a board.

The gate opened. Horsemen of Wakha came in first, slowly, looking up at the walls they had sat outside all that time. Then a chariot, and in it a boy holding the rail with both hands, and beside him a young woman in a red cloak who kept one hand flat on his back. In the kitchens they called him Va'alli'an, the second of that name. In the kitchens they also said that after Nisshudi his father's head had been brought to the queen. Ta'eli'an looked down at the queen, standing very straight among her priests, and could not tell whether it was true.

The priests of [[mov:1|Ya'kam]] had a fire going for [[dei:7|Yof]], and a ram, which died well. Then the scribe of Wakha, an old man, came forward with the tablet. It was silver. It took the white light and threw it up at the roof so that the women shaded their eyes.

He read it in the Wakha tongue first. Up in the hills they traded wool across the pass with Wakha people, and Ta'eli'an had their words for numbers and for land and for prices. So she heard it twice: once in pieces from the old man, and once whole from the interpreter after him, who had a carrying voice and no feeling in it at all.

She heard the land given: "its fields and its people." She heard "Boundary stones shall be set up," and thought, before she could stop herself, of a small house of stones under the last of the pines, and which side of a line it would stand on.

She heard the silver, twenty-one talents a year by the Khi'e standard, and the thirty-six years. She heard "If he withholds it, he has broken the oath," and saw one of the queen's priests glance at another, because the tablet said he, and the one who would or would not withhold it was standing in front of them in a woman's cloak. Nobody corrected it. The words were already cut in silver. She heard the merchants of Wakha promised the roads, and ten years of no man taking up arms, and then the gods called to watch: [[dei:6|Nili]] of the river, Yof, [[dei:8|Nish'il]] of the sky. Then the curse on whoever changed the words: "may the river refuse him water and the field refuse him grain."

The field has done that already, Ta'eli'an thought. Nobody had to change a word.

Then the hands. The young woman in red took the boy's hand and laid it flat on the silver, the way you put a child's hand on a strange dog to show him it will not bite. The queen laid hers beside it, the cut knuckles uppermost. For a breath the two hands lay there together, a woman's and a child's, and the old scribe waited, and the court was so still Ta'eli'an could hear the river.

The boy looked up. His eyes went along the roofs, as a child's will at any ceremony, looking for anything else, and found the women at the parapet, and found the bundle on Ta'eli'an's chest. He lifted his free hand a little, the way children greet other children across a street.

Ta'eli'an did not know what one did. She did nothing. The prince slept.

* * *

That night the prince woke hungry, and she sat with him under the lamp.

Thirty-six years. She tried to see it and could not, so she counted it, and because her own hands were full of him she counted on his: ten small fingers, and round again, and round again, and six more. He would be a man with a beard and sons of his own when the last of the silver went down the road. I'e Nim would have been a woman of the same age. They had been born in the same year, under the same hunger, no distance apart at all in the long count of things. One of them lay, as near as she could reckon, on the king's side of the line now, with the fields and the people. The other was the queen's.

She thought of the stone-setters going up the hill road with their ropes and their levers. She thought of the stone they would set, and of the little house on the far side of it, which no one would ever count.

The prince let go and slept, milk at the corner of his mouth, and she wiped it with her thumb. One day a scribe of the palace would write down this year in his annals. She could not read, but she did not need to, to know what he would leave out. She shifted the boy to her other arm. The milk came for him anyway, as it would have come for anyone, as it had come all those days on the hill road for no one at all.

**Author's note.** The queen's grain edict is PAL·DE·00156; the words read in the court are those of the silver treaty tablet, PAL·TR·01170, which does call the queen *he*. Wiedilash, the palace annalist, writing eight years later (PAL·CH·03198), says the queen made terms at the urging of the priests and that "Nothing of worth was lost." The record does not say who commanded at the walls; of the queen's son it keeps only his name and his years, from AY 2153 on. The famine in Yimla, which began that year, killed 55,687 people.
