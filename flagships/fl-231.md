This journal is printed in a [[lan:1|Ngano]] language. Our Khubunji descends, through [[lan:28|Khovave]], from [[lan:8|Kufalala]], one of the two primary branches of the family; the other is [[lan:9|Kunduge]]. I want to use that kinship to make a point of method. In the Ngano languages, daughters are sometimes more conservative than their parent, and a reconstruction that trusts the parent alone will go wrong.

## The family and its common laws

The record dates the separation of Kufalala to c. AY -1988 and of Kunduge to c. -1965. Within the standard list, from Kufalala descend [[lan:30|Khusadhava]] (from c. AY -995), [[lan:22|Kadhu]], Khovave, [[lan:18|Njile]], [[lan:24|Mbandu]] and [[lan:16|Khukhande]], all separating between c. AY -580 and -254, and through Khukhande, [[lan:36|Injèlu]]. From Kunduge descend [[lan:19|Kukuge]] (from c. AY -1489) and [[lan:23|Kuta]], and through Kuta, [[lan:38|Kuvu]]. Several have since died: Khukhande and Mbandu in the fourth millennium, Khusadhava, Kuta and Kadhu in the fifth.

Three dated laws precede the first separation and belong to the whole family. The first, intervocalic voicing (p t k s ʃ > b d g z ʒ between vowels), c. AY -2534, is visible in every cognate set where it could be. 'Mother', *tika, has g in Kufalala shige, Kunduge tsigi, Khukhande shige and Njile shige, while Kukuge chikh shows the same consonant altered again at the end of the word. 'Water', *mbengašo, shows the voiced ʒ, written zh, everywhere: Kufalala mbengazhe, Kunduge mvingazh, Khukhande mbengazhu, Njile mvingazhu, Kukuge mbingazhu. The second law, the merger of o into u, c. AY -2212, explains the final -u of Khukhande and Kukuge in the same word (Njile lowered it to o and later raised it again); Kufalala and Kunduge have since altered or lost the final vowel. The third, umlaut (a u > e y before a consonant and i or j), c. AY -2134, finds no clear witness in these five words, and I record the absence rather than force one.

## The Kufalala line

The remaining laws in the dated series all postdate the first separation and belong to the Kufalala line: the merger of y into i (c. AY -1853), open-syllable lengthening (c. -1794) and the loss of length (c. -1774), a second intervocalic voicing that now included f (c. -1699), and palatalization of t and d before front vowels (c. -1605). Lengthening and its loss are dated only twenty years apart, which should make us wonder whether our dates are more precise than the evidence allows.

Palatalization gives the cleanest test. Kufalala shige reflects *t before *i, turned to an affricate and later to sh. Khukhande and Njile, which separated long after AY -1605, inherit the same sh. The Kunduge line, untouched by this law, shows different and evidently independent results: ts in Kunduge tsigi, ch in Kukuge chikh. The correspondences fall exactly along the genealogical line, as a law of the Kufalala branch should.

## The case of 'king'

Now the point of my title. 'King' is *tamba. Kunduge tamvi and Kukuge tambe keep the t. Khukhande khambe and Njile kamve have a velar. And Kufalala itself, the parent of both, has ambe, with no initial consonant at all. With Kufalala as our only witness, we might suppose the consonant simply lost. The daughters tell a fuller story: the record's longer series of dated changes places a shift of t to k in the Kufalala line about AY -652, before Khukhande and Njile separated, and a later weakening of that k, and its eventual loss, in Kufalala, and independently, not until AY 4008, in Khovave. The daughters preserve a stage the mother has since passed through. Khukhande, extinct since the fourth millennium, is in this respect more archaic than its living parent.

'Silver', *aguna, offers a smaller warning in the other direction. Khukhande and Njile agree in agne, against Kufalala agone, Kunduge aguni and Kukuge agu. Shared syncope would be easy to read as a mark of a common sub-branch, but the record dates the two losses of the vowel separately; the agreement is parallel, not inherited.

## For our own language

For readers of Khubunji the consequence is practical. It is tempting to derive our words directly from Kufalala, the oldest ancestor in our line short of the proto-language. The case of 'king' shows why that is unsafe. The intermediate languages, Khovave above all, may preserve stages that Kufalala has lost, and any history of our own vocabulary should be written through them, not over their heads.
