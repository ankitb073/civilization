title: Scaled by Rule: Talenatbin, Mue'o and the Arithmetic of the Talenonoy–Shue'ethie War

The [[evt:1110|Talenonoy–Shue'ethie War]] (AY 389–398) is one of the rare wars for which both chronicles were written almost at once. Mue'o finished his tablet in AY 399, a year after the peace, and Talenatbin his in AY 402. Neither scribe can plead distance in time, so their departures from the record are choices. The defender's account, usually the one that omits, is here the more complete, and its numbers are no more honest for that.

## The tablets

The Talenonoy account (PAL·CH·00113, Nunmula, excavated at Taalinnuy in AY 4908) is complete apart from minor losses. The Shue'ethie account (PAL·CH·00114, Ie'im, excavated at Ishwyithie in AY 4882) is fire-damaged at the edges. The damage falls exactly where the cause of the war should stand: "Imtomtibon of Talenonoyi, swollen with pride, [...] of Eshue'ethie." Translators should expect the name-forms to drift. Talenatbin writes Lueshhanyeme for the Shue'ethie queen and Wiradutthe for the ford. Mue'o writes Imtomtibon for Himttumtibun and Wizatotibe for Viradhuthpi.

## Field by field

*Who began it.* In the record, [[pol:19|Talenonoy]] attacked to seize territory. Mue'o agrees, lacuna notwithstanding. Talenatbin reverses it: "Lueshhanyeme of Eshueheshye, swollen with pride, coveted the lands of Talenonoy. So the king went out to punish him." The phrase "swollen with pride" appears in both tablets, aimed in opposite directions. It was a stock phrase, not evidence.

*The battles.* The record has six engagements in [[reg:15|Baan]]: five at the ford of Viradhuthpi and the [[evt:1158|Battle of Khuele (AY 398)]]. Each side won three. Mue'o lists all six with the right victors, though he attaches an excuse to each defeat. Talenatbin lists four. He keeps his three victories and admits the [[evt:1137|Battle of Viradhuthpi (AY 393)]], also with an excuse attached. The [[evt:1146|Battle of Viradhuthpi (AY 396)]] and Khuele, the last battle of the war, vanish.

*The dead.* The record gives Talenonoy 5,100 dead and [[pol:0|Shue'ethie]] 6,706: a costly, fairly even war. Talenatbin's figures are 1,692 and 18,068. Mue'o's are 9,139 and 2,270. Battle by battle, the distortions are startlingly regular. Talenatbin's own dead are about 60 per cent of the record's in every engagement he admits, and the enemy's about three times. Mue'o's own dead are about one third of the record's in all six, and the enemy's about 1.8 times. At the [[evt:1120|Battle of Viradhuthpi (AY 390)]], the record counts 731 Talenonoy and 1,974 Shue'ethie dead. Talenatbin gives 438 and 6,002, and Mue'o 1,310 and 668. Each scribe seems to have started from figures close to the record's and scaled them by his own rule.

*Commanders.* On the one death that mattered, both agree with the record. Luesh'anieme fell at the [[evt:1142|Battle of Viradhuthpi (AY 395)]]. Talenatbin has her head brought to the king. Mue'o has "There fell Luesh'anieme, whose name shall be remembered." A queen's death could not be hidden by a scribe whose dating formula had to switch to her successor's first year. Neither tablet mentions the [[evt:1143|Siege of Ishwyithie (AY 395)]], although Mue'o's tablet was later recovered from that city.

*Outcome and land.* Talenonoy won. The [[evt:1159|Treaty of Khuele (AY 398)]] gave it six districts, Viradhuthpi among them, 28,975 kg of silver and a ten-year truce. Talenatbin has the enemy suing for peace and fourteen districts "returned to Talenonoy". Mue'o calls the war a stalemate settled by his king's grace: "The border was settled." He admits the loss of a few villages at most. Among them, on the record's reckoning, was the ford that five battles had been fought to hold.

## Two courts, two rules

The patterns of omission follow the patrons. Talenatbin wrote for Himttumtibun the Old, who had commanded at three of the six battles himself. His defeat at Khuele disappears, and his defeat in AY 393 survives only with the blame placed anywhere but on the king. Mue'o wrote for Tlamfen Lieze, a new king who had signed away territory and silver. That court could not deny a war everyone remembered, but it could deny that the army had been beaten. So every defeat acquires an excuse, and the cession shrinks to a settled border.

Distance in time explains nothing here. When a chronicle is wrong by the same proportion in every battle, the error is policy.
