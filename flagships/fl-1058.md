title: Payable in Navnga
---
Tin keeps. That was the first thing my father taught me about it, on the road out of Shivanga when I was too small to carry anything but the water skin, and the second was never to sell it to a man in a hurry. Copper goes green. Cloth feeds the moth, and grain feeds the weevil and the rat. But a bar of tin will lie in its hide in the corner of a storeroom for twenty years and come out as bright as the day it was poured, and wait, and never once ask you for anything. In my trade the man in a hurry is always the other one.

On the day the king's brother stood up before the town of [[set:56|Vuvo]] and said that he was king, everyone was in a hurry but me.

His men came for my tin at midday. There were three of them, young, in new cloaks, and the one who did the talking wanted all of it carried up the hill by evening, and wanted to know whether a man of Yida meant to argue with the king of [[pol:8|Navnga]] in the king's own town. I said I would be glad to sell the king of Navnga anything he liked, but that as far as I knew the king of Navnga lived down on the coast by the river mouth, and had been king there for nineteen years. The young man went red to the ears. I said the new king could come and ask me himself, to my face, as one man asks another, because that is how it is done where I come from; and also because not one of them had brought so much as a ring of silver, and a claim is not a thing you can load on a donkey.

They went away. The lane was full of shouting all afternoon. My wife's mother had herself carried up onto the roof, which took two of us, and sat there under a blanket shouting the old name. She had been six years old when Navnga took the town, and she told anyone below who would listen that she had waited seventy-one years to shout it from a roof. "[[pol:37|Vuvo]]! Vuvo!" she cried, in a voice like a goose's, and the lane cried it back. She did not seem to mind that the man she was shouting for was a son of the Navnga kings, who prayed to their gods and spoke the town's tongue like a man crossing a river on stones. When my wife asked her about it she said, "A king in Vuvo is a king in Vuvo," and went back to shouting.

Below her the spearmen of the town went up and down the lane in knots, singing. They had been his brother's spearmen until that morning, and they knew only one song for a king, which was his brother's: the hymn of the silver of Kiya, which had come up from the coast three years before, with [[dei:21|Duminu]] in it, and the white bull, and the battles. Each time they reached the place for the king's name, some of them sang one brother and some the other and some only shouted. Then they all came in together, gladly, on the part with no name in it.

"The wheel turns, the wheel turns; what was owed is paid."

I am a man of Shivanga. I have stood against Navnga three times: at [[evt:820|Virathkanu]]; at [[evt:825|Shivanga]] the year after, where their king fell; and at Shivanga again two years later, where his son lost too, and then made [[evt:840|the peace]] by whose terms I have traded in Navnga's towns ever since. My wife says I am the only man she knows who was paid twice for the same day's work, and that I should not say so in the market. I don't. I say it in my own yard.

* * *

He came after the lamps were lit, with two men, and left them at the gate.

He was shorter than I had expected and younger than I had been told, though I had been told twenty-one and he was twenty-one. It is only that twenty-one looks younger every year I live. He wore a plain cloak of brown wool over a good shirt, and a bronze knife, and the wrong sandals for our lanes, and he looked around my yard, at the donkeys and the hides and the storeroom door, the way a man looks at a field he has been told is his. Then he sat down on my tin. He did not ask; it was the right height, so he sat on it. That was how I knew he had been raised among cattle and not among merchants. A merchant never sits on the goods.

"My man says you want silver," said [[per:1093|Pufdun]].

"I want silver from everybody, lord."

"I have cattle."

"I'm sure they are very fine cattle." I sat down across from him on an upturned basket. "But I'm a man of Yida, and my road home goes through your brother's country, and by the next moon there will be spearmen of two kings on it. What would I do with forty head of Navnga cattle on that road?"

"Eat well," he said, and nearly smiled, and then did not. He picked at the knot of the hide he was sitting on. "They tell me in the town you're from Shivanga."

"Half the men in Yida are from somewhere near Shivanga, lord, after the second cup."

"They tell me you were there. Both times."

"They talk a great deal in this town."

"Yes," he said. "That's why I came myself." He looked at me. "In my brother's house nobody says that name. My mother doesn't say it. The priests of the [[mov:3|Ryethki]] gods say it only to the gods. When I come into a room the old spearmen stop talking. I have been hearing nothing about Shivanga my whole life. I thought I would ask a man who had no reason to be kind."

I have been asked about Shivanga before, by drunk men mostly, and by boys who want to know what it is like to kill a man. I had never been asked by anyone who needed the answer. I found I had to look at the donkeys for a while.

"What do you want to know?"

"What did he look like?"

I thought he meant his brother. He saw it.

"No," he said. "My father."

* * *

"I never saw his face," I said. "I'll tell you that first, so you don't think I'm selling you anything. I was nineteen, in the third rank, with the queen in the middle of us, and the field between your people and ours was wide enough to graze a herd on.

"But I saw him walk. Before it began he walked along the front of your people, the whole length of it, slowly. He stopped. He put his hand on a man's shoulder here and there. He walked the way a man walks his own fields in the evening when the barley is up, looking at everything as if it were already his. And as he passed each company it shouted, so that the shout came along the line toward us like wind coming through standing grain. That is what I saw of him. Then we went into each other, and after a while the standard went down."

"You saw it go down."

"Everybody saw it go down. That is what a standard is for."

"Who—"

"I don't know. Nobody knows. By the time we got home there were a great many men in Yida who had done it, and some of them had not been at Shivanga." I leaned toward him. "But I'll tell you one thing nobody in your brother's house will have told you, because it is the reason the day went so badly for you. When the standard went down, your people didn't run. They went in after him. That whole end of the line turned and went in toward the place where he had fallen, the way men go into a burning house for a child. They wanted him back. That is how a field is lost, lord. Not by cowards. By men who want something back."

He was quiet for a long time. Down the lane the spearmen had got to the white bull again.

"And the second time?"

"The [[evt:839|second time]] it was Magunnufmalin who led us, not the queen. But she came for the peace, and I was one of the men on her tent." I had not thought about the tent in years, and now I could smell it: hot hide, and dust, and the dung of the baggage oxen. "She went in. Your brother went in. They were a long time. When he came out he walked perhaps ten paces, in front of everyone, and sat down on the ground, the way a boy sits who has walked too far. There was clay on his right hand, across the palm, where he had put it to the tablet, and he held the hand open on his knee and didn't wipe it. And his men came. Nobody gave an order. The ones who had been with him that day came and stood round him, close, with their backs to him and their faces out, so that none of us should see him. A wall of them. He sat inside it with his hand open. I had never seen men do that, and I have not seen it since."

The boy was looking at his own hands.

"He was twenty-one," I said. "So was I."

For a long while he said nothing at all. Then he said, "He has been eighteen years older than me my whole life." He turned his right hand over and looked into the palm of it, as if there might be something written there. "I never once saw him twenty-one."

* * *

When he stood up he was a king again. I watched it come back over him like a cloak.

"So," he said. "The tin."

"So. The tin."

"Silver," he said. "Good weight. Payable in Navnga, when I sit there."

I have sold tin to his brother's smiths since the peace, and some of it, I have no doubt, went down to Nimiki in their spearheads. I know what the king of Navnga keeps in his storehouses, and how many spearmen eat his bread; so does every trader on the coast road. And I know what a field looks like from the side that is winning, which is a thing not many men in Navnga can say. I am not a fool. It was the worst price anyone had offered me in all my years on the road.

I took it.

"We'll swear it," he said, "by Duminu—" and stopped, because Duminu is not my god, and I waited, because I wanted to see what he would do. He thought about it. "By [[dei:22|Vinba]], then. She's yours too, they tell me."

"She's everybody's. The queen set up a stone to her at Shivanga, after the second time."

"Then she will know who owes what," he said, and we took hands on it. His palm was hot, and hard across the middle from the spear-shaft. I did not have it pressed into clay. Clay is for debts you mean to collect.

At the gate he turned. "You never asked me why."

"Why what, lord?"

"Why I've done it. Everyone asks."

"I'm a merchant. I don't ask a man why he wants tin." Then, because he had paid me so badly, I gave him something for nothing. "If you want advice, which is free: keep away from Shivanga. That field has had two of your house already."

He laughed at that, properly, for the first time, a young man's laugh, too loud for the lane. "Then it owes us one," he said, the way they all talk down there, as if the world were a wheel and a man had only to wait at the bottom of it.

* * *

My wife came out and stood beside me in the gate. On the roof her mother had fallen asleep under the blanket with her mouth open, hoarse with the old name.

"Will he pay?" my wife said.

"In Navnga," I said.

She is a Vuvo woman. She knows how far it is to the coast. She did not say anything else.

By then he was going off down the lane between his two men with one bar of my tin in his hand, because he had wanted to carry something himself, and the spearmen at the corner saw him and began his brother's song again, and he went through the middle of them slowly. He stopped. He put his hand on a man's shoulder here and there. He looked at everything as if it were already his. I had seen that walk once before, from a long way off, and I watched it out of sight the same way, from a long way off, knowing more than he did.
