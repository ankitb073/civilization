title: The Tablets Were Kept
---
The levy tablets said two thousand six hundred and sixty-seven. Sho'a Luesh'a had pressed the total herself in the spring, at the long table in the house of tablets, adding the districts one under another while old Ba'emovi read them out and grumbled about the hands of provincial scribes. Now the tablets sat in a basket at her knee in the shade of the great gate of [[set:74|Tlayiyan]], the men were coming back from [[evt:13691|Tlala'azhash]], and her work was to mark them.

A small wedge beside a name for a man who walked in. Nothing for a man who did not. It was the plainest work the palace had. A child could have done it, except that a child would have cried.

They came all day in knots of five and ten, grey with the road, carrying their spears the way men carry poles to the well. Some had their heads bound in cloth. Some were carried. The chariots came in the late afternoon, fewer than had gone out, the horses walking with their heads down, and in the third of them stood [[per:13621|Yalni Shimen]], a heavy man of fifty-seven who had commanded on the field himself and wore its dust in every fold of his face. He did not look at the wall where the women stood. He looked up at the gate, at the great beam across the top of it, as if he were measuring it for something. Then the chariot was through and gone up the street to the palace, and the women began to sing.

Sho'a knew the song. Everyone her age knew it. The mothers had made it at the threshing floors the summer she was eight, when the levy was first called against [[pol:589|Tlo'a]] and nobody in the city had ever seen a man of Tlo'a; the name was only a direction the road went in. She had stood at her mother's skirt and learned it the way children learn anything sung over their heads. "[[dei:4|Lashla'a]], count them going. Count them coming back." She had liked best the verse near the end, about the woman who had no son to give. And she had hated, without knowing why, the line that made the grown women put their hands over their mouths: "Let the count be the same."

She kept her eyes on the clay. She marked.

A woman came and crouched by the basket: Yoni Ash, who sold reed mats at the foot of the potters' steps. She had a mat-maker's hands, split at the thumbs.

"Ma'she Ada," she said. "Son of Yoni Ash. The reed-cutters' row."

Sho'a found the district, found the row, found the name. There was no wedge beside it.

"Not yet," she said.

"Not yet," Yoni Ash said, as though she had been handed something to carry, and went back to the wall.

By the second evening the tally had stopped moving. Sho'a did the reckoning twice on a scrap of wet clay, and twice it came to one thousand and thirty-eight who had not come back through the gate. She smoothed the scrap flat with her palm and carried the number up to the palace in her head, because she did not want to carry it in her hands. Behind the last of the stragglers, the runners said, the army of Tlo'a was still coming.

* * *

Ba'emovi had kept the house of tablets for longer than she had been alive. He was a small man who had shrunk further with the years, so that his apron hung on him like a mat on a peg, and he was waiting for her on the step with a lamp.

"Two days," he said. "One, if they don't stop to eat."

Inside, the shelves ran back into the dark, and the place smelled as it always smelled, of dust and old matting and the faint sourness of clay. The city's business was in there, back past the oldest king anyone could name: barley receipts and boundary lists and the letters of the dead, and here and there a song that someone, once in a long while, had thought worth the clay.

"What do we do?"

"What the old ones did." He set the lamp in its niche. "Fire is the friend of clay, child. Fire only bakes it harder. It's water that kills a tablet. Water, and feet, and men looking for something they can sell."

So they spent the night carrying. Baskets from the outer room to the inner, stacked to the beams, covered with mats, the oldest at the bottom. Her arms shook. Near dawn, lifting a basket, she found the song of the fifth burning in her hands, and stopped.

She knew it by its weight and the chip at its corner. She had copied it as a girl, when Ba'emovi was teaching her the old signs, a sign for every word, in the old tongue of the city, which scribes still learned and the city no longer spoke. It was nearly four hundred years old. Those who came back had sung it after the fifth time the city burned, and someone had written it down. The old tongue even spelled the city's name differently, shorter, as if the city had grown since. She read the end aloud, slowly, the way he had taught her.

"Let no one sing it a sixth time. Let no one sing it again."

Ba'emovi said nothing.

"There's no tablet of the sixth," she said. "Or the seventh. I looked, when I was copying. I thought I'd missed them."

"You didn't miss them."

"So they listened. It burned, and they didn't sing."

"I think they were tired." He took the tablet from her and set it back on its shelf in the outer room, among the ones they were leaving. "That one's fired hard. It'll keep." Then, because he was a pious old man and could not help it: "All things turn on the wheel. What ends will begin again. That's the teaching."

"It's supposed to be a comfort."

"It is a comfort. It's also true. The priests don't mind if a thing is both."

* * *

They came at dusk on the second day.

From the roof of the house of tablets she watched them pile brush against the gate, and saw the first smoke go up thin and white and then thicken. The beam the king had looked at took a long time to catch. When it caught, it burned with a sound she would not have believed of wood, a roar and under the roar a ticking, like a pot cooling. Arrows came over the wall in flights. They fell in the streets with a noise like hail on a roof, and some skidded on the beaten earth and lay where they stopped, and nobody went out to pick them up.

The gate fell in towards morning. After that there was a long time of shouting.

She was in the inner room by then, among the baskets, with Ba'emovi and the dyer's wife from across the lane and the dyer's two girls, who had come in at midnight because the house of tablets had no window on the street. They did not light the lamp. The younger girl slept, which seemed to Sho'a the bravest thing she had ever seen anyone do.

Around midday there were feet in the outer room, and voices in a tongue like gravel shaken in a pot. Through the gap in the hanging mat she saw two men of Tlo'a with a torch, young, dusty, with the look of men who had not slept either. One took a tablet off the shelf, turned it over, and scratched at it with his thumbnail. He said something, and the other laughed. Then he dropped it. It did not break.

They took the bronze lamp from its niche, and the oil jar, and the copper bowl Ba'emovi washed his hands in. They kicked over a basket to see if anything was hidden under the clay, and there was only more clay, and they went out.

That was all. She would turn it over for the rest of her life: that the whole house was kept, every tablet in it, because to a man of Tlo'a it was a room full of dirt.

In the afternoon the fire came over the roofs from the weavers' street and found the beams above the outer room. Fire is the friend of clay, but a burning beam coming down on a shelf is nobody's friend, and Ba'emovi was too old for ladders. Sho'a and the dyer's wife carried the water jars up and threw water on the beams until the smoke went from black to white, and the water ran down through the roof-reeds, black with soot, onto the shelves below.

* * *

On the third day the men of Tlo'a went back down the road they had come by. They took the barley out of the royal pits on their own carts. They took bronze, and oil, and cloth, and some of the people; nobody ever agreed how many, because nobody counted. Children went up and down the streets gathering arrowheads, since bronze was bronze whoever had shot it, and a handful would buy a meal. They did not find them all.

Sho'a went back into the outer room. The tablets on the shelves were whole, but the sooty water had come down across them, and the song of the fifth burning had taken the worst of it. A black stain lay over its first line like the shadow of a hand. Where the line had said "city of the barley-land", two small words were gone under the stain. She rubbed at it with a wet thumb. It did not come off. She could still read the end.

That evening the king sent for her.

He sat on a stool in a room that smelled of smoke, as every room in the city did, with a lamp someone had found for him. He looked older than he had in the chariot.

"How many?" he said.

"At Tlala'azhash, lord, one thousand and thirty-eight."

"And here?"

"Nobody has counted here."

He nodded, as if that were the answer he had wanted, or the one he had expected, which with kings was not always different. "Five years ago at [[evt:13637|Khathtanol]] the moon kept faith with us. Mi'ilie'an came home singing. I thought the gods had settled it." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Prosperity is their favour, the priests say. Look out of the door and tell me what they think of us. When I came in at the gate I looked at the beam and thought, it will hold. I don't know why I thought so. Get your clay."

She wet the clay and waited.

"From this day the [[mov:0|La'im]] is the faith of [[pol:555|Tlayiyan]]," the king said, and she pressed the signs. "Its priests shall be fed from the royal granaries."

She stopped with the stylus lifted. The royal granaries were empty pits with their lids thrown back; she had walked past them that morning.

"They will be filled," he said. "Write it."

She wrote it.

"The rites of the Tlenzo may continue in private."

She looked up at that. The Tlenzo were the gods of the men who had burned the gate.

"There are people in this city who pray to them," [[per:13621|Yalni Shimen]] said. "Neighbours of yours, I expect. Let them pray behind a door. I have seen enough doors burn. Put the year."

She put the year, the eleventh of his reign. When the edict was baked it would go into the house of tablets, onto a shelf, and it would keep. Whoever came looking for this year would find a king giving the gods their due. She knew how the palace wrote its years. She had been learning it since she was twenty.

* * *

The river was low at the end of the dry months, a brown thread between flats of cracked mud. The women went down at dusk, as the old songs said the women of the city had always gone down after a burning, to wash the ash out of their hair and say the names into the water for [[dei:3|Niele]] to carry away.

Sho'a went with them. She had brought a fresh tablet without thinking, the way she brought one everywhere, and it sat damp in her lap as she knelt on the bank.

Yoni Ash was there. Her son had never been marked. She stood in the water to her shins with her hair down and her split hands at her sides, and when the names were done she began to sing: not the levy song but the older one, the song of the burned city, in the new tongue, to a tune that everyone somehow knew without having learned it. "Your doors are ash, your doors are ash." Where the old tablet said the fifth time, she sang the eighth, and the women took it up.

"Are you writing it, palace?" Yoni Ash said, when she saw the clay.

Sho'a looked down at its smooth face. She thought of the old song on its shelf with the black stain across its first line, asking that no one sing it again. She thought of the edict drying in the palace, and of how the palace wrote its years. She thought of a threshing floor, and a skirt to hold, and the last verse coming round.

"Mother of no one, what do you give?"

She laid the tablet in the shallows, face up, and felt the river begin at once on its edges.

"I give my voice," she said. "I sing with you."

Then she waded out beside Yoni Ash and sang it the eighth time with the others, and in the morning the clay was mud, and the river had it.

**Author's note.** The city kept almost nothing of the [[evt:13692|Eighth Sack of Tliyiya]]. What survives is one line in the Annals of Tsukha (PAL·AN·00283), a diviner's omen, "fire will consume the gate of the city" (PAL·OM·00157), and the excavators' "burnt timbers and scattered arrowheads" (PAL·EX·00106). The edict and the two songs are quoted from PAL·DE·00131, PAL·PO·00103 and PAL·PO·00127. Eight years later Sho'a Luesh'a wrote the palace account of the [[evt:13620|Holy War of Tsukha against Tlayiyan]] (PAL·CH·02496). It gives 617 lost at Zhaalakhazhash, where the record has 1,038, and never mentions the sack.
