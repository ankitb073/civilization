To the most serene and exalted [[per:33579|Zhievialanath]], by the grace of the One Power Empress of [[pol:1328|Pienpedhzhe]], mother of her peoples, defender of the faith of [[mov:58|Vieshethmekh]]: her humble handmaid [[per:33955|Khakienuth]], who holds a small manor of the crown in the river country, sends greeting in the Light, and kisses the hem of her robe.

Most gracious Lady, I would not presume to lay a matter of ploughs and dung before the throne, were it not that the teachers of our faith bid us to relieve suffering wherever we find it, and there is no suffering so common in this realm as the hunger of the late winter. It is written that the righteous servant does not bury the talent entrusted to her, but puts it to use for her lord. I have been entrusted with a small talent, and I would not be found to have buried it.

Your Majesty knows the custom of our fathers: a field is sown one year and left fallow the next, that the earth may rest. Thus half the land of every manor lies idle in any year, and the people eat from the other half.

This year I set my own demesne in a different order. I divided the arable not into two parts but into three. In the first part I sowed wheat. In the second I sowed beans and peas. The third I left fallow, and grazed the beasts upon it, so that their dung fell where it was needed. In the year to come each part will move on one place: the wheat-field to beans, the bean-field to fallow, the fallow to wheat. So the round will go, year upon year, like the hours of prayer.

I will tell Your Majesty plainly what I have seen, and nothing more. Two parts of every three now bear a crop where before only one part of two bore. The beans did not rob the soil, as my steward feared, and the beasts on the fallow came through the summer fat. My tenants, who mocked me in the spring, now beg me to show them the order of the fields. This harvest my granary was full before the feast of the dead, and I sold the surplus at the market of [[set:164|Pienpedhzhe]] for good [[cur:747|Rielu]], of which the due portion has been paid to the crown's steward and the tithe to the church.

I must be honest also about where the thought came from, lest I be thought proud. I did not receive it in a vision. An old ploughman on my manor, who had worked the land since before I was born, told me that his grandfather's people used to say the beans "put back what the wheat takes out," and that wheat sown after beans stands thicker. I only wrote down the order and kept to it.

There are some, Most gracious Lady, who whisper that resting the earth by turns is a remnant of the old rites, a gift to the earth-gods of the Jinru whom the realm put away at the [[evt:34452|Persecution of the Jinru in Pienpedhzhe]]. I beg Your Majesty not to believe them. I have offered no gift to any shadow. Our faith teaches that the old gods are but servants or shadows of the One Power, and that every good thing proceeds from that Power alone. If the beans feed the wheat, it is because the One Power ordered the beans and the wheat so, from the creation, and has waited until now for us to notice. To use that order is not idolatry. It is gratitude.

Your Majesty's house has, by the [[evt:34423|Conquest of Vieneynga]] and the victory at [[evt:34421|Battle of Nudhuruigirimu]], added broad lands to the realm, and broad lands mean many mouths. I humbly propose that Your Majesty's stewards be commanded to try the three fields on the crown manors for three years, and to report honestly what they find. If I am wrong, nothing is lost but three years of beans. If I am right, there will be bread in the late winter for thousands who now have none.

I commend myself, my manor and my tenants to Your Majesty's protection, and Your Majesty to the mercy of the One Power, until the day of judgement when all accounts are opened.

Written at my manor, in the year 1407 of the Vieshethmekh, by my own hand.

Your Majesty's most humble and obedient handmaid,
Khakienuth
