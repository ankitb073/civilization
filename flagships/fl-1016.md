title: Forty-One Notches
---
From the wall of [[set:22|Val'a]], at the hour when the lamps were lit in the town behind her, Milamamshi counted fires.

There were more of them each night, and they were in the wrong places. One burned in the lambing hollow under the split rock, where the ground kept the day's warmth and the ewes went down to drop their young. One burned at the spring; she knew it by the way its smoke lay along the gully toward the sea, the way the smoke of her own fire had lain every evening of her married life. One burned on the low hump of earth at the top of the slope, where she had buried Mukhonwi six winters ago with his good knife and a jar of curds.

"You're doing it again," said Ilsha'ash.

"Doing what?"

"Naming them."

He sat with his back to the parapet so that he would not have to look out. His left forearm was bound in linen that she changed every morning and that came away a little cleaner each time. He had given [[dei:11|Nizhashta]] a lock of his hair before the battle, and the goddess had taken a strip of his arm as well, which his mother thought greedy of her and did not say. He was nineteen. Before the battle he had looked older.

Behind them the wind came off the harbour across the flat roofs, smelling of weed and pitch and gutted fish. In front of them, from the hills, came a different smell: fat dripping into a fire. Somebody's sheep.

"That one is at the spring," she said.

"Mother."

"I'm only saying where it is."

The men of [[pol:30|Yi'utl]] had come up out of the steppe more than ten thousand strong. Inal had taken a little over two thousand two hundred out through the gate to meet them on the long slope above the ford, Ilsha'ash among them with his father's spear, and Inal had brought them all back but two hundred and twenty. The town had been saying that number to itself ever since, worrying at it like a burr in a fleece. Then the gate had been shut and barred, and the Yi'utl had come down and sat on the hills, and the hills were hers.

"We didn't run," Ilsha'ash said, to the stones. "They say in the street that we ran. Inal walked us back. He walked at the rear himself, the whole way to the gate, and he didn't hurry."

"I know. I saw."

"He said it was the wrong ground. Too open. He said no one could hold a slope like that against so many."

It was good ground, she thought. It was the best ground between the hills and the sea; the grass came early there and stayed late. She did not say it. She took the tally stick out of her belt instead, the length of olive wood she had notched at the last lambing, and ran her thumb down it the way she did every night. Forty-one. Ewes, mostly. The old ram. Two wethers she had meant to trade at the quay for grain.

* * *

They slept in the house of Tlampin Tli near the quay, because Tlampin Tli's dead husband had been Mukhonwi's cousin, and because there was nowhere else. The house was narrow and smelled of oil. Tlampin Tli sold it, and her jars stood along one wall to the height of a man's shoulder, stoppered with clay, and every morning she counted them with her lips moving. Milamamshi understood that.

The hearth was at the back, under the smoke-hole. It belonged to [[dei:7|Yub]], as every hearth did, and each morning Milamamshi fed her before anyone ate, a pinch of grain and a drop of oil, the way her mother had done it on the steppe. The first morning Tlampin Tli watched and said nothing. The second morning she said, "We don't give her oil in this house. Grain is enough. She's a town goddess here."

"She's the same goddess."

"She's used to less," said Tlampin Tli; and then, because she was not unkind, only frightened, "Give her the oil if you like. It's mine anyway."

The grain went up every morning. Tlampin Tli came back from the granary yard with a smaller bag for the same scrap of silver, and stood a while in the doorway before she came in, doing sums.

Her daughter, Yi Mish, who was seven, attached herself to the tally stick. She wanted to know which notch was which sheep.

"This one is the ram. He's old and he bites."

"Which is the one with the black face?"

"This one," Milamamshi said, and touched it, and was surprised that the child had been listening when she told about the black-faced ewe, who jumped walls.

"Where are they now?"

From the doorway, where he spent his days watching the street for news, Ilsha'ash said, "In the fires."

Tlampin Tli cuffed him lightly across the back of the head, as she would have cuffed a son of her own, and he took it and went out.

In the street they said the king would go out again. They said he would not. [[per:2476|Yubshim'am]] had gone out himself two years before, onto the steppe beyond Milamamshi's hills, and had met I'pil Tlue'e of Yi'utl there, a man of his own age to the year, and had lost and come home. They said that Inal had now lost to them twice, three years ago at the [[evt:2179|Battle of Namosash]] and again under the walls, and that a general who lost twice was either unlucky or honest, and nobody could agree which was worse. They said the Yi'utl wanted silver. They said they wanted the town. At the well the women said that prosperity was the favour of the gods, and plainly the gods had withdrawn it from somebody, and when they said it they looked at the herders sleeping in the doorways with their dogs.

Milamamshi had heard worse. She had been a grown woman ten years ago, when the old kingdom of [[pol:87|I'iti]] fell to pieces and a new one rose in its place in the same year, with the same name and a new king in the same palace, and the sheep had not noticed. All things turn on the wheel, the priests said; what ends will begin again. She had always found it a comfort. It was less of one, she found, from a wall.

* * *

On the morning of the oath the town went down to the gate as if to a festival, because nobody knew what else to do with themselves.

It was said the gate would not be opened to the Yi'utl, and that this was the point; the kings would meet in the gateway itself, which was neither in the town nor out of it. Milamamshi stood with Tlampin Tli and the child on a heap of rubble by the potters' kilns and saw what anyone could see from there: priests in their order, a fire carried in a bronze bowl, a white kid led and then not led, and in the shadow of the gate two young men who looked, at that distance, no bigger than the pegs on a loom.

"Ours is the one in white," said a man with a fish-basket on his shoulder. "They're the same age, you know. Born the same year, the pair of them."

"Is that an omen?" said Tlampin Tli.

"It's a fact. They've met before, out on the steppe. Ours lost that one too."

Nobody could hear the words of the oath. The terms came back through the crowd in pieces, passed from mouth to mouth like buckets along a fire-line, and each piece set off its own noise.

"Ten years. No war for ten years."

That was a cheer, ragged, and then a real one.

"Silver. Every year. For eighteen years."

A groan, and then laughter, because silver was the palace's trouble and not theirs.

"They won't come in. They're not coming in."

That was the loudest. A woman near Milamamshi sat down where she stood and wept into her hands. Yi Mish, on her mother's hip, shrieked because everyone else was shrieking.

Then the last piece. "Five districts. Five districts go to them."

"Which five?" Milamamshi said. She did not know she had said it aloud until the fish-basket man turned.

"The grazing country," he said. "Out past the ford to the hills, and down the steppe side." He shrugged under his basket. "Grass, then. Grass and stones."

He meant it kindly. He meant: not the town, not the quay, not the granary, not the kilns, not us. Tlampin Tli's hand closed on Milamamshi's arm and stayed there.

Over the gate the smoke of the offering went straight up into a sky without a breath of wind in it, which everyone agreed was a good sign. [[dei:8|Nish'il]] had taken it. Milamamshi watched it go and held the tally stick so hard that the notches printed themselves on her palm.

* * *

That night, on Tlampin Tli's roof, Ilsha'ash said, "I'm not going back."

She had known he would say it. She had known since the gate.

"There's nothing to go back to. They've eaten it."

"There's your father."

"My father is a heap of earth with somebody's cooking fire on it."

"Then I'll put the fire out."

"And live under them." He was not shouting; the street below was still full of people singing, and he did not want them to hear. "Pay them. Bow to their moon. Have them count your sheep for their king."

"Someone always counts your sheep for a king," she said. "Kings are weather. [[dei:9|Shimintlo]] sends a storm over the hill, and I don't move the fold for him."

He turned his bandaged arm over in the lamplight and looked at it as if it belonged to someone else.

"Ten years," he said. "Ten years of truce. Then we take it back."

She looked at him and saw his father, and behind his father her own father, who had said a thing very like that about something else entirely. All things turn on the wheel; she believed it. But she had never in her life heard anyone say it about a thing they wanted to stop.

"Stay, then," she said. "Stay in the town. Carry a spear for the king, if he'll have you. Come at lambing, and I'll feed you, if they let you over the ford."

He did not answer. After a while he put his good hand on the back of her neck, the way Mukhonwi used to, and left it there.

* * *

When the fires on the hills had thinned to nothing, she went out by the landward gate with a bag of grain Tlampin Tli would not let her pay for, and her son's hand on her shoulder the length of the street, and no further.

At the ford there was a Yi'utl soldier, a boy younger than Ilsha'ash, sitting on a stone with his feet in the water and his spear across his knees. He stood when he saw her coming and then seemed not to know what to do. Nobody had told him what to do about women with sacks.

She went past him into the water, to the knees. In the middle she stopped, as she had stopped every time she had crossed there since she was a bride, and scooped up a handful and let it fall.

"For [[dei:6|Nili]]," she said, "who is owed it."

The boy said something. Half the words were hers, bent a little, the way a word bends in the mouth of someone from three valleys over. One of them was the river's name, or nearly. He crouched on his stone, took up a handful of water and let it fall back, and looked at her as if to ask whether he had done it right.

She did not know. She nodded anyway, and went on up the slope.

The lambing hollow was a black ring and a scatter of bones. She knew one skull by its broken horn and did not look for the others. Somebody had cooked on the flank of Mukhonwi's mound; she knelt and pushed the ashes off it with her hands, and smoothed the earth back, and talked to him a while about the town and the oil jars and his son.

She was going down to the spring for water when she heard it: a cross, cracked voice from the thorn gully behind the rocks, where no soldier with a full belly would have bothered to go. The black-faced ewe. Another behind her, lame. And between them a lamb, born in the siege by the look of it, all legs and fury, who had never seen a person in its life and did not think much of this one.

She stood a long time. Then she took the old stick out of her belt and looked at it. Forty-one. She did not break it. She put it back where it had been, against her hip, and with her bronze knife cut a fresh green length from the olive by the spring, and sat down on her husband's mound, and made three notches.

"Three," she said aloud, to whoever owned the hill now, and to the river, and to the wheel.

It was not many. But it was a number. You could begin from it.

**Author's note.** The record gives the [[evt:2199|Siege of Bau]] no length, no commander and no dead; it records no sack, and the city's archives did not burn.
