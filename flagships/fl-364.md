**CORRESPONDENCE.**

**THE NEW ZHIZI AND THE HONEST TRADER.**

*To the Editor.*

Sir, — I ask the hospitality of your columns, not as a politician, for I have never sat on a committee in my life, but as one who has kept a counting-house in [[set:5|Nilu]] for thirty-one years and who has, until the last two, been able to tell his clerks what a coin was worth.

The facts are not in dispute, and I state them plainly. Until the year before last the [[cur:1130|Zhizi]] of [[pol:1988|Fuvu]] contained seven grams and seven-tenths of fine silver. It now contains five grams and a fraction over a quarter — 5.259 grams, by the Treasury's own published figure. A plain sum on the back of an invoice shows what the public has been slow to grasp: every Zhizi struck since the [[evt:47571|Debasement of the Zhizi]] carries a little more than two-thirds of the silver of the coin it replaced. Nearly one part in three has been taken out and nothing has been put back in its place but the King's head and a fresh date.

We were told at the time that the measure was temporary, that it was forced upon the Treasury by the charges of the [[evt:47431|Mbilu–Fuvu War]] and the obligations that followed it, and that the patriotic merchant would understand. The patriotic merchant did understand. He has understood for two years. What he cannot understand is why he alone is asked to go on understanding.

Consider what passes at my own door. When I buy from the foreign houses, they no longer quote in Zhizi at all; their agents wire for the price in weight of silver, and convert at the day's rate, and the day's rate is never in my favour. When I sell to the country dealers, they pay me in the new coin at its face. I am squeezed at both ends like a lemon in a press. Grain stands in the Nilu market this week at a Zhizi and thirteen hundredths the measure; the labourer who buys it is paid in the same light coin, and his wife knows to the hundredth what her basket costs, whatever the official papers may print about stability.

Worse than the loss is the uncertainty. A merchant can bear a bad year; he cannot bear a coin that may be lightened again by a stroke of the pen. Since the troubles of the [[evt:47622|Fuvu Revolution]] the country has been promised good government, open accounts and an end to secret expedients. Very well. Let the accounts be opened. Let the Treasury publish, quarter by quarter, how much silver it has bought, how much it has struck, and at what fineness. If the public is to be asked for sacrifice, let it at least be told the size of the bill.

I do not write against His Majesty [[per:46388|Nashuzzhe]], whose good faith I have never doubted, nor against the country, which I have served in my small way since before many of the present ministers were breeched. I write against a practice. The teachers of the [[mov:24|Uthungala]] have told us from childhood that the one Power asks of us justice in our dealings and moderation in all things. There is neither justice nor moderation in a coin that says one thing on its face and another in the scales. If a grocer shaved his weights by a third he would be in the dock by Thursday. I see no reason why the rule should change when the shop is the Mint.

I propose three things, and I put my name to them in all but print:

1. That no further reduction of fineness be made without a vote of the Chamber, published a full quarter in advance.
2. That the Treasury receive the old heavy Zhizi at its true silver value, and not at par with the new, so that the prudent man who hoarded honest coin is not punished for it.
3. That contracts made before the change be payable, at the creditor's option, in silver by weight.

I am told these proposals are impractical. So, I reply, is bankruptcy.

I am, Sir, your obedient servant,

A WHOLESALE DEALER OF THIRTY-ONE YEARS' STANDING.

*Nilu, 27 Bine 4840 of the Uthungala.*
