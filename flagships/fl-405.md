title: Notes from a Dry Year in Mbambanluli

**4365. Week 3.** Started this notebook because my sister said I should write things down instead of saying them to her at midnight. So: the rains are late. Not very late. The radio says the seasonal outlook is "below normal, low confidence." Low confidence is what they always say.

**Week 7.** Still nothing. The soil-moisture map on the agriculture service's page has gone from yellow to orange across most of [[reg:29|Mbambanluli]]. I teach the older children how to read those maps. Today one of them asked whether orange is bad. I said it depends. It doesn't depend.

**Week 10.** Planted anyway, like everyone. If you wait for certainty you plant nothing. The seed cost a third more than last year. The man at the co-operative said it is because of the ports, not the weather. I don't think he believes that either.

**Week 13.** The first rain. Forty minutes, then sun. The children ran out into it. By evening the ground was hard again.

**Week 17.** Reservoir at the dam is under half. The water authority has started rotating the pumps: our side of town gets mains water four days a week. People have dug out old cisterns. My neighbour found one under his kitchen floor that nobody knew about.

**Week 20.** The vegetation index for the whole south basin is the lowest in the record the service keeps. A meteorologist on the evening news used the phrase "the Great Drought in Yimla." She said it carefully, as though trying it on. By the morning everyone was saying it. It is strange how a name makes a thing more real. Before it was just a bad year. Now it is [[evt:41825|the Great Drought in Yimla]] and it has a logo on the news.

**Week 23.** Harvest, such as it is. Our plot gave perhaps one sack in five of a normal year. My sister's husband did not bother to cut his. He says it would cost more in fuel than it would make.

**Week 26.** Maize meal has doubled in price since the start of the year. The regional office has published a food-security assessment: two districts at "crisis," ours at "stressed." I looked up what stressed means. It means people are skipping meals but not yet selling the goats.

**Week 29.** They are selling the goats. The market is full of goats and nobody is buying, so the price of a goat is half what it was and the price of maize meal is twice what it was. You can do that arithmetic in your head.

**Week 31.** Three children in my class have stopped coming. One family has gone to the city to find work. I don't know about the other two. The school is now giving out a midday meal from the relief stocks. Attendance rose the next day. I am not proud of how glad that made me.

**Week 34.** The government has declared a famine emergency in the region. The phrase on the radio is "[[evt:41829|Famine in Mbambanlule]]," pronounced the way they say it in the capital, which nobody here does. The announcement gave numbers of people "at risk." It did not give the number of the dead. I do not think they know. I know of two, both old, both in the hills.

**Week 36.** Relief trucks came through. Sacks of grain, cooking oil, water tablets. The queue at the distribution point was four hours long and orderly. Nobody shouted. That is the thing I want to remember about this year if I remember nothing else: nobody shouted.

**Week 39.** The long-range forecast for next season came out. "Below normal." I did not read the confidence line.

**Week 41.** My sister asked what I am going to do with this notebook. I said I don't know. Maybe somebody reads it in a year when it rains, and thinks: so that is what it was like in [[reg:29|Mbambanluli]] in the year of the [[evt:41829|Famine in Mbambanlule]]. I hope it rains. That is all. I hope it rains.
