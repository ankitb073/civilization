The [[evt:4038|Collapse of the Navnga State]] in AY 819 is conventionally explained by the disasters of the 760s: [[evt:3665|The Marsh Pestilence]] and three defeats in the war with Tallin. The documentary consensus lists them among the causes, and rightly. But [[pol:40|Navnga]] survived those disasters by half a century. This article argues that the 760s weakened the empire without breaking it, and that the break came through the throne, in a run of usurpations of which the [[evt:3982|Usurpation of Bünin I]] in AY 810 was the last.

## The war and the pestilence

The Tallin–Navnga War (AY 761–770) was a war of conquest launched by Tallin, and it ended in stalemate with the Treaty of Vuvu. Navnga in fact won four of its seven battles. The three it lost were nonetheless severe. At the [[evt:3696|Battle of Shivange (AY 764)]] Navnga fielded 26,687 men against 13,753 and lost 7,300 against 3,438. At the [[evt:3713|Battle of Taulin (AY 766)]] it lost 1,499 men against 1,497, and at the [[evt:3727|Battle of Nilinge (AY 768)]] 2,084 against 797. Over the war Navnga lost 14,299 men to Tallin's 12,607. Its ruler, Dulomin the Conqueror, commanded in person at Shivange in 764 and at Taulin, and lost both.

The pestilence and the war cannot be separated. The record traces the sickness to Taulin in 765, and a year later the two armies fought at Taulin. Every battlefield of the war, from Rile and Shivange to Nilinge and Vuvu, appears among the thirty-two towns it reached. The record puts the deaths across all stricken states at about 1.48 million, with roughly three in ten dying where it struck. Armies are efficient carriers of disease, and I think it likely, though the sources cannot prove it, that the war spread the pestilence as much as the pestilence weakened the war.

## A recovery, then a collapse

What is striking is how well Navnga absorbed all this. On the record's estimates its population, about 8.1 million in AY 760, dipped to about 7.8 million by 770, held there through the plague years and passed 8.4 million by the collapse. Its territory grew in the same decades. The treasury fared worse. It fell by about three tenths between 750 and 790 and never regained its earlier level, which suggests that the war and plague left a fiscal wound rather than a demographic one. The climate offers a secondary strain: the 740s and 770s were cold decades, but no famine in the record strikes Navnga.

Dulomin reigned until 796. In the twenty-three years that followed, the throne changed hands four times, three of them by usurpation. Beroyramathen seized it in 796, Vakran in 807 and Bünin I in 810. In 816 Bünin II, remembered as "the Last", succeeded under a regent. A state whose crown had been taken by force three times in fourteen years had no settled claim to offer its provinces. They acted accordingly in 819.

## The successors

The six successor states support this reading. The core around the capital at Navnge took the old imperial name of [[pol:165|Yida]], a claim to an ancient legitimacy that the house of Navnga could no longer supply. The others formed around provincial centres: [[pol:166|Noni]] at Nin, [[pol:168|Rele]] at Rile, [[pol:170|Nothinga]] at Nodhinge, [[pol:167|Tivintusa]] at Tivintukhe and [[pol:169|Bäni]] at Khuele. Nin, Rile and Nodhinge had all been struck by the pestilence, and Rile had twice been a battlefield. It is tempting to read their independence as the delayed fruit of the 760s. I would put it more cautiously. Those towns were where the centre's failure to protect had been most visible. When the centre's legitimacy failed in turn, they had both the grievance and the experience to go their own way.

Weighed, then, the causes fall into an order. War came first in cost and pestilence first in suffering. Neither came first in consequence. The treasury loss was real but survivable. The usurpations were not, because they removed the one thing an empire of six potential states needs most: a single answer to the question of who rules. The Marsh Pestilence explains why Navnga's provinces had reason to doubt the centre. The succession explains why, in 819, nothing held them.
