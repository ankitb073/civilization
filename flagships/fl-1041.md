title: Four Rain, Three Death

Her mother had taught her to milk before she could carry the pail, and the first thing she taught her was the end of it. The milk comes out of a she-goat in two kinds, her mother said. At the start it is thin, with a blue look to it in the bowl. At the finish it is thick and yellow at the rim, and the last pulls, the ones you have to work for with the side of your thumb, are worth all the rest together. Those are the strippings. The first milk you can sell. The strippings are for the house.

Awbenem had not milked anything since the winter. The last two goats had gone to the king's soldiers in the spring, and the soldiers had paid an [[cur:757|Ozhatl]] apiece for them, which would not buy half a measure of grain. Her husband had gone with the levies the year before, when the rebels first came down on the city, and had not come back or sent word. Her mother had not lived to the spring. So in the year 1532 of the [[mov:57|E'eshio]], when there was nothing left on the steppe above Taenakhath but the steppe, she tied her son on her front and walked to [[set:76|Nemeth]], because people said there was bread in Nemeth, and because everyone on the road seemed to be walking that way.

There was bread in Nemeth. It cost what it cost. In the grain-sellers' row the price was called at two Ozhatl and three-tenths the measure, and the people who could pay it paid without arguing, and the rest stood and listened to the number as if it were weather. Awbenem remembered the row when it was an Ozhatl and a half and people grumbled. She remembered it because of the day the crier stood on the steps at the top and read out the peace the boy king had sworn: the emperor's merchants to trade in the markets of [[pol:1380|Tsey]] "free of all tolls", and fifteen years of truce, and at the end a line the crier read twice, because it was about himself: "Let it be read aloud in the markets of both lands." She had been seven. The emperor's merchants still had the shaded stalls at the top of the row and still paid nothing for them, and this was the fifteenth year.

She sat down with the other women against the granary wall, where the mothers sat. In the afternoon a broad woman in good wool came along the wall with a boy behind her carrying an empty basket. She looked at the babies, not at the mothers. Most of the babies were quiet. Awbenem's son was not; he had been shouting since noon.

The woman stopped in front of him.

'That one has a voice on him.'

'He's hungry.'

'They're all hungry.' She looked along the wall. 'Most of them have stopped saying so. Is he yours? Have you milk?'

Awbenem showed her. The woman watched the bead come and nodded as if she had been shown a coin and found it good.

'Get up,' she said. 'Bring him.'

* * *

They washed her in a stone room with a drain in the floor and gave her barley bread and a broth with fat standing on it in rounds, and she ate until she was sick into the drain, and then they gave her more and stood over her while she ate it slowly. The milk comes from the bread, the broad woman said, as though Awbenem might not know.

Then they took her up to the old woman.

She sat by a window with a wrap across her knees, though the day was hot, a small woman with a cough she kept closed in her fist. The broad woman bowed lower to her than to anyone they had passed on the stairs and said, 'Lady Eshlo,' and Awbenem understood that this was the king's mother, who had governed Tsey for him when he was a boy. She was fifty-five. She looked older. This year everyone did.

The old woman did not look at Awbenem's face. She took her hands and turned them over and ran a thumb along the insides of the fingers, where the skin was hard, and across the pad at the root of the thumb.

'Where are you from?'

'The steppe above Taenakhath, lady.'

'Goats?'

'Goats. A few sheep. Before.'

Eshlo let go of her hands.

The king's daughter lay in a basket by the bed, eleven days old, red and creased and furious, with a fuzz of black hair. She had been born on the day Four Rain and named for it: Yawne Ta. The queen's milk had not come. There had been a daughter three years before, Nine Wind, who had not lived to see out the year she was born in, and the old woman would not wait on the queen's milk a second time.

'Here is how it will be,' Eshlo said. 'My granddaughter first. Every time. Your son after, if there is any left.'

'There will be some left, lady.'

'See that there is.' She looked at the boy then, for the first time. 'What is he called?'

'Ye Lehtle.'

Behind her the broad woman touched two fingers to her breast, the sign of the wheel, against the name. Three Death. He had been born on the day, and her husband's mother had wept over it; but her own mother had said only what the women of the steppe always said, that the day gives the name and the milk makes the child, and had put him to the breast.

'Well,' said Eshlo. 'It isn't his fault.'

* * *

She did as she was told.

When the king's daughter cried, Awbenem put her to the left breast and then to the right, and when the first rush was over and the milk slowed and the small jaw had begun to work for it, she took her off, gently, a finger at the corner of the mouth, the way you ease a kid off its dam, and laid her in the basket. Then she put her son to the breast, and he took the rest.

The king's daughter first. Every time.

She did not think of it as cleverness. When she thought of it at all, she thought of it as doing exactly what she had been told by someone who had not asked the right question. The first you can sell. The strippings are for the house.

The palace fed her four times a day. She had forgotten what it was to be full, and then she remembered, and for the first days it frightened her more than hunger had. Her milk came in heavy. Her son put on flesh in folds at the wrists and the backs of the knees. The king's daughter grew too, more slowly, a long thin baby with a hard little grip, and slept, and cried less, and the broad woman said she had never seen a child take to a nurse so.

From the nursery window, at first light, Awbenem could see over the wall to the north gate and the carts going out through it, covered, one after another, with a man walking at each horse's head. On the steppe they had had no carts for it. After the third morning she fed the children with her back to the window.

Outside the walls the king's army lay in its camp and ate, and the smoke of its fires hung over the fields every evening like a second dusk. Everyone knew why it was there. The rebels had come last year from Ehaemew and been beaten, and everyone knew they would come again.

* * *

They came on a morning near the end of the month. Yawne Nem, who called himself a king in Ehaemew, brought ten thousand and sixteen men down on the city, and the king went out to meet him with thirty-three thousand seven hundred and five. The women of the palace climbed to the roof and saw nothing but a long brown smear of dust beyond the walls, and heard a sound like hail on a far-off roof that went on and on, and then came down again, because the children needed feeding whether there was a battle or not.

By evening it was over. Of the rebels, nine hundred and ninety stayed on the field, and of the king's men three hundred and thirty-five. Nobody in the palace knew those numbers. They knew the king was coming back.

He came to the nursery late, after the lamps. [[per:34156|Hehie]] was twenty-five years old. He had been king since he was ten, and his father had been killed at Kezhietle when he was nine, and there was dust in the creases of his neck and he smelled of horse. He picked his daughter up without asking, the way a man picks up a thing he is not sure he will be let hold again, and she woke and did not cry.

'Four Rain,' he said to her. 'My mother wanted to wait for a better day. The priests said a day is a day.'

Then he saw the other basket. 'And that?'

'My son, lord.'

He bent over it. The boy was asleep on his back with his arms flung up beside his head, round as a lamb in spring, his mouth still working at a dream.

'What is he called?'

She told him.

The king laughed, once, not unkindly. 'They'll find something like it for me, I expect.' He looked from one basket to the other, his daughter long and thin in the crook of his arm, and Awbenem stood with her hands knotted under her apron and did not breathe.

'He looks well on it,' the king said, and gave her back his daughter, and went out.

* * *

Eshlo came three nights later.

Awbenem had the king's daughter at the right breast. The milk slowed. She took her off and laid her down and lifted her son, and the old woman was in the doorway with a lamp in her hand, and had been there long enough.

They looked at each other. The boy began to cry. Awbenem put him to the breast because there was nothing else to do with him.

Eshlo came in and sat down on the chest by the wall, slowly, the way old women sit, and watched him feed. When he had finished, and fallen off asleep with his mouth open and a bead of yellow at the corner of it, she said:

'Strippings.'

Awbenem said nothing.

'I milked goats too, when I was a girl. Before I was anybody's wife.' Eshlo coughed into her fist and waited for it to pass. 'Did you think she found you by chance? I sent to the market for a herder's wife with a living child. This year a living child is a recommendation.' She tilted her head at the basket where the king's daughter slept. 'I said my granddaughter first. I never said what she was to have first.'

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below them in the dark a gate opened and shut.

'When my son was ten,' Eshlo said, 'I took him to Kezhietle to swear a peace with the men who had killed his father. He asked me if he ought to cry. I told him not in front of them. They had won, so they wrote it. Their merchants in all our markets, free of tolls. They have had the first of everything in this kingdom for fifteen years.' She looked at the boy, at the yellow at his mouth. 'We lost nine thousand men there in one day, the year before his father. Men who would have been ploughing. Everyone in the city says this hunger came from Kezhietle. Perhaps it did. I learned something there, anyway.'

'What, lady?'

'That the first isn't the best. It's only the first.'

She stood, one hand on the wall.

'This is the fifteenth year. The truce is out. The priests of the [[mov:57|E'eshio]] say that what ends will begin again. They mean it as a comfort.' At the door she turned. 'Go on as you are. She is a king's daughter. She will be fed all her life.'

She did not say the rest. She did not need to.

* * *

Awbenem sat a long time after the lamp had gone down the stair. Toward morning the king's daughter woke and fretted, and Awbenem took her up.

This time she let her stay. She let her go on past the slowing, past the place where the small jaw had to work for it, all the way to the end of the left side, strippings and all, while the hand opened and shut on her finger in the dark. Then she gave her son the right.

Nobody had said anything about that either.

The hunger went on into the next year. The rebels came again and were beaten again at the [[evt:34842|Battle of Nimid]], and the peace that ended the revolt was made in Nemeth, with an amnesty; the record calls it the [[evt:34843|Suppression of the Ehaemew Revolt]]. Eshlo died that year, at fifty-six. By the time the [[evt:34756|famine]] was over it had killed or driven out 511,282 people, one in nine of [[reg:11|Tsey]]. Yawne Ta, Four Rain, lived to be seventy-one. Of Ye Lehtle the record has nothing at all.

The day gives the name. The milk makes the child.

---

**Author's note.** The record sets this famine down to the [[evt:34642|Battle of Chezhietle]] (Kezhietle, in the speech of Tsey) of AY 3680, where Tsey lost 9,197 men, and does not say how. The peace [[per:34156|Hehie]] swore there at ten survives on a bronze tablet (PAL·TR·02234); its toll clause and closing line are quoted from it. Taenakhath's period form comes from the muster roll of AY 3706 (PAL·MU·00871). The Ehaemew chronicle (PAL·CH·06235) passes over all four battles. The record gives the king's daughter her dates, not who fed her. Tsey took up crop rotation in AY 3699.
