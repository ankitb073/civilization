title: A Wheel to Match

The lion came back to Nadige with three wheels and a tail that a king had chewed.

Nayedvu brought it down to the yard in a fold of her shawl, after the lamps in the lane had been put out, and set it on his bench as carefully as if it were hot. She was the boy's nurse. She had been his nurse since the night he was born in the palace on the rock at [[set:140|Adunambi]], and in all that time Nadige had seen her twice: once when she came down to order the lion, and now.

"The wheel," she said, and opened her other hand. The fourth wheel lay across her palm in two halves, split clean along the grain. The axle-peg was gone.

He picked up the lion first. Limewood, as long as his forearm, the mane cut in ridges and rubbed with ochre that had mostly rubbed off again. The body had gone dark at the shoulders, where small hands went. The tail stood up in a curl, the way no lion's tail does, because he had carved it to be held, and the tip of it was pitted all round with little crescent dents, close and shallow: the marks of milk teeth. [[per:4386|Natudne]] had been two years old when he got it, and king of [[pol:132|Tadudüve]] for a few months.

"He was running it down the palace steps," Nayedvu said. "The way he isn't allowed. It got away from him and hit the bottom step. That was before he took sick. He cried because it couldn't go at all."

"How sick is he?"

She did not answer that. "He keeps asking for it. In the fever he says it can't walk on three legs. The priests have been with him since yesterday. The queen has sent everyone out but me." She drew the shawl up over her hair. "Can you make the wheel by morning?"

"It's a wheel," Nadige said. "I make wheels."

He made them for ox-carts, mostly: great slow discs of three planks pinned together, as tall as a man's chest, that went out to the fields full of seed and came home full of grain. He had made the lion in the year of the [[evt:4032|Treaty of Adhizhili]], when the boy's name was set to the peace that gave away fifteen districts and a town, and the boy could not yet say his own name. His father had gone out to the [[evt:4021|Battle of Divnüzith]] the year before and had not come back. The queen had wanted the child to have something that went when he pulled it. That was how Nayedvu had put it, standing in this yard six years ago. *Something that goes when he pulls it.*

At the gate she stopped. "There are men up at the palace I don't know," she said. "On the far side. Since dusk." Then she was gone up the lane, quickly, close to the walls.

* * *

Namumbe had been awake the whole time, lying on the shelf behind the curtain with her eyes shut, and as soon as the gate closed she was at the bench in her blanket.

"That's the king's."

"It's the king's."

"It's ugly."

"It was the first lion I ever made," said her father. "Hold the lamp."

He went through the offcuts under the eaves until he found a piece of elm left from a cart wheel he had made in the spring, close in the grain and dry. Elm because elm will not split; that was the whole of what he knew about elm, and it had always been enough. He laid one of the three old wheels on the board and drew round it with a stick of charcoal. Then he set a peg in the middle of the drawing and swung a cord from the peg, to see where the drawing lied. It lied a little on one side. So did the old wheels, he saw, holding them up one after another against the lamp. They were good wheels. He had been younger.

He sawed the disc out with the small bronze saw and began to bring it round with the knife, paring toward the cord's line. Namumbe held the lamp too close, and then too far, and then, when he said nothing, just right.

"Is he going to die?"

"The priests are with him."

"Grandmother says old Natsunga was a baby when they made him king, the same as this one." Her voice went down into her grandmother's. "Three years old, and he sat on the throne fifty-nine years, and the priests said it was the great wheel bringing the old kings round again."

"Your grandmother says a lot of things."

"Was it true?"

"It was true." He blew the shavings off the edge. "He was king when I was born, and king when you were born. I didn't know there could be another one until he died."

When the disc was round he went to the hearth, and before he put the awl into the coals he took a pinch of the elm shavings and fed them to the fire, for [[dei:53|Bine]], whose house the hearth is, so that she would bite where he wanted her to bite and nowhere else. When the bronze was hot he set its point to the centre of the disc and leaned on it, turning, and the smoke came up sweet and sharp, and the hole went through clean and round with a black lip.

Out in the lane there were feet. A great many, going up toward the rock, not hurrying and not talking, and the soft knock of spear-butts on the edges of the paving, and once a voice, quite close, saying a name, and another voice telling it to be quiet. Nadige put his hand over the lamp. They stood in the dark with the smell of burned elm between them and listened until the feet had gone.

"Whose men?" Namumbe whispered.

"Somebody's."

"The voice said [[per:4427|Nanüdi]]."

"Then they're Nanüdi's." He took his hand off the lamp.

He cut a new peg from ash, fitted the wheel, and set the lion on the floor and drew it toward him by its cord. It went like an ox with a stone in its hoof: up and down, up and down, and a hitch at every turn.

"It goes funny," said Namumbe.

"It's the new one." He knelt and turned the lion over. The three old wheels had worn flat in places from six years of palace floors and palace steps, each in its own way, and the toy had learned to roll on its flats the way an old man learns to walk on a bad knee. Only the new wheel was round. Among the others it was the only thing wrong.

He took it off again and carried it out to the doorsill, which was a single slab of river stone, and sat down and began to rub the new wheel's edge against the stone. Not all round. He held each of the old wheels up to the lamp in turn, and read where it was worn, and rubbed the new one there and nowhere else, a few strokes, and looked again.

"You're spoiling it," Namumbe said from the doorway.

"I'm making it the same."

"Why?"

"Because he'll know."

When the flats were right he rubbed the pale elm with soot from the lamp and with oil from the side of his own nose, the way you darken a new tool-handle so that a man will trust it, until it was the brown of the others and had their dull shine. Then he fitted it and drew the lion across the floor, and it went up and down, up and down, in its own old rhythm, and Namumbe, who had fallen asleep against the doorpost, did not see it go.

* * *

The lane was still shuttered when he went up it. Nobody was at the well. On the steep street below the rock a dog stood in the middle of the way and watched him pass and did not bark.

At the kitchen gate there were two men he had never seen, with spears, and the dust of a long night on their legs.

"What's that?" said the younger, nodding at the bundle.

"The king's lion. I've mended the wheel."

The young one laughed. "Which king?"

The older one did not laugh. He looked at the lion in its cloth and then at Nadige's face, for a while, as if he were deciding how much of the morning to give him.

"The boy died in the night," he said. "The fever. Nanüdi is king. If you have business with the kitchens, go round to the kitchens, and don't go into the great court. They're busy in the great court."

Through the arch beyond the kitchen yard Nadige could see the priests already at work: white robes, smoke going up straight in the still air, and an ox for [[dei:48|Kudze]] standing patient with its head down among them, as oxen stand. Someone was chanting what the priests of [[mov:7|Kumbumbi]] chant on the great days, that all things turn upon the wheel, and that what ends will begin again. From somewhere higher in the palace, behind shutters, came the other sound, the one the women make.

Nayedvu came out of the kitchen with her eyes swollen half shut. A little girl held on to the back of her skirt with both fists and looked round it.

"He asked for it twice," Nayedvu said. "I told him you were making the wheel. He said, Will it be the same?" She put out her hands and Nadige gave her the lion, and she held it against her as she had held the boy, he supposed, as she had held him last night. "They'll take him down to the tombs when the priests have finished. [[dei:49|Tuve]] keeps kings well. That's what they say."

"Who's this?"

"His wife." She said it flatly, as if it were the name of a town. "She's three. She's been with me since they took him in to be washed. She doesn't understand any of it, and nobody has thought what to do with her."

* * *

The man who came in under the arch from the great court was not tall. He was grey at the temples and in the beard, a man near fifty, in a plain cloak with dust on its hem, and he stood the way men stand at harvest who have worked through the night and do not mean to sit down until it is done. Two men came behind him. Everyone in the kitchen yard went down on their knees, the cooks, the water-carriers, Nayedvu with the lion; Nadige knelt a moment late.

"Get up," said Nanüdi. "There's a great deal to do today and none of it can be done on the knees." His eye went round the yard as if he were counting what was his now and finding it more than he had expected, and less. It stopped on the lion.

"What is that?"

Nayedvu could not speak. Nadige said, "The king's lion, lord. A wheel was broken. I've made a new one."

Nanüdi held out his hand, and Nayedvu put the lion into it. He turned it over. He looked at the tail for some time. Then he crouched, there on the stones of the kitchen yard, and set the lion down and drew it toward him the length of his arm, watching the wheels, not the lion, the way a man watches the wheels of a cart he is about to buy. It went up and down, up and down.

"Which one is new?"

Nadige had to look twice. "That one, lord. The near one at the back."

"It goes straight."

"It goes the way it always went, lord."

Nanüdi glanced up at him at that, and held the cord a little longer than he needed to.

"My daughter had a duck," he said, to no one in particular. "On wheels, like this. It squeaked. I can't remember who made it." Then, in another voice: "Who ordered the work?"

"The king's household, lord."

"Then the household owes it." He spoke over his shoulder. "See he's paid. And see that nobody in this yard is put out. Not the cooks. Not the nurse." His eyes came down to the child behind Nayedvu's skirt. "Is that—"

"His wife, lord."

He looked at the girl, and the girl looked at the lion. He held out the cord to her. She did not move, and he did not move either. He stayed there on his heels in the kitchen yard with the cord held out, a king since the middle of the night, while the chant came through the arch from the great court and the ox lowed once and was quiet. The men behind him studied the wall. At last the girl let go of the skirt with one hand, and then the other, and came and took the cord, and ran.

She ran straight out through the arch into the great court, where no one was to go, and the lion went after her on its cord, bumping over the paving between the white robes and the smoke and the legs of the ox for Kudze. Nobody stopped her. One of the priests lost his place in the chant and found it again. Nanüdi stood up slowly and watched her all the way across, and when she was gone through the far door he said, "Go on," to the men behind him, and went back through the arch to his work.

Nadige stood in the kitchen yard with his hands empty. The last thing he had seen was the lion going away from him over the stones, rising and dipping a little on its worn places as it always had; and from where he stood he could not have said which of its four wheels was the new one, though he had made them all.

**Author's note.** The record gives [[per:4386|Natudne]] eight years and a natural death, and I have believed it. He was made king at two, after his father fell at Divnüzith; the enemy's tablet of the peace (PAL·TR·00237) says "The war which Nadudhni stirred up is ended," though that war began the year before he was born. His grandfather's sarcophagus lid (PAL·IN·03557) comes from the royal tombs of Adunambi, as does that of Nanüdi's daughter Navavun (PAL·IN·03558), who reigned after her father. The girl lived to be forty-six. The lion and its maker are mine.
