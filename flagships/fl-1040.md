title: From the Boat-Sheds to the Burying Hill
---
The rain stood over the east of the island every afternoon that summer, and it never once came west.

The girl watched it from the burying hill while her mother sang. It built out of a clean sky above the eastern flats: first a whiteness, then a bruise beneath the whiteness, then the grey threads let down, so fine at that distance that they looked like a warp strung on a loom before the weft goes in. Under them, two days' walk away, barley was drinking. Here the wind came off the dead fields smelling of hot stone, and the grave her uncle had opened with the digging stick was pale and dry to the bottom.

"Of the house with the whale rib over the door," her mother sang, "daughter of—" and then the name of the dead woman's mother, and then the dead woman's own, given once and given again, so that Plum, who receives the dead, would know exactly who was being sent down to her in the dark.

That was the custom on the harbour. You gave the house, and the mother, and the name. Then you gave one thing the dead had done with their hands, so that Plum would know them by more than a sound. For the net-maker's widow it was: *She mended nets with the cord between her teeth.* Everyone on the harbour had seen her do it. The mourners nodded and looked at their feet.

The girl was twelve that summer. She had carried the water jar to burials since she was small enough to need both arms for it, and she carried it empty now. They washed the dead in seawater. The wells were for the living.

"The storm god has moved house," her uncle said on the way down. It was what people in the harbour were saying that year. "He's found himself a better village."

"Don't," her mother said. It was the fourth burial of the month, and the month was half gone.

* * *

The joke had teeth because everyone knew which side of [[reg:0|Nukumuku]] was the wet one. The west had always had the rain. The east was the thin side, the side that walked over in bad springs to beg seed from the harbour farms and paid it back in dried fish and complaint. Now it was the second summer of [[cli:93|the drought]]. The harbour's grain pits had been scraped for seed in the spring, and the seed had come up and burned, and every afternoon the east stood green in plain sight.

"Again," her mother said that night. "From the boat-sheds."

They lay on the sleeping-bench with the door open for an air that did not come. Her mother had made a road out of the dead. She had made it long ago, when she took the singing from her own mother, and it ran the way the harbour ran: from the boat-sheds at the water, up the long street and through every lane off it, to the burying hill. At each door stood the people of that house who had gone down to Plum, the oldest at the back, each holding the thing they were known by.

"Boat-sheds," the girl said. "The old man who drowned in a flat calm. His oar came home without him."

"Name."

She gave it, and his mother's. She went up the street. The house with the ochred doorpost, where the potter's first wife stood with the bowl she had been burnishing when her pains began. The house with the gull skulls on the lintel. The house with the whale rib, where the net-maker's widow now stood, so new to her place that the girl had to stop and set her there, cord in her teeth, before she could go on. In the dark beside her, her mother's hand moved as she listened, two fingers lifting a little at each house they passed, as if to touch the post.

"Good," her mother said. "Go on."

When the girl first learned the road it had been a sparse walk, old people standing at long intervals like boundary stones. Since [[evt:15406|the hunger]] began it had filled. There were doors now with five standing at them. There were children on the steps. Lying in the dark, the girl could feel the weight of them, as if the whole street had tilted and they were all sliding down it toward her.

"Why do I have to carry them?" she said. "They're yours."

"Because I'm tired," her mother said. "And because the living can say their own names." Then, because she was fair: "And they aren't mine. They were my mother's first. They go on."

They had eaten seaweed boiled with a fistful of acorn meal, the pot passed back and forth across the hearth. Her mother had taken one mouthful and said she had eaten at the burial. There had been no food at the burial.

* * *

At harvest there was nothing to cut. In other years the last sheaf from the harbour fields was plaited and hung under the roof for Dligaga, who is the grain, so that she would winter in the house and go back into the ground with the seed. That autumn her mother pulled a bundle of yellow grass on the burying hill, plaited it into the shape of a sheaf, and hung it from the roof-tree.

"She'll know what we mean," she said.

The boats came over the water late in the year, three of them, low in the swell, and the harbour went down to the shore as it always did. The girl's uncle came back up the street in the evening with nothing in his hands and sat down on the step.

"They're dry over there too," he said. "Fields as bad as ours. They want fish. They want timber. They've brought bronze." He turned his empty hands over. "You can't boil bronze."

Some went back with the boats anyway, young men mostly, and two families with small children; it was said there was work on the far shore for anyone who would work for food. More went east on foot. Every few days another door was pulled shut and tied with a cord, and the girl began to notice how differently the road of names ran now: past houses where nobody lived, where only the dead stood on the step.

"A shut house still has its people," her mother said. "I'll keep them."

"Come east," said her uncle. "All three of us. There's grain over there, they say, and harbour people getting a bowl a day for work. Two days' walk."

"And who sings, if I go? They die here every day."

"They'll die over there as well. And over there they have their own singers."

"Not for these," her mother said.

They argued in low voices until the hearth was ash. In the end it was settled the way the girl had known it would be. Her mother took off the bronze ring she had worn since her marriage, a thing from over the water that had come down through her own mother's line, and pushed it onto the girl's thumb, the only finger it would stay on.

"Give it for meal," she said. "Not for kindness. Kindness you'll get or you won't."

Then: "From the boat-sheds. All of them."

It took most of the night. The girl went up the street door by door, past the shut houses and the open ones, house and mother and name and the thing in the hands, and twice she lost a child off a step and her mother waited without a word until she found it again. At the top of the street she came to the burying hill and the newest ones, the people she had stood beside that summer holding the empty jar, and she named them too, the net-maker's widow last.

"Good," her mother said. Her voice had gone far back in her throat. "You have them. Take them east. Bring them back."

* * *

The road went down off the harbour hills into flat country, and for a day and a half it was the same land they had left: fields cracked into plates, oaks letting go of their leaves as if it were already winter, a dead heifer in a ditch that nobody had had the strength to butcher. They walked among other harbour people, and among farm people from the west of the island who had already walked further than this, and nobody spent breath on talk.

On the second afternoon the girl smelled it before she saw it: wet earth, a smell she had forgotten she knew. They came over a low rise, and on the far side the ground was dark. A line ran across the grass where the rain had stopped, as plain as the line a wave leaves on sand. She stood with one foot on the pale side and one on the dark, and her uncle, who no longer made jokes, did not tell her to hurry.

The eastern villages had their harvest in, and they knew what was coming down the road. There were dogs at every gate and boys with slings on the roofs of the grain stores, and at the first village a man stood in the road with a staff and told the harbour people to keep walking. At the third, a broad woman with a red face looked for a long moment at the ring on the girl's thumb, and took it, and said they could sleep in her byre and beat sheaves on her threshing floor and eat what threshers ate.

Threshers ate twice a day. The girl had forgotten what that did to a body: how it steadied the hands and made the eyes slow and stupid with comfort. She hated, a little, how quickly she took to it.

The woman's daughter was seven or eight, round-faced in the way of a child who has never missed a meal, and she followed the girl everywhere, copying the harbour way of talking, which the east found funny. On the threshing floor she came close to listen.

"Who are you talking to?"

The girl had not known she was doing it aloud. "The harbour."

"Is it far?"

"Two days."

"Say it louder."

So the girl said some of it louder, the boat-sheds and the first few doors, and the child said it back, wrong, and then right. It became a game for the dark of the byre. *Do the whale rib,* the child would say, and the girl would do the whale rib.

* * *

In the east it rained into the winter, and after the solstice it snowed. A man from the harbour, one of the late ones, found them at the threshing floor with the news, which he gave quickly and while looking at something else, the way people gave news that year: that her mother had gone down in the cold after the solstice; that they had carried her up the hill on the same morning as three others; that the ground had been too hard to go deep.

"Who sang?" the girl said.

He looked at her as if he did not understand. Then he did.

"Nobody," he said. "There was nobody."

* * *

She went up the low rise next morning, the eastern child trailing after her through the wet with a sack over her head. From the top you could see a long way across [[reg:0|Nukumuku]]: the dark country under the cloud, then the line, then the pale land running away west toward the harbour hills, dry as old bone beneath the same grey sky. The rain fell behind them and on them and did not go one step further.

She faced west and began at the boat-sheds.

It took a long time. She went up the street door by door, house and mother and name and the thing in the hands, past the shut doors, past the children on the steps, and her voice came back to her flat and small off the wet ground. The child stood beside her and moved her lips at the parts she knew. At the top of the street the girl came to the burying hill, and the newest ones there, and the net-maker's widow with the cord in her teeth. Then the newest of all.

She gave the house: the singer's house, two doors below the whale rib. She gave her grandmother's name, which she had heard sung at a graveside when she was small and had never thought of as belonging to anyone. She gave her mother's name, once, and again, so that Plum would know exactly who was being sent down to her in the dark.

Then the thing in the hands. She had not thought about it until now, and now she did not have to. She saw again the sleeping-bench in the dark, and her mother listening, and the two fingers lifting at every house they passed.

"She touched every door," the girl sang.

The child beside her said it back. Rain ran off the sack and down her face.

The girl looked a while at the long pale land beyond the line, and at the place where the harbour was, too far off to see. Then she began again at the boat-sheds, slowly, so the child could keep up.

**Author's note.** The record counts 27,908 people of Nukumuku dead or fled in the [[evt:15406|Famine in Nuekhmokh]] of AY 1946–1950, twelve in every hundred, and keeps none of their names. The [[cli:93|Great Drought of Dyimbi]] behind it ran from [[evt:15405|its onset in AY 1946]] to 1957, and its map in the record takes in the island's west and its harbour, not the east. The only contemporary text linked to the drought is a temple record from a far country (PAL·RE·00063); the island's own first surviving words are a diary of AY 2201 (PAL·DI·00009).
