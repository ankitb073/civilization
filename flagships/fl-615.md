Few kings of [[pol:229|Ana]] were remembered by so confident an epithet as [[per:6827|I'pil Luesh'a III]], "the Conqueror", and few left so much evidence that can be weighed against it. He reigned for fifty-two years, from AY 1094 to 1146, longer than any king of his house before him. He fought five wars and came out of all five with the advantage. He swore four treaties that moved the frontier or filled the treasury, and a fifth that made a neighbouring kingdom his servant. The palace remembered all this as a single unbroken march. Read beside the treaty tablets, the enemy's chronicles and the record of the battles themselves, the reign looks longer, costlier and much less his own.

## A child on the throne

He was born in AY 1089, a Lizo of the royal house founded half a century earlier by the first king of his name. I'pil Luesh'a means "Seven Stone"; it was the founder's name, and to carry it was to carry the dynasty. His father, [[per:6720|Akhshish]], remembered as "the Lame", had come to the throne at thirteen in 1081 and died of natural causes in 1094, aged twenty-six. The boy was five.

The [[evt:6510|Accession of I'pil Luesh'a III]] was therefore a regency, and for eleven years the realm was governed by his mother, [[per:6826|Nie'khotlue'a]]. She is almost invisible in the court sources, which count every year from her son's accession as his. Yet the first war of the reign, the [[evt:6603|Second Ana–Nimit War]], began in AY 1103, when the king was fourteen and she still held the regency. Its palace chronicle, the Ana account of the war (PAL·CH·01141), set down by a scribe in 1144, opens "in the 10th year of I'pil Luesh'a", as though a boy had chosen to march. We should credit the decision to the court around the widow, and the fighting to a veteran general who had served the king's father. Nie'khotlue'a lived until 1135. The record says nothing of her part in affairs after 1105, and that silence is not proof that she had none.

## The wars of the young king

The war with [[pol:212|Nimit]] shows how court memory worked. The Ana account and the Annals of Ana, AY 1100–1199 (PAL·AN·00125) record victories at Izimue in 1104 and 1107 and the plundering of [[set:76|Nimid]], the enemy capital, in 1107. They pass over the battle of Nis in 1106, which Ana lost and in which, by the record, 1,693 of its soldiers died. Queen [[per:6761|Numih]] of Nimit raised a limestone stele for that day claiming 9,900 enemy dead (the Victory stele of Numih: Battle of Nis, PAL·IN·00456), and a later Nimit chronicler counted 3,915 enemy dead against 117 of their own. Both courts exaggerated in the usual way of royal monuments. What survives without exaggeration is the outcome. By the [[evt:6640|Treaty of Nimid (AY 1107)]] Numih ceded sixteen districts, most of them in Nis. The Boundary stone of I'pil Luesh'a III in Nis (PAL·IN·06503), found in that country, still marks the new line and calls down a curse on the enemy.

Meanwhile the kingdom of [[pol:230|Miezanitia]] had crossed the frontier. The Miezanitia–Ana War (1105–1109) was a defensive war, launched by Queen [[per:6770|Esiesh Mish]], and Ana won all three engagements the record knows, twice at Sase and finally at Onithie. The [[evt:6660|Treaty of Onithie (AY 1109)]] took no land. Its first clause made Miezanitia "a servant of Ana", bound to share its friends and its enemies. For a king of twenty it was a large inheritance of prestige, but other men had won it. The record does not name him as commander in any battle of these years.

## The holy war and the man in the field

He first appears in the field in his thirties. The [[evt:6734|Holy War of Ana against Mwehosh]], begun in 1119, was fought to bring the kingdom of [[pol:234|Mwehosh]] under the gods of Ana, and its prize was the old land of Mii. At the [[evt:6764|Battle of Biyitl (AY 1121)]] the king led 17,034 men against 2,000, and the enemy king, [[per:6924|Ia Igwia II]], fell on the field. The victory was real, and it cost only 142 men against the enemy's 540. A year later at Muvush an army under another commander was beaten. The king came back to the same ground twice. In 1125 he defeated [[per:6925|Ia Igwia III]], 14,090 men against 3,365. In 1126 the general [[per:7069|Mianim]], who had beaten Ana at Muvush, could field only 3,272 men against 10,009.

The [[evt:6812|Treaty of Muvush (AY 1126)]] survives in its tablet text, the Text of the Treaty of Muvush (PAL·TR·00427). Its preamble says the enemy "sent tribute to my camp". Its clauses gave Ana twenty-four districts, the towns of Biyitl and a river frontier, and they required that the worship of the La'im gods be permitted in Mwehosh and that its ruler make offering at their altar. That was what a holy war achieved: not the conversion of hearts, which no tablet could secure, but a king's public bow. The Annals record one of the three battles of Biyitl and none of the losses. For the whole war the record gives 3,031 dead for Ana and 3,079 for Mwehosh.

The pattern recurred against [[pol:274|Khyeka]]. The king's generals fought the Ana–Khyeka War (1132–1136), and he appears only at its end, swearing the [[evt:6913|Treaty of Kheye (AY 1136)]] with [[per:7038|Nathluun]], who had held his throne for barely a year. Its terms were the heaviest of the reign: sixteen districts, free passage for the merchants of Ana, and thirty-three talents of silver every year for twenty-seven years.

## Pestilence and the last war

The last war was not of his choosing. In 1138 Nimit, now ruled by Numih's son [[per:6949|Lush'a'mukha]], tried to win back what had been lost, and the Nimit–Ana War was fought around Nimid. Its numbers show how far the balance had tipped. At the [[evt:6937|Battle of Nimid (AY 1139)]] the king, defending with 18,369 men, routed a force of 895 under [[per:7247|Nitllu Lush'a]] and lost 96 men to the enemy's 418. At the [[evt:6978|Battle of Nimid (AY 1142)]], against 868 men led by Lush'a'mukha himself, he lost 82 to the enemy's 144. Armies twenty times the size of their enemies do not usually lose many men, and these did not.

The heavier toll of those years lies in the Annals' entry for the 46th year: "Pestilence struck the cities: the Harbour Flux." The record puts the toll of that plague, which lasted from 1139 to 1142, at about half a million dead in twenty-three cities, among them Ana itself, roughly one in seven of the people it reached. Beside it, the war cost Ana some 250 men. The [[evt:6979|Treaty of Nimid (AY 1142)]] brought an indemnity of 159 talents of silver, and the Annals end the reign with the words "the land had rest". For many households it was a time of burials.

## The king and his heirs

He married late. His first child, a daughter, was born in 1121, the year of Biyitl, and died three years later. His sons [[per:6943|Ine'ash]] and [[per:6944|Esiesh Niema]] followed in 1123 and 1124. Their mother's name does not survive. The king died of natural causes in AY 1146, aged fifty-seven, and the accession of Ine'ash passed without disturbance.

What came next qualifies the epithet. Ine'ash reigned eight years and died at thirty-one. His brother reigned two and died at thirty-two, leaving an infant on the throne under a regent, as I'pil Luesh'a himself had once been. The realm he had enlarged by fifty-six districts and about a million people held together for another century. In 1250 Ana broke apart, after revolts in the borderlands his grandson's treaties had brought within it.

How much of this was his? The record lets us say that he commanded in five battles and won all five, each against a much smaller enemy, and that most of his gains came by treaty after his generals' victories. It also shows that the annalist, who remembered six of the thirty-one battles Ana fought in the century he covered, gave the plague a single line. "The Conqueror" was a name earned by fifty-two years on a throne, by survival and by other men's work. The best monument to I'pil Luesh'a III is the boundary stone in Nis: it is genuine, it is modest, and it has outlasted everything it claimed.
