title: An Empire Pays Tribute: Matatren, Nyebin and the Second Virnado–Talenonoy War

The [[evt:1416|Second Virnado–Talenonoy War]] (AY 455–458) is short, one-sided and well attested, which makes it a useful test of method. Both palace accounts are clay-tablet copies recovered by Nambili's campaigns. The Virnado account (PAL·CH·00165, Radape) was excavated at Virnadhu in AY 4931. The Talenonoy account (PAL·CH·00166, Nunmula) came from Taalinnuy in AY 4908. Both were written within four years of the peace: Matatren's in AY 459, Nyebin's in AY 462. Distance in time excuses neither. My argument is that the two scribes distorted different things for one reason. Each court had to explain a humiliation of its own, and only one of them had actually lost the war.

## Field by field

*Who began it.* The record names [[pol:22|Virnado]], which went to war to exact tribute. Nyebin says so plainly: "Nginulin of Birnado, forgetting the oaths his fathers swore, set his hand against Talenonoy." Matatren reverses it with the stock formula of victors' annals: "Duvemitun of Talenonoy broke the peace and sent raiders across the border of Virnatho. So the king went out to punish him."

*The battles.* The record has three engagements in [[reg:7|Tunnge]], all won by Virnado: the [[evt:1431|Battle of Jerathnu (AY 456)]], the [[evt:1439|Fourth Battle of Tunnge]] in AY 457 and the [[evt:1449|Battle of Nuwkennga (AY 458)]]. Matatren has all three, with the right years and commanders. Nyebin has two. Jerathnu is simply absent, and the two defeats he admits come with excuses: at Tunnge "by treachery our army gave ground", at Nuwkennga "in a great storm our army could not hold the field".

*The dead.* The record gives Virnado 1,114 dead (500, 383 and 231) and the [[pol:54|Talenonoy]] forces 27 (7, 5 and 15). Matatren gives his own side 212, 163 and 98, which comes to 473, and the enemy 16, 11 and 34, which comes to 61. Each Virnado figure is about 42 per cent of the record's, and each Talenonoy figure is roughly two and a quarter times it. The enemy musters are also scaled: "39 men and chariots beyond counting" at Jerathnu, 60 at Tunnge and 75 at Nuwkennga, against the record's 21, 32 and 40. Nyebin gives Virnado 734 and 443 dead in the two battles he admits, roughly 1.9 times the record's figures, and his own side 3 and 8. Ironically, the defeated scribe's total for Virnado's dead, 1,177, lies closer to the record's 1,114 than the victor's own figure does. He got there by inflation, not accuracy.

(The record's own proportions are strange: Talenonoy detachments of a few dozen men cost Virnado hosts of two to three thousand forty times their own losses. I flag the problem without claiming to solve it.)

*Commanders.* No commander was killed. Neither tablet says otherwise.

*Outcome and land.* Virnado won. The [[evt:1450|Treaty of Nuwkennga (AY 458)]] ceded nineteen districts of Tunnge and imposed 1,004 kg of silver a year for 26 years, with a ten-year truce. Matatren has Talenonoy coming "to beg for peace", and he counts forty-five districts won, presented as land "returned". Nyebin calls it a stalemate: "wearied of bloodshed, the king granted peace to Birnado ... The border was settled." He admits a few villages at most, and no tribute at all.

## What each court needed

Matatren wrote in the fourth year of Takaelit, the commander who had become king in the middle of the war. His victories were the reign's credentials, so the cost had to be small and the prize large. A war launched for silver had to look like the punishment of a raider.

Nyebin's problem was worse. He wrote for Duemitun, sovereign of a state that called itself an empire, and that empire was now paying yearly tribute to a kingdom. Denying the whole war was impossible within living memory. But Duemitun had commanded at Jerathnu and Tunnge himself, so the first defeat was forgotten and the second became treachery. A dictated treaty became a peace "granted". His one concession, that Virnado began the war, cost him nothing: an empire that is attacked loses no face by saying so.

An archaeologist's caveat: both tablets are copies, and a copyist may have touched the figures. But each text follows one consistent rule, which points to its author rather than to accidents of transmission.
