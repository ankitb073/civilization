title: Cold Years and the Black Metal (AY 1750–2000)

In AY 1750 a boy in his second year at the tablet-house of Adhgumgi, in the kingdom of Khanji, copied out the names of the gods, the praise of his king and a list of proverbs. Then he wrote the new letters in order three times. His master corrected him in the margin. The boy had spelled the sea-god with "the letter of the throat," and the master wrote: "The sea has no throat. Again!" The boy had also written that the conquered must pay silver for "thirteen" years. "Thirty-one," the master wrote, "not thirteen. Would you rob the king of eighteen years of silver? Again!" At the foot of the tablet is a verdict that any teacher will recognise: "the second row is crooked; the third is good. This boy will be a scribe."

The tablet, *The New Letters* (PAL·PH·00070), was excavated at Adhgumgi and survives almost complete. It is an early witness to the Mbingi letters, made in Khanji two years before, and its preface makes their promise plain. Under the old signs a boy "sat ten years in the tablet-house and came out with a bent back." Now there were "Few letters, many words. A boy learns them in one season." The age that followed spread that promise from court to court. The Ladhu script was made from the Mbingi letters in Khishkhizi in 1757, the Ngayelumbe script came from the Ladhu in Kayagu in 1764, and in 1807 the kingdom of Nambe adopted the Luzhe script, which is the script in which this book is printed.

The two and a half centuries from 1750 to 2000 were the coldest since AY 500–750. The mean temperature stood 0.36 °C below the long-run mean, and in the 1790s and 1830s it fell to almost 0.8 °C below. They were also deadlier for plague than any period this history has yet described. Even so, the political map held its shape remarkably well. The record counts thirty-nine states in 1750, thirty-nine in 1870 and thirty-nine in 1990. The people living under them numbered 62.3 million, then 62.9 million, then 65.4 million. The great collapses came in two clusters, one at the beginning of the period and one in its middle. Between them came a cold spell, two great plagues and the first iron.

## The old empires fall (1750–1802)

The [[pol:414|Tagath]] empire, whose wars over Pyivi have run through the last two chapters, entered this one at its height. Under [[per:13014|Namumbiv]] the Old (1704–1751) it ruled 344 districts. It survived him by only ten years. In 1751 [[per:13450|Naguvu]] took the throne in a usurpation that the record counts among the causes of what followed. In 1753 the Silent Ague broke out at Ajü and spread to twenty-one more cities, Tagath's among them. It killed 620,859 people, nearly a third of those it reached. The record traces the plague into two rebellions: the [[evt:13524|Secession of Kambizhin]] in 1759 and the [[evt:13540|Secession of Kayagu]] in 1760. In 1761, under [[per:13514|Nanüthnan]] the Last, came the [[evt:13555|Collapse of the Tagath State]]. Its four heirs included the kingdom of [[pol:598|Luthe]], which lasted until 2263, and the kingdom of [[pol:599|Kane]]. [[set:9|Dagafu]] had been an imperial capital for almost eight hundred years.

The [[pol:410|Pibetie]] empire, ruled from [[set:91|Pivithie]], outlasted Tagath by a generation. Its greatest size came late: 8.9 million people and 365 districts in 1780, under the queen [[per:13670|Nimemovi]] the Red. The cracks, however, were already old. The record traces them to the usurpation of I'pil Lieze in 1762, after the death of [[per:13211|Ma'she Movi]]. They widened with the secession of Etlishithyi in 1794 and a revolt at Myinve in 1797. In 1802, under [[per:13865|Ma'she Foyatl]] the Last, came the [[evt:13988|Collapse of the Pibetie State]], and six states emerged. One kept the name and the capital: a new [[pol:612|Pibetie]] that would last until 2134. Another, the kingdom of [[pol:613|I'a'ie'i]], kept the annals that are now our best view of the aftermath (PAL·AN·00290, AY 1802–1870). Read beside the annals of Veni (PAL·AN·00291), another heir, they describe the same breaking from two different gates.

The droughts of these decades were regional again. The [[evt:13433|Onset of the Great Drought in Khishkhih (AY 1754)]] spread over 255 districts and brought famine to Shithiu. In the same years the [[evt:13640|Onset of the Great Drought in Baan (AY 1779)]] covered as many districts again. The old faiths also kept gaining at court. The kingdom of [[pol:565|Mii]] adopted [[mov:0|La'im]] in 1766. The [[evt:13743|Conversion of Tlayiyan to La'im]] came in 1774, and [[pol:605|Dzebampiba]] adopted [[mov:2|Jinru]] in 1788. In 1791 [[pol:604|Tunnga]] adopted [[mov:5|Lelmi]].

## The cold years (1790–1850)

The record dates the onset of the Cold Spell to 1790, and it lasted until 1849, sixty years in all. Then the mountains made it worse. Mount Tsimi erupted in 1797, an eruption of the sixth magnitude on the record's scale, and the 1790s were the coldest decade since the 1240s, 0.73 °C below the mean. Mount Lamlekheze erupted in 1836, and the 1830s were colder still, at 0.79 °C below. The median price of grain across forty-odd markets rose from 12.2 grams of silver the measure in the 1750s to 15.3 in the 1790s. It was still above 15 in the 1840s.

Into the first cold decade came [[evt:13851|The Spotted Death]]. It broke out at [[set:155|Yinnge]] in 1799 and spread along the trade roads to fifty-three more cities. It killed about 1,617,547 people, roughly one in five of those it reached. The record follows its consequences for decades: two secessions of Ttogannga in the 1830s, the end of Oshodonga in 1837 and the end of Nonenga in 1868. Drought followed. The [[evt:13962|Onset of the Great Drought in Tsiey (AY 1805)]] and the [[evt:13961|Onset of the Great Drought in Daadüdhih (AY 1806)]], the second across 424 districts, struck within a year of each other.

The empires that came through the cold were led by rulers with very long reigns. In [[pol:439|Yeda]], [[per:13831|Enelngath]] became queen at eleven and reigned from 1797 to 1836, through most of the cold. Before her, the queen [[per:13443|Ngalath]] had ruled from 1750 to 1780. [[per:13788|Bomakotnakhen]] ruled [[pol:580|Yennga]] from 1781 to 1825. In the [[pol:506|Puvu]] empire at [[set:12|Kabaf]], [[per:13888|Nashyidi]] (1792–1826) brought the state to its greatest size, 4.7 million people, in 1820. Puvu nevertheless came to its end in 1842. In [[pol:526|Los]], [[per:14083|Im'am'igi II]] reigned from 1815 to 1849.

## Fever, iron and the new teachers (1850–1910)

The worst plague of the period came as the cold ended. [[evt:14468|The Black Ague]] broke out at Rile in 1853, reached forty-seven more cities and killed about 1,644,500 people, more than a quarter of those it reached. In the same year the [[evt:14467|Onset of the Great Drought in Nis (AY 1853)]] began the dry spell that the record later ties to the famine of 1876 in [[reg:11|Tsiey]] (182,415 dead). The famine sent 159,243 Unsatlmum migrants into Mikhukhizhi.

The year 1853 also brought a new way of thinking. At Tlilisyi, in the city-state of Tlalesyi, [[per:14484|A'insim'a]] founded the [[mov:47|Pieshayi]] teaching. Its followers held, as one witness put it, that "a man may find the truth by his own light and reason." That witness was a smith. In 1866 Yiedi'ada of Tlalesyi had a tablet set up at the market gate (PAL·NP·01079). He announced that he had drawn "the black metal" out of the red stone that potters ground for paint. His furnace was "taller than a man," worked by "six boys at the bellows, three and three." The first lump came out "like a loaf of black bread, full of holes, ugly, smoking. I struck it with the hammer. It did not break." He saw its value at once: "It does not need tin, which comes from far away and costs much." He also saw who was listening. The priests claimed a share for the gods, while the Pieshayi reasoners "stood by the furnace all night and asked me how many measures of charcoal, how many of stone." Iron was then invented again, independently, in Kambizhin in 1898, in Dubegupuinga in 1928 and in Shavi in 1935. This repeated reinvention says something about the age. Each smith worked within reach of an idea that had not yet travelled to him.

The old empires, meanwhile, went on fighting the wars that would end them. In the [[evt:14461|Yeda–Yufkoy War]] (1849–1857), Yeda attacked the [[pol:625|Yufkoy]] empire in [[reg:16|Yidhe]] to make it pay tribute. Yeda won five of six battles and gained thirteen districts, three towns and about 30 talents of silver a year for twenty-seven years. It lost 25,564 men to Yufkoy's 5,834, and 13,497 of them died on a single day at Navnge in 1851. In the [[evt:14607|Tomözel–Va'mi War]] (1864–1873), the kingdom of [[pol:492|Tomözel]] attacked the [[pol:529|Va'mi]] empire in [[reg:14|Yimla]] and gained nothing. At Nirun in 1867 it lost 7,469 of 20,758 men. In the [[evt:14777|Turamtu–Va'mi War]] (1885–1894) the kingdom of [[pol:466|Turamtu]] fought Va'mi in [[reg:4|Ayshi]] and won outright: seventy-nine districts, six towns and about 75 talents of silver a year for eighteen years. In the [[evt:14844|Second Yennga–Yeda War]] (1891–1900), Yennga invaded Yeda's lands in [[reg:8|Diebeguvebenga]] and took fourteen districts and two towns.

Each of these wars appears in the record's list of causes for the collapses that followed. The Yennga court account of the last one (PAL·CH·02739) shows how such histories hid their costs. For the battle at Nilu in 1896 it reports 7,453 Yeda dead against 826 Yennga, where the record gives 4,403 and 2,261. It does not mention that the Yennga king, Shekhshen I the Brave, fell in that battle. The next entry is simply dated to "the 1st year of Shekhshen," and only the regnal count shows that a new king, the eleven-year-old [[per:14845|Shekhshen II]], had taken his father's place.

## The breaking of 1892–1911

The bill came in four instalments within nineteen years. The first fell on Tomözel, ruled from [[set:3|Ishwyithie]] by [[per:14501|Eshuese]] the Last, who had become queen at the age of two. With the [[evt:14851|Collapse of the Tomözel State]] in 1892, the record names the defeats of the Va'mi war and of a later war with Tlayiyan as causes. Its heirs included the [[pol:649|Eshueethie]] empire, which lasted until 2137, and the city-state of [[pol:650|Fienanna]]. Va'mi followed in 1906, broken by its defeats in Ayshi. The [[evt:14987|Collapse of the Va'mi State]] left the kingdoms of [[pol:658|Vami]] and [[pol:659|Iti]] among its heirs.

Yeda had reached its greatest size only in 1890: 8.6 million people and 297 districts under [[per:14559|Pavbelshewen]] the Stout. Twenty years later, under the queen Pemshenlamath the Last, came the [[evt:15030|Collapse of the Yeda State]]. The record's list of causes runs back to the slaughter at Navnge in 1851 and forward through two secessions to the defeats of the Yennga war. A new [[pol:664|Yeda]] at [[set:0|Taulin]], destined to reach 9.1 million people, took up the name, beside the kingdom of [[pol:665|Jenontono]]. A year later the victor of the Va'mi war followed the loser. The [[evt:15052|Collapse of the Turamtu State]] in 1911 had among its causes the Black Ague of 1853 and three of Turamtu's own defeats in the war it had won. The [[pol:670|Turamtu]] empire and the kingdom of [[pol:671|Bimunga]] emerged from it. Readers who know the scholarship on the Age of Iron will recognise the pattern: rival states fell together, bound by the wars they had fought against one another.

Hunger made the breaking worse. The [[evt:14938|Onset of the Great Drought in Daadüdhih (AY 1903)]] spread over 520 districts, and the famine that followed killed 388,018 people, one in seven. The [[evt:14937|Onset of the Great Drought in Baan (AY 1903)]] began in the same year. At Shyih in Daadüdhih, grain cost 16 grams the measure in the 1880s and 69 in the 1900s, the highest price of the period. At Taulin it rose from 19 to 36 in the decade before the old Yeda fell, and at Yinnge from 16 to 45. Unlike the cold years, this was a crisis in particular markets. The median across all markets reached 15.7 grams in the 1910s and then fell back.

## Roads, horses and the new century (1905–2000)

Some of the most original thinking of the period came out of these hungry years. In 1905, at Itlishithyi, where grain would cost 65 grams in the 1910s, Setlietlada founded the [[mov:48|Eshuenatlse]] school of philosophy. In 1914 Najige founded the Puzhje faith at Kashunji. A dialogue from that year, *Najiga and the Priest at the Gate of Khudhas* (PAL·SC·00117), records how the faith broke with one of the oldest ideas in our sources, that "all things turn on the great wheel." Najiga asks the priest about the men killed at Adhgumgi: "Many died, and many had no sons. Where is the wheel for them?" His own answer is new: "I say the road runs straight. It begins once, and it ends once, and at its end every man is weighed, the king with the herdsman."

The kings did not take up the new thinking, and their stones went on inflating their victories. In the [[evt:15096|Second Luthe–Nilu War]] (1915–1925), Luthe attacked the kingdom of [[pol:577|Nilu]] and lost twice as many men as it killed, 20,404 against 10,256, for a stalemate. Its king Natumzu, in the forty-sixth year of his reign, claimed 6,400 dead at Khadambe (PAL·IN·01079); the record gives 930. Mount Lamliladol erupted in 1940, and the 1940s fell to 0.61 °C below the mean. In 1946 a new kingdom of [[pol:686|Nilu]] arose, which would last until 2271.

The greatest war of the period came late. In the [[evt:15750|Third Tamthal Trade War]] (1970–1978), Eshueethie went to war with the [[pol:555|Tlayiyan]] empire over the roads of [[reg:18|Tamthal]]. An Eshueethie veteran's account (PAL·CH·08436) explains the war through tolls: "They took a tenth; then they took a fifth; then they took a third." Eshueethie's young queen Ishfama led the army herself. At Tliyiya in 1972, the captain says, the enemy "broke like a pot thrown on stone. Fourteen thousand of them lay on the ground, and Esiesh Shimen their king lay among them. Of us fewer than two thousand fell." The record agrees almost exactly: 14,738 and 1,686, and the king dead on the field. Unlike the royal stones, the captain also admits the defeats: "the day went against us. I do not hide it." Tlayiyan lost 39,782 men to Eshueethie's 16,693. By the Treaty of Tliyiya it gave up forty-four districts of Tamthal and agreed to pay about 190 talents of silver a year for forty years. It broke apart in 1995.

The other wars of the late century show the familiar pattern. In the [[evt:15781|Yufkoy–Tiy War]] (1974–1984), the [[pol:623|Tiy]] empire defended itself and won an indemnity of some 19,400 kilograms of silver. Yufkoy's king Loban III still raised a stele claiming 22,300 enemy dead at Dainagafu (PAL·IN·01139), where the record gives 4,845. A year after systematic philosophy was first set out at Kambizhin in 1982, its author Naguvu gave a lecture there on the explanation of victories (PAL·SP·00007). Whatever explains every result, he said, "explains everything, and therefore it explains nothing." The [[evt:15942|First Turamtu–Yennga War]] (1991–1998) would have served him as an example. Yennga won four of five battles, held its lands and lost 25,587 men to Turamtu's 13,113. The Treaty of Zhivampive (PAL·TR·01095) ended the war with a ten-year truce and a marriage. [[per:15623|Tathreltafvafen]] the Victorious, then sixty-two, was to marry the daughter of his enemy.

The last new idea of the century came from a stable. In 1992 Nalumbunu, keeper of the royal horses at Kabaf and "the daughter of a groom," wrote to her king about the battle at Kashunji (PAL·LT·01708). She had watched the chariots stick in the river mud while hill herders "sit upon the backs of their beasts and cross the same mud as a duck crosses water." "Why," she asked herself, "does the horse of the king pull a box, when it could carry a man?" She spent two years teaching boys to ride, and the record credits her with the first mounted cavalry. Speech changed as it always had: the record dates sixty-nine sound changes to the period and six new languages by 1899. Of the 838 documents that survive from these years, 333 are inscriptions and only 28 are letters, so we know the cavalry's inventor by luck. In the next chapter her horsemen ride into a world dominated by Eshueethie, Yennga and the new Yeda.
