*Printed from the author's pocket-diary, the entries of the five days ending 17 Ewsha 2605 of the [[mov:57|Eheshio]], without correction.*

**13 Ewsha.** Levee at the palace at ten. His Majesty [[per:45307|Melianuo]] in good colour, talked for a quarter of an hour about the new harbour cranes at [[set:13|Tzeventoa]] and not at all about the crowds in the great square before the arsenal, which one could hear through the closed windows. The Chamberlain asked me afterwards whether the morning papers had been kept from the King. I said I had no idea. This was a lie: I keep them from him myself, on instructions.

**14 Ewsha.** The figures again. Every sheet in the city prints them in black type a finger high, and every sheet is right. In [[evt:46030|the war in Luthe]] the expedition lost twenty-four men in three years, killed, by its own count, more than eighteen thousand of the enemy, and lost every battle at Kavu: [[evt:46035|the first]], [[evt:46042|the second]], [[evt:46055|the third]]. Then [[evt:46056|the treaty signed at Kavu]] bound us to pay an indemnity to a people whose dead outnumbered ours by more than seven hundred to one. The coachmen can do this arithmetic. So can the stokers on the night steamers. The Crown still cannot.

I note for my own conscience that I drafted the despatch in which His Majesty called the second battle "a reverse of no consequence." I wrote what I was told.

**15 Ewsha.** Couriers all day from the provinces: the dockers out at three ports, the bargemen at four locks of the canals. By evening no coach had left the capital for nine hours. In the Assembly a member read the indemnity clause aloud, slowly, twice, and sat down without a word. They applauded him for a full minute.

Dined with three officers of the Guard. None of them will fire. One said: "We swore to the King, not to his treaty." I wrote it down at the table, which they noticed and did not mind.

**16 Ewsha.** Morning: tens of thousands in the square before the arsenal, by the police estimate, which is always low. They sang the old hymn of compassion, the one every schoolchild knows, about the wheel that brings down the high and raises the low. The priests of the Eheshio stood on the cathedral steps and did not tell them to go home.

Noon: the Council of Ministers resigned, with one exception. The exception was myself. I went to the King at two. I told him what the Guard had said. I told him that I could get him out of the city by the coast road before dark, with his family, his papers and his dignity, and that I could not promise any of the four after dark. He asked me whether I had become one of them. I said I had been one of them since Kavu, and so had he, if he would only read the papers.

He signed at a quarter to five.

**17 Ewsha.** Six in the morning. The Assembly sat through the night and at a little after four proclaimed the Republic of [[pol:1943|Mana]]. They then elected a President. I will not pretend to modesty in my own diary: it was me. The minority could have sat at one table. [[per:45401|Sha Eme]], courtier, is now Sha Eme, citizen and President, and must learn to sign his name differently.

The former King left the palace at midnight in a closed carriage, under escort of the same Guard that would not fire on the crowd. No one jeered. I asked that it be so, and the crowd, to its credit, did not need asking.

So ends [[evt:46121|the revolution]], or so the morning editions say. A revolution is not ended by an edition. I have to find the money for the indemnity, which has not become smaller because the man who signed it has gone; I have to keep the mails running; and I have to persuade the people in the square that the Republic is not a magic word.

Melianuo, for all his faults, never had to do that. He only had to be King of [[pol:1943|Mana]]. I have to be believed.
