No portrait of [[per:41437|Piemkathrelen]] survives, and no letter, seal or grave. What survives is two account books, bound ledgers, complete and original, still kept in the monastery library at [[set:170|Zhiyiyzhikhti]], the town where he wrote them. Both (PAL·LE·02997 and PAL·LE·02998, six years apart) open: "Journal of the house of Ruyiku, kept at Zhiyiyzhikhti by Puyimkathrilin". They were translated by an earlier Navaavun (AY 4786–4865), a woman with whom I share a name, a trade and, so far as the record shows, no kinship. The rest must be read from the world around them.

## Sixty years under one king

He was born in AY 4335, a man of the Jinru people; his name means *eagle strong*. The record names no father, mother or wife, and is silent on his childhood. Of his faith it shows only that he dated his books by the [[mov:58|Veshethmekh]] era: "2131 of the Vishithmikh".

His whole life fell inside one reign: [[per:41070|Livdun]], an Imu, held the throne of [[pol:1710|Binge]] from 4326, aged twelve, and outlived the merchant by a year. Piemkathrelen was thirteen when Binge attacked the Kingdom of Ayshi in 4348, and twenty-four when that war ended in defeat: by the Treaty of Nilithna in 4359 Binge paid Ayshi an indemnity and accepted a fifteen-year truce. The anonymous panegyric sung for Livdun's forty-third year (PAL·PO·00044) made the payment a virtue: "a peace of mercy, bought with tuthshi, not with sons."

Four years later, at twenty-eight, he performs the first act the record gives him: the [[evt:41837|Founding of the House of Riek]] at Zhiyiyzhikhti in 4363. Its goods were horses, olive oil and grain; its partners were the towns of Rurin, Tinlamvukhnir, [[set:147|Binge]], Muethka and Puunganga.

In the [[evt:41882|Binge–Biniynge War]] (4365–4369) the empire's generals beat larger armies three times near Yiranunyirin. On the stele set up there for the last of those battles (PAL·IN·03139), Livdun claimed: "He killed 94,300 soldiers." The record puts Biniynge's dead at 16,689, out of 71,022 men in the field. The [[evt:42015|Holy War of Binge against Tiniziwikhizim]] (4378–4382) won Binge eighteen districts and three towns. Between the survey of the 4330s and that of the 4380s, Binge grew from 172 districts to 234, its army from 59,286 men to 161,699, and its town-dwellers from 727,225 to 1,281,497.

## What the prices say

Both ledger years fell in lulls, 4386 four years after the holy war ended and 4392 three years after the [[evt:42148|Binge–Ayxi War]] ended without a settlement. Neither book carries a margin note. A rival's does: in 4381 Pimtibumen of the House of Umuy, at Shiyithtinnge, wrote that "The roads are unsafe because of the Holy War of Binge against Tiniziwikhizim" (PAL·LE·03010). Piemkathrelen's books are all figures.

The book of 4386 opens with a balance of 4,000 [[cur:956|Tuthshi]], a silver coin of 6.3 g that the record shows unchanged from 4283 to the empire's end. The nine entries price grain at Zhiyiyzhikhti and Rurin at 1.27 to 1.37 Tuthshi the measure, olive oil at Rurin, Binge and Tinlamvukhnir at 1.05 to 1.13 the jar, and horses at Rurin and Muethka at 32.7 to 38.1 a head. A horse at Rurin cost about thirty measures of grain. Muethka then lay in the Kingdom of Ban, beyond the empire's border.

In 4392 the balance opened at 10,450, about 2.6 times as much, and every price in the ten entries was below any for the same goods in 4386: grain 1.10 to 1.15, oil 0.83 to 0.96, horses 29.1 to 32.0. Among them are oil and horses priced at Yiranunyirin, by then in the Kingdom of Biniynge, the town near which Livdun's steles stood.

It is tempting to make him a war profiteer, selling horses to a swelling army. The record does not say who bought his horses, and the two books do not bear the story out: between them his balance rose while horses grew cheaper. They show a man who traded beyond the empire's borders, in a coin that held its weight, and wrote down nothing but the price.

## The house that outlived the empire

Piemkathrelen died a natural death in 4395, aged sixty, in the year the [[evt:42237|Binge–Nivange War]] began. He lived to see a grandson, Ulupiemken, *brave eagle*, born in 4388. His son [[per:41779|Shienen]], born when Piemkathrelen was twenty, took the house at forty and held it until his death in 4418; then Ulupiemken took it. Ulupiemken's ledger of 4436 (PAL·LE·02999) lies in the same library and opens in the same words, "Journal of the house of Ruyiku, kept at Zhiyiyzhikhti by Ulupuyimkin". It carries a margin note of a kind his grandfather never wrote: "Insurance on goods doubled for the Ninth Iith Trade War."

The house passed from father to son through seven heads; the sixth, a descendant in the fifth generation, was called Piemushiefen, *eagle glory*. The house ended in 4566, the year its seventh head, Luekhvavsheyen, died, thirteen years after the Binge Empire collapsed. Of the sixteen houses and banks the record shows founded in that empire, none lasted longer. The House of Umuy, founded in 4375, ended in 4385, when its founder died. Of the 2,505 houses, banks, companies and corporations the record knows down to my own day, only 29 have lasted two centuries.

The record does not say why the [[hou:2051|House of Riek]] endured, but it shows two things that may bear on it. No battle, siege or sack is set at or near Zhiyiyzhikhti between AY 4001 and AY 4690, and the record's counts put the town at about 45,000 people in 4300 and 81,000 in 4400. And the family kept itself. In 4399, four years after his death, the record counts the district of Zhiyiyzhikhti as Imu, Livdun's people; it counts every head of the house as Jinru, down to the last.

Livdun's two steles at Yiranunyirin (the other is PAL·IN·03138) multiplied his enemies' dead by three and by almost six. His merchant had no one to impress; his sums are the only words of his that survive. I trust them more.
