title: The River Will
---
His father's back was the widest thing the boy knew, and in the evenings he walked on it.

That was the custom of the house. His father lay face down on the mat by the hearth with his forehead on his folded arms, and the boy stepped up onto him the way you step into a boat, one foot and then the other, and walked from the waist to the shoulders and back while the knots shifted and gave under his heels. His father groaned into his arms like a man being thanked. Across his forehead, where the carrying strap sat all day, ran a groove worn as smooth as the lip of an old jar. The boy had put his finger in it when he was very small. It was the first thing he could remember.

His father carried for the house of [[dei:28|Khothaze]]. He carried grain up from the boats at the landing to the god's storehouses, one sack at a time, the strap across his brow and the load riding high on a folded cloth between his shoulders, and at the storehouse door a scribe pressed a mark into wet clay for every sack. He could not read the marks and had no need to. At the end of the day he said his number before the scribe had finished counting, and the scribe counted, and nodded, and gave him a sideways look, as if a porter who could count were a dog that could sing. Some of that grain was the god's and some was the great king's. His father had carried the bread of [[per:4169|Nagovo]] on his back for half his life and never once seen his face.

"Higher," his father said. "There. Stay there."

The boy stayed there, on the knot under the left shoulder blade, and rocked on it. His mother, Nathe, was breaking the bread. His little sister was asleep on the fleeces with her mouth open.

"The boatmen say there's a sickness at [[set:25|Kavo]]," his father said into his arms.

"Kavo is a long way off," said his mother.

"That's what I told them." He breathed out under the boy's feet. "They say the faces go white. The lips and all, white as flour. They say the dead are lying in the doorways."

"Boatmen," said his mother.

"Boatmen," his father agreed, and that was the end of it. The grain stood thick in the fields outside the wall that summer. The men had come home from the [[evt:3909|Battle of Shyih]], the god's storehouses were full, and in ten days the reapers would go out and his father would carry the harvest in, and come home at night with his legs shaking, and lie down on the mat, and say: *Walk.*

* * *

Nobody saw it come into [[set:5|Nelo]]. There was no day on which it arrived; there was only a day on which everyone understood that it had been there for some time.

The boats from upriver stopped first. Then the scribe at the storehouse door was not at the door, and a younger one sat in his place who did not know the porters' names, and then no one sat there. The reapers did not go out. From the roof of the house the boy could see over the wall to the fields, and the grain stood in them, yellow and then paler, and leaned, and began to drop its own seed, and the birds came down on it in clouds that rose and settled and rose again, and no one cut it.

Afterwards it had a name, the [[evt:3876|White Pestilence]], and people spoke of it as if it had been one thing. In the lane it was not one thing. It was Najege the potter, whose wheel stood in his yard with a pot dried hard on it, half pulled up, the shape of something that had been going to be a jar. Then it was Najege's wife, Nayenje. Then the dyer's two girls, and the old man who sat by the well and corrected everybody.

The men who dug graves beyond the gate dug for some days, and then there were too few of them, and then there were none. The dead were rolled in their sleeping mats and laid outside their doors for the diggers, the way you put a jar out to be filled. The diggers did not come. The heat came, and the dogs. Soon the lane smelled so that people tied cloths over their faces and still did not go out, and the boy's mother kept the door shut and the little one inside, and stopped the crack under the door with a fleece.

His father stood at the shutter most of a morning, looking at Najege's mat. Then he went to the peg and took down the strap.

"No," said his mother.

"He made our jars."

"You'll bring it home to us."

"Then I won't come home." He fitted the strap over his forehead, settled it in the groove, took it off again and coiled it. "I'll sleep at the landing. Bring me bread in the mornings. Leave it on the mooring stone and go back. Don't come close."

"And take him where? The ground's full."

"The river," his father said. "The river doesn't get tired. It goes down to the sea, and [[dei:29|Bombe]] takes what's brought to him."

The boy watched through the gap in the shutter. His father rolled Najege's mat tight, bound it at the head and the feet with cord, passed the strap round the bundle and knelt with his back to it. Then he stood up the way he stood up under a sack, all at once, from the legs, and went down the lane bent forward, with short steps, as he had gone down to the boats every morning of the boy's life.

* * *

Every morning the boy took the bread down to the landing and set it on the mooring stone and went back as far as the end of the reed fence, and from there he watched.

His father came down the lane with someone on his back and went down the mud of the bank and into the river until the water was at his waist. There he turned and slid the strap free and let the bundle down into the water, and held it, and folded the mat back from the face. He cupped the river in his two hands and poured it over the face, and said the name. Then he let go. The current took them slowly, turning them like a boat being poled off, down past the last houses and the boat sheds toward the mouth, where the river opened into the sea.

He said the names loud enough to carry. Najege. Nayenje. Navande, whose name meant harvest, who had sold onions at the gate. Nakheje the boatman, whose name meant lion and who had been afraid of dogs. Nangathando. Nakange. The dyer's two girls, whose names the boy had never known and his father did.

The boy counted. He had learned to count from his father, on the joints of his fingers, and he counted on them now, and when his fingers were used up he began again and kept the full hands in his head.

"Don't count them," his father said one morning from the river. He had not turned round. "I can see you doing it."

"You count the sacks."

"They aren't sacks." He poured the water. "Say their names. If you have to do something, do that."

On another morning an old priest came down from the house of Khothaze and stood at the top of the bank in his robe, with a cloth over his mouth, and watched for a long time without speaking. When he did speak, he spoke the way priests speak, as if the words had been handed to him in order and he were handing them on in the same order.

"Many gods share the world, each with their place and their due. The dead have their place. They go into the ground, and their children pour water for them, and they are not thirsty. Who will pour water for these?"

His father was standing in the river with a woman on his back. He looked up at the priest for a while.

"The river will," he said.

The priest said nothing. He stood there until the woman had gone down past the boat sheds. Then he sat on the bank and took off his sandals.

After that he came every day. He stood at the edge of the water with his robe hitched up and said what is said for the dead while the boy's father poured, and when he had said it he said what the [[mov:4|Khopande]] teach, that all things turn on the great wheel and what ends will begin again. The boy, at the reed fence, did not believe it. He said the names instead, under his breath, after his father, the way the priest said his prayers after whoever had taught him.

* * *

At night his father slept under the boat sheds, and the boy lay on the roof and looked toward the place where the sheds were. [[dei:31|Kombe]] came up over the river and laid her road down the middle of it, white. He could not see his father, but he could see the road the dead went down.

"Does your back hurt?" he called one morning, from the fence.

His father laughed, which he had not done since it began. "My back's the same as ever. It's the only thing that is."

The boy came closer, a step a day, and his father let him. By the end he stood at the mooring stone itself, near enough to see that yesterday's bread had not been eaten.

The last one was Nabezhshe, who lived across the lane and had thrown pebbles at him all summer from her roof and hit him every time. She was light. His father carried her without the strap, in his arms, as he had carried the boy's sister on the day she was born, and he did not wade out far, and he poured the water very gently, and said her name. Then he came up out of the river and sat down on the bank with his back to the boy and his hands hanging between his knees.

The priest looked at him, and then at the boy.

"Go home," said his father, without turning.

The boy did not go home. He went round in front of him and looked, and it was true: the lips, and the skin about the mouth, white as flour, as the boatmen had said on the evening his father laughed at them.

"Don't carry anybody," his father said. "You aren't big enough. Promise me."

The boy promised.

His father looked up the lane, where people still lay in front of their doors, and then down at the river. He got up, slowly, from the legs. He took the strap from his shoulder, where it had hung all these days, and gave it to the boy to hold. Then he went down the bank without a load, for the first time, and lay down at the edge of the water, at the place where he had stood all those mornings, with the river running over his feet.

The boy sat beside him. The priest sat a little way off and said no prayers, and the boy was grateful.

Late in the night his father said, "How many?"

The boy did not understand him at first. Then he did. It was what his father said at the storehouse door at the end of a day, before the scribe had finished. The boy had the number. He did not say it. He began instead to say the names, from the first: Najege. Nayenje. Navande. Nakheje. He said them all, in order, the dyer's girls and the old man by the well and the woman the priest had watched go down past the boat sheds, and Nabezhshe last; and his father's breathing went along under the names like a man walking behind a load, and somewhere among them it stopped keeping step.

* * *

In the morning [[dei:30|Pogazho]] put her first light on the fields where the grain still stood.

The boy did not carry his father. He had promised. He took him under the arms and drew him down the bank into the water a little at a time, the way his father had shown him to ease a full sack off a boat, and once the river had him his father weighed nothing at all.

The boy stood in the river up to his chest and held him. There was no mat to fold back. He cupped the river in his two hands and poured it over the face, over the groove in the forehead, smooth as the lip of an old jar, and the priest on the bank began the words for the dead, and then stopped, and let him.

Then he said his father's name. He said it as his father had said all the others, clearly, over the water, so that whoever waited where the river met the sea would know who was coming.

Then he opened his hands.

**Author's note.** The man at the river is one sentence in the record, written half a century later by his grandson, a scribe of the house of Khothaze, on the Tablet of the Breaking of Nelo (PAL·CH·08441, AY 851): "My father's father carried the dead to the river on his back, and then he lay down and died." It gives him no name. The record says the White Pestilence began at Kavo in AY 798, reached thirteen towns and killed about 459,933 people across Nelo and Lothe; it does not say when it reached the city.
