title: King Stone
---
Taadha had washed him twice, once as the priests required and once as she wished, and after that there was nothing left for her hands to do, so they held the stone.

It had hung on a cord round his neck for thirty-two years: a seal of green stone no longer than the top joint of a thumb, cut on its face with a running horse and, over the horse, the moon, which was [[dei:138|Tiwil]], his lady. She had untied it to wash him. Afterwards she found she could not tie it back, because his neck was cold, and the stone, kept against her palm, was the only warm thing in the room apart from the fire. So she sat on the felt beside the bier and held it, and listened to the wind going round the shutters looking for a way in.

The king's house in [[set:284|Nimxi]] smelled as every Tinim house smells, of horses and dung smoke and sour milk, and today of juniper as well, which the priests of [[mov:21|Küniwmü]] had kept burning over him all night for [[dei:137|Xiydadtli]], who would be coming to take him down.

Her mother had nursed him. That was the whole of their kinship, and for fifty-seven years it had been enough. He had drunk at the same breast as she had, a season behind her, and when they were small he had trailed after her along the horse lines like a foal after a mare. Then he had become [[per:27430|Kaalwixa]], king, and she had become the woman who kept his linen and his dead mother's keys; and still, when nobody was near enough to hear, he called her *sister*, and told her to go to bed, as if he were the elder.

The rains had begun to fail the year after he was made king. Nobody said so to his face; everybody remembered it. [[dei:136|Diwmno]] no longer drove his storms over the mountains, the high grazing went as thin as an old man's hair, and every year since, it seemed to Taadha, the herds had been smaller and the wars larger. This was the fourth time in her life that the gates of the city had been shut against an army. Twice before, the walls had held and the kingdom had come out smaller, thirteen districts signed away the first time and four the second, and each time the king had come home from the swearing and slept a day and a night through. Last year the walls had not held. From the door of his house you could still see the black roofs.

This year the queen of the [[pol:1177|Witti]] had come herself. She was younger than the youngest of his sons. He had ridden out to meet her at the [[evt:28209|Battle of Nimxi]], and he had come back through the south gate lying across his own saddle, with his men walking beside the horse to hold him on. Since then the Witti had sat down all round the plain. At night, from the roof, their fires were so many that the plain looked like a second city, a larger one, with this one shut inside it.

It had hardly been a battle, the men told her afterwards, as if that ought to help.

* * *

The priest who came for her was one of the young ones, and he would not look at the bier.

— The Witti have sent a scribe, he said. The peace is being written in the hall. They want the king's seal.

— The king is here.

— They want the seal, aunt.

She did not tie it back on him. She carried it through the house shut in her fist, past the inner room where the king's wife sat among her women with the door closed, past the yard where the horses that were left stood with their heads down in the dust, and into the hall.

The hall was full of hard grey light and of men who had not slept. Three priests sat along one side of the king's table, and at the head of it stood his chair with nobody in it. Four Witti soldiers waited just inside the door, keeping their hands well away from their swords, and at the foot of the table, with a board across her knees and a pot of ink on the bench beside her, sat a woman.

She was near fifty, broad in the shoulder, grey at the temples, with ink worked into the creases of every knuckle. When she spoke, Taadha heard the town in her mouth. That was the thing she would remember after the rest had gone: the enemy's scribe talked like the women at the cisterns. The people of the town had always called it Nimshi, as the Witti did, and spoken the Witti tongue, near enough, at their stalls and their weddings and their gravesides; only the king's people, the Tinim, said Nimxi, with a sound in the middle of it the town had never learned to make. Since before Taadha was born the town had been ruled in one language and had haggled and married and buried its dead in another, and now the other one had come up the road with an army.

— That's the seal? the scribe said.

— It is the king's.

— Good. Sit, before you fall down.

Taadha did not sit. The scribe shrugged and read out what she had written so far, slowly, so that the old priest could follow. She had begun as the Witti begin things, in the name of their own faith and then of the king's: "In the name of Kievi and of Kuneumu." *Kuneumu*, she said, as if the word had been left out in the rain. Then:

— "Peace is granted by Milamaf, queen of Iethithi, to…" She lifted the pen. That's the place. To whom?

— To Nimxi, said the eldest priest.

— Peace goes to a man, the scribe said. A man swears it and a man keeps it. A city has no hand to put on the altar.

— To his son, then. To Maxi Iiw.

— Is he king?

Nobody answered. The young priest looked at the empty chair. At last the old one said that the son would be made king when the father had gone down to Xiydadtli as a king should, and that this could not be done in a city with the Witti sitting on every road out of it.

— Then he isn't king yet, said the scribe, not unkindly. And I can't make him one with a pen. That's above me. She looked at Taadha's fist. Whose seal did you say that was?

— The king's.

— His name.

— Kaalwixa.

The scribe dipped her pen.

— He's dead, said the young priest, as though somebody ought to say it.

— I know he is. I counted him. She was writing as she spoke, and did not look up. Twenty-one thousand eight hundred and eleven of ours went out on to that field, and forty-three of ours didn't come back. Fourteen hundred and twenty-four of yours, and eighty-two. And him. I wrote it all out for her by lamplight the same night; it's what I'm for. My queen grants peace to the king of Nimshi. He was king of Nimshi on the day she beat him, and nobody has been king of it since. She told me to write what you would swear to, and to be back before dark.

She turned the board round so that Taadha could see. Taadha could not read, not the Witti letters nor the king's own, but she could see where the pen had been: a little knot of strokes, all leaning the wrong way, in the place that had been left empty.

— Read it to me.

— Kaaliwisha.

— Kaalwixa.

The scribe tried. It came out of her soft and flat, the way the town said it, the way the town had said it in the market for thirty-two years when it thought the Tinim were not listening. The Witti had put a small vowel into the middle of him, like a pebble in a boot.

— It won't matter, the scribe said. Everyone who ever reads this will say it the way I do.

— It means king stone, in our tongue. His mother wanted a son who would stay where he was put.

The scribe looked for a while at the green stone in Taadha's hand.

— Did he?

— Always. Taadha heard her own voice go strange. Ask your queen.

— I don't need to, said the scribe. I was there.

She read the sentence through then, the way it would stand: "Peace is granted by Milamaf, queen of Iethithi, to Kaaliwisha of Nimshi, on these conditions." Nobody in the hall said anything against it, so she went on, and her pen went on a little ahead of her voice: "Nimshi cedes to Iethithi the land of Yimla, 3 districts in all."

— We are standing in Yimla, Taadha said.

— Three districts, said the scribe. The grazing up under the mountain, and the low pasture east and south of the walls. Not the walls. Not this one. Not the field where he fell, either, if that is anything to you.

Taadha thought of the summer pastures under the snow line, where she had ridden as a girl with the king's mother's herds and a boy who would not stop following her. The city kept its walls. It always did. Every peace she had lived through had left the walls standing, and each time there was less outside them for the walls to keep. A herdsman's king, and they were leaving him a city with no grass.

"There shall be a truce of 15 years, and raiders of either side shall be handed over for judgement."

Fifteen years. She would be seventy-two, if the gods were careless.

The scribe wrote the place of swearing and a year Taadha had never heard of, two thousand nine hundred and sixty-four, counted from the founding of a city she would never see, and under it: "Each party keeps a copy sealed with the seal of the other." Then she wrote the whole of it out a second time, quicker, on a second skin. When it was done she warmed a cake of wax at the brazier until it went dark and soft, worked it on to the cords at the foot of the first copy, the one that would ride back to the Witti, and held out her hand for the stone.

Taadha put the stone into the old priest's hands instead. They shook so that the scribe took his wrist in her inky fingers and steadied it, and between them, the queen's woman and the king's priest, they pressed the king down into the wax.

When they lifted the stone the horse was there, running, and the moon over it.

— The other copy she seals at the gate tomorrow, said the scribe, when she swears. That one stays with you.

She wrote one more line at the foot of each skin, small. Taadha asked what it said.

— That I wrote it. "Witnessed and written by Yi Iji."

— And what is that?

— Three Water. The scribe blew on the ink. In a dry country everybody is named for water.

Taadha, whose own name had rain in it, said nothing. The scribe gave her back the stone. It was hot from the wax.

* * *

The juniper had burned down while she was gone. She laid a fresh branch on the coals and sat down on the felt beside him.

— They've given you peace, she told him in Tinim. Fifteen years of it. You'll keep it better than any of them.

Somewhere beyond the walls the scribe would be riding back towards the fires with the skins rolled in a case under her arm, and in the wax of one of them the horse and the moon: the first of him to leave the city since they had carried him in. It would go to the Witti and be kept there, she supposed, in some cold room, and every reader it ever had would call him Kaaliwisha.

She opened his hand, which had stiffened and then softened again in the days he had lain there, and put the stone into it, and closed his fingers round it, and closed her own round his. The stone was still warm. She held on while it cooled, until it was as cold as he was, and then a while longer, because there was nobody left now to tell her to go to bed.

**Author's note.** The record calls this the [[evt:28210|Fourth Siege of Nimxi]] and ends the [[evt:28184|Iethithi–Nimxi War]] with the [[evt:28211|Treaty of Nimxi]]: three districts of Yimla ceded, a fifteen-year truce.
