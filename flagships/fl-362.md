*[A single parchment leaf, folded twice; the address is written on the outer fold. The hand is a practised commercial script.]*

To the elders who now keep the city of [[set:248|Tivinnu]], and to all who sit in judgement in the market-hall, a dealer in grain and oil of the lower quay sends greeting, and asks a hearing.

I do not write as an orator, but as a man who has weighed silver every day of his life and knows its heft as a shepherd knows the cry of his own ewes. Read to the end, and then judge whether a citizen does wrong to complain.

You know, all of you, what was done to the [[cur:507|Ihal]] two years ago. In the hard year, when the treasury of the old kingdom of [[pol:944|Tivinnu]] was drained by the [[evt:22110|Tenth Mikhukhizhi Trade War]], the mint took the coin in hand and made it lighter. This was the [[evt:22129|Debasement of the Ihal]]. A piece that once held its full weight of silver was struck again to hold only two parts in three. The face was the same; the name was the same; the silver was not. A third part of every coin was carried off, and no herald cried it in the streets.

Consider what followed, for it is the nature of such things to follow. The farmer who brings barley to the quay is not a fool. He bites the coin, he weighs it in his palm, and he asks more of it. Where a measure of grain once went for a coin and a third, it now goes for nearly two. The baker pays more; the porter's wage does not rise; the widow's jar stands empty. And the man of the quay, who stands between farmer and baker, is cursed by both, though the fault is neither his nor theirs.

I am told that this was a necessity of war. I do not deny that the war was hard. I lost a nephew in the year of the [[evt:22156|Battle of Lishimti]], where our general fell and the field was lost. I rejoiced with the whole city when our men held the field at the [[evt:22170|Battle of Kivunge]]. But I ask you: was it the light coin that held the field at Kivunge? It was the arms of citizens. And those same citizens, coming home, were paid in silver that had been shaved by their own magistrates.

Our fathers, too, knew defeat. They knew it at the [[evt:21777|First Battle of Zhaalämiviyi]] and again at the [[evt:21872|Battle of Zhaalämiviyi]], and they paid for it in blood and fields. Yet they did not pay for it by cheating the scale. A city may lose a battle and remain honest. It cannot lose its honesty and remain a city.

Now the old kingdom is gone. We have all seen the [[evt:22174|Collapse of the Tivinnu State]]; we have seen Kibunge go its own road in the [[evt:22176|Rise of Kibunge]], and we have seen this city stand up again under its old name in the [[evt:22175|Rise of Tivinnu]]. A new house is being built. I beg you not to build it on a false foundation.

The teachers of [[mov:25|Miyituyishiu]] say that the gods ask justice in dealings and moderation in all things. What is a coin but a promise of justice in dealings, stamped so that every man may read it? The wise, they tell us, withdraw from wealth and appetite. Very well: but the treasury that thins the silver is not withdrawing from appetite. It is feeding it on the poor.

Therefore I ask three things. First, that a new coin be struck of honest weight, and that its weight be proclaimed at the gates. Second, that the light Ihal be taken back at its true value, by weight, and not forced on the quay at its old value by edict. Third, that the scales of the market-hall be tested before witnesses at each new moon.

Do this, and the farmers will come back to the quay, and Tivinnu will again be named in the markets as it was in our fathers' days: a city whose word is its weight.

Written in the year 404 from the founding of Tivinnu, at the lower quay, by one who has signed his name to many bills and here withholds it, lest his complaint be judged by his name and not by its justice.
