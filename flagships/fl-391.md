I set down what follows in the 13th year of [[per:28245|Nitllo Tlü]], queen of [[pol:1205|Owa]], one year after the troubles, while the memory is still warm and before it is smoothed over by those who would prefer that it had not happened. I do not write to inflame. A man who writes in anger writes only for his own generation; I write for those who will come after, that they may weigh the matter with a cooler hand than mine.

Let me begin with what is not in dispute. In the last year there came the [[evt:28823|Persecution of the Ya'cuam in Owa]]. By the word of the crown, the shrines of the [[mov:1|Yakam]] were shut, their priests forbidden to pour, and their festivals struck from the calendar of the city. All this was done in the name of [[mov:58|Vexefimekh]], which teaches that there is one divine power, and that the gods our fathers honoured are but its servants or its shadows.

I ask the reader to consider that teaching for a moment, for there is a strange thing in it. If our gods are the servants of the one power, then to honour them is to honour their master, as a man who greets the steward greets the house. And if they are only shadows, then a wise faith has no more reason to fear them than a grown man has to fear the shadow of a tree. In neither case does it follow that their altars must be broken. Yet broken they were. I leave it to the philosophers to explain how a doctrine of compassion and of release from suffering came to inflict so much of the latter.

Here is what I saw. In the spring the officers of the crown came to the shrine of my quarter in [[set:153|Owa]]. They came with a writ and with soldiers, but the soldiers were the worse part, for the writ could not read and the soldiers could not be reasoned with. They carried out the bronze vessels and weighed them in the street, for the metal was to go to the treasury. They pulled down the painted screens. An old priest who had served there since before I was born stood in the doorway and would not move, and they moved him. He lived three days after.

Then came the fines. Every household known to keep the old rites was assessed. Ours was set at twenty [[cur:603|Xixli]], which is no small sum for a family of weavers; neighbours poorer than we were sold their looms to pay. Those who would go before the priests of the new faith and declare themselves converted were forgiven the fine. Many went. I do not blame them. A man's conscience is his own, but his children's bread is not.

I did not go. My mother hid the household images in the grain store, beneath the barley, and on the nights of the old festivals we poured a little water in the dark, without lamps, without singing. This is how the gods of [[mov:1|Yakam]] were honoured in my house that year: in silence, like thieves.

There were worse things in the country districts of Ayshi, which I will not set down, because I did not see them myself, and an honest witness does not repeat what he has only heard.

Now, what of the queen? Many of my people curse her name. I will not. I have stood in the crowd when she rode through the city, and she did not look like a cruel woman. It is said that her counsellors and the priests of the new faith pressed her hard, that they told her a realm with two altars is a realm with two hearts. That may be so. But a ruler is answerable for what is done under her seal, and posterity will say: in her reign it was done.

To those who come after, I say this. A city is not made stronger by forcing men to lie about what they believe. It is made full of liars. The shrine of my quarter is a granary now. The bronze is coin. The old priest is under the earth. And yet in a hundred houses of [[pol:1205|Owa]], in the dark, without lamps, the water is still poured.
