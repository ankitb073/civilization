*To the Honourable Wardens of the Mint of [[pol:1761|Nesh]], at [[set:52|Eämew]], from the counting-house of Aatlege by the river stairs: greeting, and a request for patience with a long letter.*

Honoured Sirs,

You have asked me, through your clerk, to set down in writing what I am doing in my house, since the talk of the quays has reached your table in a form that I suspect does me little credit. I am glad of it: a thing done in the open may be judged; a thing whispered about is already condemned.

First, the circumstance. When the [[evt:41935|First Coinage of Nesh]] was proclaimed last year in the name of our young king, [[per:41401|Omafmamshel]], and the [[cur:971|letlmesh]] began to be struck in place of the old shima, every merchant in the city did what merchants do when the coin changes: he weighed what he had, hid what he could, and trusted no one. A cloth-dealer who wished to buy dye at the river towns must carry his silver along roads where it was not safe to carry a loaf; a widow who had sold her husband's boat must keep the price under her floor and lie awake over it.

My remedy, which I have practised since the spring, has two parts.

The first is the deposit. A merchant brings his coin to my house. It is counted before witnesses, entered in my book under his name, and locked in my strongroom. He receives a note under my seal, stating the sum. That note he may keep, or he may write upon its back, "Pay to such a one," and hand it to his creditor, who may bring it to me and receive the coin, or may in his turn write upon it and pass it on. I have seen a single note pay three debts in one afternoon while the silver it stood for never left my cellar.

The second is the bill. A merchant of Eämew who owes money in a river town pays the sum into my house. I write to a correspondent of mine in that town, a person of known credit, instructing her to pay the bearer the like sum. My correspondent, for her part, receives payments from the merchants of her town who owe money in Eämew, and writes to me in the same manner. Each season we compare books, and only the difference need travel by road. The silver stays home; the robbers go hungry.

I anticipate three objections, and I answer them in order.

It will be said that the depositor is at my mercy if I fail. So is every master at his steward's. I therefore propose that my books be open to your inspection on any day you choose, and that I hold at all times coin to the full amount of the notes outstanding, save what I am expressly permitted to lend.

It will be said by some of the clergy that a note is a promise, and a promise is not silver. I reply that a promise kept is better than silver stolen. The teachers of the [[mov:108|Todeye]] hold that the good life is one of compassion and release from suffering. I have released more than one widow from a sleepless floor.

It will be said, lastly, that money is the King's, and that I usurp his prerogative. I strike no coin. My notes promise his coin, and only his coin; they can do nothing but increase the credit of the letlmesh, since every one of them says, in effect, that the King's silver is worth waiting for.

I ask, therefore, not for a monopoly, which I should think unjust, but for a register: that whoever keeps deposits or draws bills in Nesh be enrolled with your office and show their books. Let the honest be known by their openness. I will be the first to enrol.

I am told that others now claim to have devised these practices before me. I make no quarrel with them; let them show their books with dates, as I can show mine.

Your servant in all things lawful,

[[per:41522|Aatlege]]

*Given at Eämew, 27 Tse 1262 of the Todeye.*

*Postscript.* Grain stood this morning at one and four-fifths letlmesh the measure, and the price has been steadier since the notes began to pass than at any time since the new coin. I offer the observation without claiming the cause.
