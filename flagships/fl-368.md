**To the most excellent council of the treasury, and through it to the king, [[per:22898|Ikhil Mukha]], whom [[dei:88|Mahtu]] keep; from the master of the mint in [[set:52|Ihaemiw]]; in the year 2656 from the founding of Ihaemiw.**

I set down here, as the council has asked, the reasons for the new weight of the [[cur:491|If]], the manner in which it has been struck, and the dangers which a prudent man must foresee in it. I write plainly. A mint master who flatters his masters is like a balance with a thumb upon it: it tells the truth to no one, and least of all to the man who owns it.

**I. Of the causes**

The council will not need reminding of the years just past. In the [[evt:23072|Third Movosh–Ihamiw War]] our arms did not prosper. The field before the walls of this city was lost, and the field at Ashmath after it, where the king himself commanded and could not hold; and at the [[evt:23138|Treaty of Izimue]] the kingdom of [[pol:911|Ihaemiw]] put its seal to terms it had not chosen. I do not speak of these things to wound. I speak of them because a treasury, unlike a poet, cannot forget. The silver paid out under that treaty did not return. The walls that were repaired after the siege were not repaired with words.

Now, in the third year of the war against Kizhyitli, the army must again be paid, fed, and shod. The soldier does not take his wage in promises; and if he did, the baker would not.

**II. Of what has been done**

By the king's order, given in council this spring, the If is now struck at a lighter weight. Where the old coin carried seven and some six-tenths of our small weights of fine silver, the new carries something less than six and three-quarters. The loss to each coin is somewhat under an eighth part. Put otherwise: every hundred of the old coins, melted and struck again, now yields one hundred and thirteen of the new, and a little over.

The face of the coin is unchanged. The sun-disc of Mahtu stands upon it as before, and the king's name runs round the rim as before. Only the hand that weighs it will know the difference; and I fear many hands will learn to weigh.

**III. Of the dangers**

I will not hide from the council what every money-changer in the lower market already knows. The price of grain stands near one If and nine-tenths the measure. When the merchants understand the new weight, and they will understand it within a season, they will ask more Ifs for the same measure, and the gain to the treasury will be eaten by the price of bread. This is not wickedness in the merchants. It is arithmetic, which serves no king.

Further: men will hoard the old coin and spend the new, for it is their nature to keep what is better and pay out what is worse.

I add one reflection of my own. A coin is a small oath. Each time the city strikes one, it swears that this is what it claims to be. When the oath is lightened, it is still an oath; but men remember that it was lightened.

**IV. Of what I recommend**

First, that the [[evt:23210|Debasement of the If]] be made once and not again; a single loss is borne, a habit of loss is not.

Second, that the soldiers' pay be counted in the new coin openly, so that no captain may say he was cheated in secret.

Third, that the priests of [[mov:13|Unhatlmum]] be asked to give thanks at the house of [[dei:86|Shu]] for the harvest, and to pray at the house of [[dei:85|Niwi]] that the river rise well this year; for if the barley is plentiful, the price will hold, and the new coin will pass without murmur.

Fourth, that the old dies be broken in the presence of two councillors, lest any be tempted to strike in the old weight and sell the difference.

So much I submit, in duty and in order, as the gods give each man his place. Let [[dei:83|Hath]] of the sky witness that I have written what I know.

*Sealed with the seal of the mint.*
