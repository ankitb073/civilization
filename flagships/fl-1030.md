title: Six Thousand Seven Hundred
---
The scribe had brought the words on a strip of paper folded into eight, and by the time it reached the mason the fold had worn a white line through the middle of the number.

'Six thousand seven hundred,' the scribe said, and held the paper flat against the stone with his thumb. 'That's a seven under the crease. Not a one.'

Luesh'a'faya looked at the paper and then at the stone. Twenty men and an ox had stood it up that morning, a slab of pale limestone a head taller than himself, socketed in rammed earth on the slope below the walls of [[set:283|Khasnin]], a long bowshot out. There were other stones on the slope already, old ones, leaning a little, the way old men lean. Up on the walls the Imshi watched, small as starlings. They had watched for six days, while the [[pol:1397|Ayshi]] army lay on the plain and buried its own dead, which were not many, and the general decided whether there was anything more to be had from a town that would not come out a second time.

'He's going home,' the scribe said. 'The stone stays. You have four days.'

Luesh'a'faya read the paper through, moving his lips. He could read. A man who cuts letters he cannot read cuts them wrong, his father had said, and had made him learn.

> Nislo'li'an, great king, king of Ayshi, defeated Imshi in Khasnin. He killed 6,700 soldiers. He erected this stele for Nili, his lady.

Under that the scribe had written the prayer, which was on every king's stone Luesh'a'faya had ever seen and which he could have cut in his sleep.

He had watched the battle from the wagons. He had seen [[per:33881|Ie'i Faya]] ride up and down in front of the line all morning on a grey horse, and he had seen the Imshi give way at last and go back up the road to their gate, and he had not seen the king, and neither had anyone else. On the second day the Imshi had asked for their dead, and the general had let the women of Khasnin come down with carts. Luesh'a'faya had watched them load. He had not counted. He was a mason; he counted courses and blocks and the days until he would be paid. But he had a mason's eye for a heap, and he knew what six thousand of anything looked like, and that had not been it.

'Where does the number come from?' he said.

The scribe looked at him as if he had asked where the wind came from.

'It's a king's number,' he said. 'It isn't for counting with.'

* * *

She came on the third evening, while he was cutting the six.

He heard her before he saw her, the dry grass hushing against her skirts: a woman of fifty or so, in a head-cloth, a water jar on her hip, coming down the slope from the gate as if the slope were hers and there were no army on it. She stopped a little way off and looked at the stone and not at him.

'What does it say?'

He told her. He read it out the way you read to people who cannot read, slowly, with a finger under each word, and when he came to the number his finger stopped on the six he had just finished and the bare stone where the seven would go.

'Six thousand seven hundred,' she said.

'That's what it says.'

'We didn't bury a fifth of that.' She shifted the jar. 'I know what we buried. I washed them in the temple yard with the other women. It took two days.'

He said nothing. There was nothing in his trade for it.

'My son's in there,' she said, with her chin at the stone. 'Nilo Iji. Five times over, by your count.'

'It isn't my count.'

'You're the one holding the chisel.'

She walked along the row of old stones then, and he followed her, because it was easier than standing beside the number. She knew them the way people know the trees on their own land. That one was Yi Li, a king of Imshi long ago, black stone, and he had killed ten thousand. That one was a queen, Ta'ath, and she had killed seven thousand six hundred. That one was from somewhere else altogether, in letters nobody in Khasnin could read, and no doubt it had killed a great many people too.

'Everyone who comes to Khasnin kills a great many people,' she said. 'And the town is still here.'

He bent to Yi Li's stone. The basalt was so old the letters had gone soft at the edges, like the crust of a loaf, but he could read it, and at the bottom he read the prayer aloud, because his mouth was already the shape of it:

"May Nili destroy the name of the enemy."

'Nili,' the woman said. 'There isn't even a river here.'

He laughed before he could stop himself; *nili* was only the word for a river, and the lady of it had a long way to come to Khasnin. The woman looked at him properly for the first time. She did not laugh. She looked at the chisel in his hand and the half-cut number on the white stone, and then she set the jar down in the grass at his feet, full, and walked back up the slope to the gate with nothing on her hip.

He finished the number by lamplight. He cut the prayer last, the way you always did, so that it would be the cleanest line on the stone and the first to take the morning. Then he drank her water.

* * *

They came back the next year with more than twice as many men.

The engineers marched in the rear with the ladders, two men to a ladder, and Luesh'a'faya's other end was carried by a boy called Kaüli'an, seventeen, who had never been further from home than the next market and who talked the whole way. He had heard about the stone. Everyone had. It was the kind of thing that got told at fires, and the wind off the steppe was cold enough that year, as it had been every year of Luesh'a'faya's grown life, for a great deal to get told at fires.

'Is it true? Six thousand seven hundred?'

'It's what it says.'

'Then they can't have many left.' The boy worked it out on his fingers, frowning, as if [[pol:1398|Imshi]] were a jar of beans. 'Can they?'

But the talk at the fires was that the Imshi had found more men too, and that their king had come himself: [[per:33945|Ma'shi Mish]], thirty-three years old, who had been king two years, since his mother the old queen fell fighting our own king in the [[evt:34435|Battle of Ash]]. A man does not forget a thing like that, the old soldiers said, and spat, and turned their meat.

On the last night before Khasnin the general walked the engineers' lines. He stopped where Luesh'a'faya was greasing the ladder pegs.

'You're the one who cut the king's stone.'

'Yes, lord.'

'Did it come out well?'

Luesh'a'faya considered the question honestly, because it was about his work. 'It's good stone, lord. The king's name is very deep.'

[[per:33881|Ie'i Faya]] was quiet for a moment. He had fought on this plain three times. He had won here twice, and the old queen had beaten him here once, five years ago, so badly that the men who had been there still did not talk about it. His own name was on none of the stones.

'Keep your chisels sharp,' he said. 'We'll want another.'

* * *

Luesh'a'faya never saw the walls that day.

The engineers waited behind the line with the ladders laid flat in the grass, the way they had been told, for the moment when the field would be won and the walls would be the next thing. From where he crouched he could see nothing but backs and dust and, once, a long way off, a banner going over. Khasnin was horse country. That was all anybody said afterwards, as if it explained it: that they had come out of the dust on horses, more of them than anyone had thought there were horses, and that the line had bent, and held, and bent, and then was not a line.

He lost Kaüli'an in the first rush back. He lost the ladder. He lost his tools, all but the one chisel in his belt, and he ran with the rest, back the way they had come, until a horseman came up beside him at an easy trot, looked down at him, saw a man of forty with a chisel and no sword, and pointed with his crossbow at the ground. Luesh'a'faya sat down in the grass. It seemed the only sensible thing.

The [[pol:1397|Ayshi]] lost seven thousand five hundred and fifty-five men at Khasnin that day, and the Imshi one thousand five hundred and seventy-one. Nobody on the plain knew either number yet. They knew only what they could see, and they could see enough.

* * *

The Imshi kept their prisoners on the slope below the gate, because it was open ground and the walls looked down on all of it, and so he slept that night among the stones. He did not need to look for his. He could see its pale edge against the dark whenever he opened his eyes.

By day they dug. The Imshi gave them mattocks and spades and walked them out onto the plain, and they dug for their own dead from first light until they could no longer see the edges of the pits. On the second day Luesh'a'faya stopped looking in the faces for Kaüli'an, because he had begun to hope not to find him and did not like the feeling. On the third day he stopped trying to judge the heaps. He knew what six thousand of anything looked like. That was the trouble. In the evenings an Imshi priest came out to the pits and said the [[mov:69|Kievi]] words over the day's work, the words about the wheel and what begins again, and they were the words the priests said in [[set:27|Tu'shi]], to the letter. The guards spoke of the counters at the gate, of tallies kept and tallies lost, of numbers that grew each time somebody said them; he did not need their numbers. He had dug.

On the fourth evening the woman came down from the gate with the water carriers, a jar on her hip. She was greyer, or perhaps it was the dust. She went along the line of sitting men filling their cups, and when she came to him she stopped, and he saw her know him.

'The mason,' she said.

'Yes.'

She filled his cup. She did not hurry. Behind her the stone stood in the last light with its letters full of shadow, the king's name very deep, and the number, and under the number the prayer he had cut last of all.

'Now it's true,' she said.

He looked where she was looking. He killed six thousand seven hundred soldiers. He had read it a hundred times as a lie about her people. It read differently from where he sat, with the earth of the pits still under his nails. It was not a lie any more. It had only been early, and about the wrong men.

He got up. The guard by the gate watched him go to the stone and did not stir; one Ayshi more or less among the stones was nothing to him, and the stone was not his king's. Luesh'a'faya put his hand flat on the prayer. Every king's stone said it. He had cut it with his eyes half shut. He thought of the women washing the dead in the temple yard last year, and of the pits on the plain, and of Kaüli'an counting on his fingers, and he could not think whose name it was that the lady of a river that was not there should be asked to destroy, or how she was to tell one from another, when they all spoke the same words and were all lying in the same ground.

He had one chisel. He found the place by feel, the way you find the grain in a stone, high in the last register where the bed of the limestone ran, and set the edge there, and took a fist-sized rock from the grass for a mallet.

'You'll break your own stone,' the woman said.

'It's the king's stone.'

'You cut it.'

'I cut it.'

It took three blows. On the third the crack ran out sideways along the bed, quick and clean as a thread pulled from cloth, and the face below it came away in his hands, the whole of the prayer in one flat piece, and the stone stood just as it had stood, with a raw pale scar at the foot of the writing.

He turned the piece over so the letters were against his chest. Then he looked up at the number.

'Leave that,' the woman said.

He waited.

'Leave the number,' she said. 'Let them read it when they come again.'

He left it. In the morning he carried the broken piece out to the plain with the spade and the mattock, and put it face down in the bottom of the pit they were filling that day, under all of them, where nobody would read it, not the king, not the river, not anyone.

**Author's note.** The stone is [[per:33916|Nislo'li'an]]'s limestone stele for the [[evt:34450|Battle of Khasnin]] of AY 3659 (PAL·IN·02622), excavated at Khasnin in AY 4882 with the older stones of Yi Li and Ta'ath (PAL·EX·00281, PAL·IN·02081, PAL·IN·02174). A fracture runs through its final register and the last lines are lost; the record does not say what they asked, or who broke them. His rock-cut stele for the victory of 3661 (PAL·IN·02623) survives complete. Khasnin was never sacked. The Ayshi came back in 3662 and 3664, and lost both times.
