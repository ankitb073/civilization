*Lecture delivered by Chairman [[per:47103|Nazhigi]] at the opening of the new reading-rooms of the State Archive in [[set:85|Nambi]], 18 Khidha 2527 of the Vimikhulu. Printed by order for distribution to schools.*

Comrades, scholars, and young people of [[pol:1638|Khishkhih]],

I have been asked to open these rooms, and I shall do it in the way most useful to you: by telling you why they matter.

Some forty-five centuries ago, in the age of clay and bronze, the world suffered what our historians call the Great Collapse. In the space of six hundred and sixty years, a great state broke apart fourteen times over, and from the wreckage rose sixty-seven lesser ones. It began with the [[evt:1589|Collapse of the Yida State]], which fell into six. It continued with the [[evt:2822|Collapse of the Talenonoy State]], the [[evt:4038|Collapse of the Navnga State]] and the [[evt:4077|Collapse of the Tallin State]]. Nelo broke, and broke again; the [[evt:4130|Collapse of the Nelo State]] was not its last. Egu, Luthe, Khie'a and Bäni followed. The [[evt:5441|Collapse of the Mii State]] scattered six successors across the plain; the [[evt:6753|Collapse of the I'ith State]] scattered six more. And the list our historians give ends with an old name heard once more: the [[evt:6958|Collapse of the Tallin State]], into six.

Why? Our scholars have argued it for a century. Famine, they say. Usurpers. Too many wars. All true. But I put it to you that there is a cause beneath these causes, and it is in these rooms.

The ancient states did not know the truth about themselves.

Consider. For the [[evt:548|First Navnga–Vuvo War]], one ancient chronicle tells us that Vuvo began it, that Navnga won fourteen districts, and lost two hundred and thirty-eight men. A second chronicle tells us there was no battle at all, only an honourable peace, and that no land changed hands. Each claims to be the true account. Our archivists, setting the old boundary-stones against the tax-rolls, find two battles, both won by Navnga, which began the war; eight districts taken; and the Navngan dead more than five hundred. Each chronicler told the story his masters wished to hear.

Consider the [[evt:1110|Talenonoy–Shue'ethie War]]. One tablet says Talenonoy lost fewer than two thousand men and slew eighteen thousand. Another says it was a stalemate that cost a few villages. The registers show six battles, three won by each side, and Talenonoy, in the end, the victor, with six districts. Consider the [[evt:1416|Second Virnado–Talenonoy War]]: one account claims forty-five districts won, another a stalemate; the stones show nineteen.

A state that lies to itself about its battles will lie to itself about its granaries, and about its taxes, and about the loyalty of its provinces. Then one morning it wakes in six pieces, and every piece blames the others.

The faith of the [[mov:70|Vimikhulu]] teaches that all things turn on a great wheel, and what ends will begin again. Some of our young people take this to mean that collapse must come round again, as winter comes. I say no. The wheel turns; but the faith also teaches duty, order and right sacrifice, and a people that does its duty may set its shoulder to the wheel. The faith teaches that teachers are honoured but no one owns the truth. Very well: then the truth must be kept in public, in rooms like these, where any citizen may come and check the State's figures against the State's records. That is what we open today.

And I will tell you, since you have been patient, that we are not the ancients. When the enemy came against us at Dambüzhi and at Dadunde, our armies did not break. They broke him, and by the [[evt:48363|Treaty of Dadunde]] his state is now our vassal. This very year, by the [[evt:48386|Founding of the Colony of Yindu]], our flag stands across the sea. A people that knows itself can do such things. A people that lies to itself cannot.

So read, young people. Read the old tablets, and learn how to catch a liar. Then read our own records, and learn that you will not need to.

Thank you.
