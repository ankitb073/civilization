title: The Thirty-Sixth Year
---
The stone came up the far side of the canal on rollers behind four yoke of oxen, and it took the men of [[pol:291|Tantlotl]] most of a day to stand it upright on the rise where the barley had been. It was black. Even from the gate Yi Tlue'e could see that much: a black slab taller than the men who steadied it with ropes, so that when they let go and stepped back it seemed to stand there by its own will, facing the city.

"Basalt," said Yawni Lieze. "There's none this side of the river. They've dragged it a long way."

"For us."

"For their god. We're only what it's pointed at."

They had no business on the wall. The wall was for spearmen and slingers, and the two of them were palace men, with clay under their nails and nothing in their hands but the heel of the morning's bread, which neither of them had eaten yet. But nobody sent them down. Since the queen had come back from Yinishma, nobody sent anybody anywhere. The spearmen sat with their backs to the parapet and slept when they could.

Under Yi's elbow, set into the face of the gate, was the slab he had watched the masons cut seven years before, in the year he turned twenty and was made historian of the palace. The signs were still sharp. He did not need to lean out to read them.

"[[per:10917|Tlamfen Mish]], queen of [[pol:462|Yisamashtsi]], built this gate in [[set:31|Waga]]. May [[dei:0|Etlie]] bless the city."

The stone on the rise had nothing on it yet. In the morning the cutters came out from the tents with their bronze points and their bags of sand, squatted at its foot, and began.

* * *

Every day after that there was more of it, and every day it was no more readable from the wall than the tracks of birds on a mudbank. Yi went up anyway. A sentence you cannot read is still a sentence. It has a shape, and a length, and an end, and he watched the end come down the stone toward the grass.

In the city, grain that had cost a [[cur:237|Shone]] and a half the measure at the start of the year now went for whatever silver a man could put in the pan of the scale, and spearmen stood by the scales in the market to see that it went at all. Then the horses went: the chariot horses, the city's first, bought only last year and trained at such cost to stand still in the noise. The city ate them one at a time and did not talk about it.

Last year, after the battle under the walls, the men of Tantlotl had come in and taken what they wanted, and they had passed through the tablet house too. They had looked along the shelves, and gone out again carrying the lamp stands. There is nothing in a tablet that a soldier can carry home and sell. Yi had found that a comfort at the time.

He worked at night. There was no reason to; nobody would ask for this year's annal while the year was still happening to them. But the lamp in the tablet house was the last the palace still filled, and he had the habit of the work the way other men have the habit of drink.

The shelves went up into the dark. On the highest, in baskets gone soft with age, were the old man's tablets. A'in'ash had been historian of the palace when Yi was a boy carrying water to the clay pit: a long, coughing man who wrote standing up, and who in all the years Yi knew him never once put the name of the new kingdom at the foot of a tablet. When the old state of the kings of Waga came apart and the queen's father took the seat, and the land took the name it bore now, A'in'ash went on dating his work by the years of Waga as if nothing had happened at all. Yi had copied his master's colophons until his hand knew them without him:

"Thus recorded by A'in'ash, scribe of the palace of Waga, in the 110th year of Waga."

"The hundred and tenth year," he had said, the first time he understood it. He was twelve. "There's been no Waga since before I was born."

"There's been no king of Waga," the old man said. "That is a different thing." He coughed for a while into his sleeve. "A king's years stop when he stops. A kingdom's years stop when the last man who keeps them puts down his stylus. You've heard the priests of [[mov:0|Le'im]]. Everything turns on the wheel. What ends will begin again. And when it begins again it will want to know how old it is. Somebody has to have kept count."

"And the king lets you?"

"The king has better things to do than read the bottoms of tablets." And he laughed, and coughed, and laughed.

Tonight Yi had this year's tablet in front of him, damp under its cloth. He had written the year, the fifteenth of [[per:10917|Tlamfen Mish]], and the place, Yinishma, where the queen had gone out a second time to meet the army of Tantlotl and come back with fewer than she took.

Yawni came in out of the dark with a cloak over his head and sat on the lid of the clay bin.

"Seventy-three," he said. "I went round the barracks again. Nine hundred and sixty-eight went out with her. Seventy-three didn't come back."

"And theirs?"

"How would I count theirs?"

Yi looked at the wet clay. There was a way the old man had written years like this one, and he had copied that too until his hand knew it, and it came into his fingers now, wanting to be pressed: "because the river rose and cut the army in two, our army withdrew in good order." It had a sound like a door closed softly so as not to wake a child. It was not, he thought, quite a lie. They had withdrawn. Most of them.

He wrote nothing. He laid the cloth back over the tablet and left the line empty, the way a house leaves a place at the table.

* * *

On the last night the queen came down to the tablet house. She had one woman with her, carrying a lamp, and she sent the woman to wait by the door.

Yi had seen her close perhaps five times in his life. She was thirty-seven, two years older than the kingdom, and thin now like everyone; her face had the look of a field after the water has been let off it. She stood by the long table and touched its edge.

"You were the old man's boy."

"I carried his water, lady."

"He let me sit under this table when I was small. He gave me clay to keep me quiet. I made dogs." She almost smiled. "He said it was only proper, for a Nine Dog."

Yi reached up into the dark of the top shelf, between two of the soft baskets, where for as long as he could remember there had stood a small lopsided animal of fired clay with three legs and the stump of a fourth. He had always supposed it was a pig. He put it in her hand.

She looked at it a long time.

"Tomorrow," she said, "he comes in through my gate, and I swear, and he swears. And after that, what will you write at the bottom of your tablets?"

"Whatever they tell me. The fifth year of Yalni Imin, I suppose."

"On theirs."

He did not answer.

"And on yours?"

"Not yours, lady," he said. "Forgive me. A king's years stop when the king stops."

"I'm thirty-seven. I may live another forty years."

"I hope you do. But not as queen of anything, and I can't count a queen who isn't one." He heard his master in his own mouth and was ashamed, and went on anyway. "I'll write the kingdom's year. The thirty-sixth year of Yisamashtsi. And next year, the thirty-seventh."

"There is no kingdom after tomorrow."

"There was no Waga after you were two," Yi said. "He wrote the hundred and tenth year of Waga with you under his table, making dogs."

She laughed then, once, a sound so unlike a queen's that the woman at the door turned her head. Then the queen looked up at the shelves going away into the dark, the baskets of her father's years and the old man's, and of all the kings before them whose names Yi had copied as a boy for practice, and she said, not to him, "Who will now remember the kings of old?"

He had no answer, and she did not wait for one. At the door she turned back and set the clay dog on the end of the nearest shelf, among the tablets.

"It's safer here," she said. "They never take anything from this room."

* * *

In the morning the great king of [[pol:291|Tantlotl]] came in through the queen's gate, under her name, with spearmen before him and scribes behind, and in her father's hall the queen and the king swore, and [[pol:462|Yisamashtsi]] and everything in it passed into the hands of Tantlotl. Yi did not see it. Nobody he knew saw it. What the city saw was strange spearmen on the walls and the gates standing open, and by evening, for the first time in many days, somebody selling bread.

In the afternoon a man of Tantlotl who could read came to the tablet house with two spearmen behind him. He looked along the shelves the way the soldiers had looked along them the year before, and found the same nothing. His name was Asnide, and he told them so, as if it might help. He had a little tablet of his own, and on it, in signs Yi could read and words he could only half follow, the way the year was to be written from now on. He said it aloud to be sure they had it. He said the city's name the way his people said it, with a cough in the middle: Wakha.

When he had gone, Yawni said, "Come and read their stone."

They went out through the gate. Nobody stopped them. The rise was trampled to dust and the barley stubble was gone under it, and the black stone stood in the middle of the trampling with the morning still cold on its face. Close to, the signs were their own signs, the same signs as on the queen's gate, cut deep into a stranger's stone in a stranger's tongue. Yi read them aloud the way the old man had taught him, one sign at a time, his finger in the grooves, guessing at the words where their tongue left his.

"Yalni Imin, great king, king of Tanchethi, defeated Yisamashchi in Wakha."

"Yisamashchi," said Yawni.

"That's how they say it."

"He killed 900 soldiers."

"Two hundred and ninety-seven," said Yawni at once, as if answering a roll. "I wrote the levy. 'Each man to bring bread for ten days.' I counted them out through that gate and I counted them in again. Two hundred and ninety-seven."

Yi's finger went on down the stone.

"He erected this stele for Nili, his lord. May Nili destroy the name of the enemy."

He stood with his hand on it. The basalt was cold and very hard, and it had been cut by men who would be paid for cutting it, and it would stand there, he thought, long after anyone who had been counted out through the gate or in again.

"The name," he said. "Not the city. Not the people. The name."

Yawni studied the last line for a while with his head on one side, the way he looked at a column of figures that would not come out.

"Then their god's been given the wrong one," he said. "Let him destroy Yisamashchi. Nobody ever lived there."

* * *

That night Yi wrote for the new masters, lists and receipts, the stores of the palace counted over for men who would take them away, and at the foot of each tablet he put the fifth year of Yalni Imin, neatly, the way Asnide had shown him. They would be read by men who could read, and they would be correct.

Then he took the cloth off his own tablet. The line for Yinishma was still empty. He left it so. Below it, where the old man would have put his name and his year, he pressed the signs slowly, giving each its full depth: so it is written in the annals of Yi Tlue'e, scribe of the palace of Yisamashtsi, in the thirty-sixth year of Yisamashtsi.

Twenty years on, in a city that belonged to Tantlotl, he would set the whole war down again, and fill the empty line at last, and end his account with the queen's question, because he had never found an answer to it. Under the question he would put the year, which was the nearest thing to an answer that he had: the fifty-sixth year of Yisamashtsi.

But that was later. Tonight it was the thirty-sixth, and the clay was soft, and outside, over the black stone on the rise, [[dei:4|Lashla'e]] was up and lighting it for anyone who cared to read it. Yi set his tablet on the shelf to dry, beside the queen's dog, and blew out the lamp.
