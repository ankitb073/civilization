title: The Historian Called Eight Death: Oi Lyizi and the War Books of Ane

The name [[per:53795|Oi Lyizi]] means *Eight Death*. A novelist would think it too neat for a man who spent his life counting his country's war dead. The record gives the name plainly, and little else. A Lizo, born in AY 4700, he was named historian of [[pol:1656|Ane]] in 4720, at twenty, and held the title until his natural death in 4779, at seventy-nine. The record names no parents, wife or children. For his first seven years an older historian, Iinitluie, shared the title; whether as master and pupil, the record does not say. No document of his time mentions him except his own seven books.

All are chronicles of war, kept in the state archive of Ane. They cover Pivitye (written 4729), Onitie (4740), the [[evt:45590|Second Ane–Fiw War]] (4746), the [[evt:45793|Tsey–Ane War]] (4752), the [[evt:45860|First Wemu–Ane War]] (4756), the [[evt:45867|Ane Colonial War in Daadüdhih]] (4762) and the [[evt:46046|First Holy War of Ane against Fyinan]] (4776). I quote them in translation. Readers from Khishkhis, like me, usually meet him in the Daadüdhih book, where our country is the enemy. I have tried to read the other six with the same suspicion, and the same sympathy.

## A president without a name

Oi Lyizi gave the office its proper title and kept the person out. The Fiw war begins because "Our president could not suffer the insult" (PAL·CH·08005). Against Onitie, "So the president went [...] punish him" (PAL·CH·08029). Against Fyinan, "So the president went out to punish him" (PAL·CH·08063). Ane had been a federation since 4702, led by presidents elected for five years, and each sentence had a holder: Oi Tluie in 4715, Ipil Tlyi in 4731, Yi Luisha in 4765. None is named. The office takes offence and goes out like a king of the old annals, and Fiw, Pivitye and Onitie are "joined to the realm". The Treaty of Likhtli of 4743 names "the Republic of Tiye and the Republic of Ane" and is signed for Ane by President [[per:45134|Isyi Tlyi]] (PAL·TR·02825); the Tsey book says only that Tiye "came to beg for peace".

His country changed under him. By the end of the 4700s Ane counted some 33.3 million people and an army of 390,392 men; by the end of the 4780s, 51.2 million people and 989,839 men. The steam engine came to Ane in 4748, and in 4758 the paper [[cur:1038|Withe]] gave way to the Muie. Yet his books keep the shape of the old annal: a date reckoned in the era of the cult of [[mov:25|Mie'to'ieshio]], an insult, a battle, a flank turned at noon, thanks given in the houses of the cult.

On the insult his own archive contradicts him most directly. The Daadüdhih book opens: "there came word that Navavun of Khishkhizi had gathered an army against Ane. The gods commanded that the wrong be avenged." Ane's printed Assembly proceedings record the motion "That this Assembly approves the declaration of war against Khishkhizi". It passed 291 to 206 after the head of government, Shimap, assured members: "We seek no territory, only security." (PAL·PD·00016). In Nambi the paper said, "Hostilities have been opened by Ana" (PAL·NP·00337). The record agrees with the paper. Ane was the aggressor in five of his seven wars, and in his pages all five begin with the enemy's offence.

## The arithmetic of victory

At Pivithie in 4724, he writes, "Ie Tlyi had gathered 1,064 men there"; "145 of them lay dead on the field, and of ours only 5." The record gives Pivitye 380 men against Ane's 177,241, with 48 Pivitye dead and 15 of Ane's. Ane's muster roll for that battle, written by the scribe Isyi Mof and held in the same archive, totals 177,241 and ends, "The horses to be counted again at the river." (PAL·MU·01548). None of his seven books gives the size of Ane's own army.

Faamni in 4750 was the largest battle in the record between 4700 and 4779. Wemu brought 418,592 men and Ane 418,319. Wemu lost 117,619 men and its commander Xyimaf; Ane lost 29,519. Oi Lyizi credits the enemy with 1,023,973 men and writes that "415,474 of them lay dead on the field, and of ours only 10,333". On paper, he has killed nearly the whole army Wemu fielded.

Across his four great wars, against Tsey, Wemu, Khishkhis and Fyinan, his books add up to about 189,000 dead of Ane and nearly 2.8 million of the enemy. The record counts about 527,000 and 844,000. In Daadüdhih, where the record makes the losses almost even (218,602 for Ane, 215,809 for Khishkhis), he cut Ane's dead to a third and more than doubled ours. Land grew the same way: Pivitye yielded one district, and he wrote two; Fiw yielded seven, and he wrote seventeen. Of Fyinan he wrote, "The land which they had stolen was returned to Ane, 116 districts in all." The treaty text says 49 districts (PAL·TR·02833), and the empire had never held any of them. They had been Tsey's until 4761, when Fyinan was born from Tsey's collapse. His rivals did the same in mirror image. The Wemu historian says Faamni "cost us 32,879 men, and them 89,495" (PAL·CH·08041).

## He left out no battle

What redeems him a little: the record knows thirty engagements in his seven wars, and he omits none. Six of the seven historians on the other side whose accounts survive pass over at least one. Nadhash's Khishkhis account (PAL·CH·08046) drops three of nine, all Ane victories at Nzhivi.

He recorded seven defeats, but gave them their own grammar. They happen "through the failure of the supply trains", "through the blunders of the general staff", "when the allies failed to come", or "after a night of rain that bogged the guns". His armies withdraw "in good order" or give ground, and their generals vanish. Oi Toe, born the same year as the historian, is named in six victories; at Itlishithyi in 4746 and Faamni in 4747, which he commanded and lost, no general is named. At Itlishithyi an Ane army more than twice Wemu's size lost 115,670 men to Wemu's 42,519; the chronicle gives 40,488 against 150,193. One beaten commander does keep his name: Khozhin at Lunge in 4741, "whose name shall be remembered", because he died there. Pyienyime, killed at Itlishithyi in 4752 in a battle Ane won, leads the troops in his entry but does not die. A death in victory, it seems, spoiled the count.

## What the ground remembers

He did leave out the burnings. The record holds sacks of Fiukh in 4716 and Pivithie in 4724. His books have only gates that fell or "could hold out no longer". Nambili's trenches found both: at Fiukh, "burnt timbers and scattered shot, dated to AY 4716" (PAL·EX·00043); at Pivithie, "destruction debris with traces of a great fire, dated to AY 4724" (PAL·EX·00047). In the Fiw book, written twenty-nine years after the war, a gap has eaten the one word that mattered: "1,868 [...] lay dead on the field, and of ours only 184." The record has 603.

Nor does the Daadüdhih book have room for the people whose ground it was fought over. Three years before it appeared, Ane-speaking subjects of Khishkhis in Tatuthivi printed an open letter in the capital: "We have no figures for our own dead, for no one counted them" (PAL·LT·01767). He did not count them either.

Yet the man who shrank Ane's dead also kept what nobody else did. Closing his Tsey book, he copied one line from the Treaty of Lunge of 4288: "In the name of Hinimu and of Veshethmekh." The treaty is otherwise lost; its eight words have survived 464 years only because he quoted them (PAL·TR·02570).

His last surviving book was "Set down for the instruction of later days" in 4776. He died in 4779, the year the [[evt:46153|Ane–Nemeth War]] ended, and its Ane account was left to his successor, Yithinyime. In 4791 Wemu sacked [[set:7|Ane]]. The archives did not burn, the record notes; three of his books were catalogued after the fire. He was not a liar of the omitting kind but of the arithmetic kind, the kind later readers can catch. Because he left every battle in, we can lay his numbers beside the muster rolls, the treaties and the ash at Pivithie, and see how a state wished to remember itself. It is not the instruction he intended, but it is a real one.
