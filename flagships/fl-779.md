**5 Vavu 2414.** Turned out the cupboard under the stairs looking for the good buttonhook and found instead my old apprentice book, the blue one, from the year of the [[evt:46966|Khanji Revolution]]. I was fifteen. The ink has gone brown. Half of it is patterns and prices; the other half is shouting in the street. I will copy some of it here, since the times seem to want it.

*From the blue book, 2398:*

> *Mistress says no work today, the shop shutters are up, the square is full of people from the mills. Somebody has painted on the wall of the Ministry: REMEMBER KHAMBIZHINU. I asked Mistress what it meant. She said the old war, that we lost, and the [[evt:46606|Treaty of Khambizhinu]] that gave the provinces away, and that the Emperor's people never said sorry for it. Bread is up again.*

> *Second day. Soldiers at the end of our street but they did not fire. A boy gave one of them a flower and the soldier put it in his cap. Mistress cried. We sewed armbands all night, red and white, three hundred of them, for the committee. She did not charge them.*

> *Fourth day. They say the palace has signed. They say there will be a parliament, and a paper called a constitution, and that the crown will be a King's crown now and not an Emperor's, and must ask before it spends. Everyone in the street kissing. I was kissed by a coal-man and had to wash my face twice.*

**6 Vavu 2414.** Reading the blue book again in bed. How sure we were. We sewed three hundred armbands for a paper.

To be fair to the paper, it held a long time. It held through the Khishkhis war, though not much else did; through the [[evt:47125|Treaty of Shudhunvidhi]], when they put the indemnity on us and every newspaper in [[set:71|Lamlivje]] printed the sum in black borders. It held through the bread queues after. My husband, who reads the debates, says the parliament argued about everything and agreed on nothing, which he says is what a parliament is for.

Then last year the [[evt:47187|Usurpation of Namvave]]. I do not understand it and nor does anybody in this street. One king went and another came, not by the proper line, and the newspapers were careful for a week and then very careful. My husband says the constitution still stands. He says it the way you say a sick man is still breathing.

**7 Vavu 2414.** Work. Three wedding dresses for the season and one mourning coat. The bride's mother complains of the price of silk; I said it is the war debt, everything is the war debt, and she laughed and said then the war debt can pay for her daughter's sleeves. Bread is steady at least. Grain in the newspaper today at half a [[cur:1111|Shunjih]] the measure, which my mother would have called cheap, but wages are not what they were.

On the tram home I saw someone had chalked on the wall by the Assembly: REMEMBER 2398. The paint was fresh. A policeman was standing in front of it, not rubbing it out, not doing anything, only standing. I do not know if he was guarding it or hiding it.

Tonight [[per:46238|Namvave]]'s portrait went up in the window of the draper's on the corner, framed in gold. Last month it was not there. I looked at it a long time. He has a narrow, careful face. My daughter asked if he was a good king. I said I would tell her when I knew.

I have put the blue book in my sewing box, under the tissue paper, with the last red-and-white armband I kept. If they come to ask, it is patterns and prices.
