title: The Water Is Good

Across the lane, in the felt-maker's house, someone had been coughing since the lamps went out, and A'menide was counting.

It was a habit of the road. On the road you counted because there was nothing else to hold on to: paces from one cairn to the next, days to the ferry, mouthfuls left in the skin. Now she lay on the sleeping-bench in her sister's house in [[set:139|Wathshi]], with her niece's hot feet pushed against her shins and rain ticking on the eaves, and counted coughs the way she had once counted paces. Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine. Then a silence so long that she began, against her judgment, to hope. Then a cough dragged up from the bottom of a chest, and a woman's voice saying a man's name, softly, as though she were asking him a question. Forty.

It was the third wet season since the rains came back. For more than twenty years before that the sky over Wathshi had given next to nothing. The wells went down a rope's length, and then another. The priests of [[mov:1|Ya'kam]] burned barley for [[dei:8|Nish'il]] until the smell of scorched grain was the smell of every feast day, and children grew as tall as a door-latch without once seeing the lanes run with water. Aselo was one of those children. She was nine. Then the rains returned in earnest, and the people of Wathshi stood in the lanes with their faces turned up and their mouths open like nestlings, and the timber-yards smelled of wet bark again, and grain grew cheaper. And now, in the forty-first year of [[per:11809|Ine]], there was the cough.

People had begun to call it the weeping cough, because of the eyes. It started like any cough of the wet months, a tickle and then a bark, and on the second or third day the eyes began to run and did not stop. A'menide had seen a man she knew, a big man who carted stone, sitting on his own doorstep with tears pouring down his face and nothing in the face at all but tiredness. Some got up again after ten days. Some did not.

She turned toward the wall. On the shelf above the grain jars, where her sister kept the few things in the house worth keeping, the tablet lay in its cloth. She could not see it in the dark. She did not need to.

* * *

In the morning the caravan yard behind the old warehouses was loud with oxen. The merchant house that built those warehouses had shut its gates five years before, and someone had chiselled its mark off the gatepost, but the carters still used the yard, because there was nowhere else in Wathshi wide enough to turn a wagon.

Mim'ede was lashing beams onto the lead cart. Twelve years ago he had driven her baggage as far as [[set:22|Wal'e]], and complained about it every day of the way, and she had liked him ever since.

"Envoy," he said, and bowed with the rope still in his fists. It was half a bow, the one he always gave her, as if he were keeping the other half back in case she asked for more.

"You're going to Wal'e."

"Beams for the queen's people. They've nothing down there to build with but mud." He grinned at her. "A poor place of mud houses."

"I wrote what was there."

"They'd have your ears for it." He turned his face into his elbow and coughed, and came up laughing. "Dust," he said. "Twenty years of it in my chest, and now the rain's washing it loose."

She looked at his eyes. The rims were red, from the cold or the yard, but they were dry.

In the corner of the yard a money-changer sat cross-legged behind his balance. A man from the wagons watched him lay silver in one pan and small polished stones in the other until the beam hung level: one [[cur:273|Yish]], and another. The man's eyes were running. He wiped them with the back of his wrist, without interest, the way you wipe off sweat.

"Wait ten days," A'menide said.

Mim'ede stopped with the rope half through the knot.

"Wait ten days and see who in this yard is still coughing. Then go."

"The silver's weighed, envoy. The beams are promised." He pulled the knot tight. "And it's here, the cough. Not there. The further I get from Wathshi, the better I'll like it."

She opened her mouth to tell him what she knew about roads, and found she did not know how to say it. That a road was not a way out of a place. That a road was the place's arm, and a town reached along it with whatever it held in its hands: timber, copper, the king's word, the king's envoys, and anything else it happened to be holding.

At the gate a priest was feeding the brazier of the little sky-shrine, and the carters coming in stopped one by one to throw a pinch of grain on the coals. The smoke went straight up into the wet air.

"The wheel turns," the priest told each of them. "What ends will begin again. The dry has ended."

He did not say what had begun. He did not have to.

* * *

Aselo wanted the cities.

She asked every afternoon while her mother was at the loom, and A'menide had long since stopped pretending to refuse. She took the tablet down and unwrapped it on her knee. It was her own copy, the one she had pressed by lamplight on the night she came home, while the clean one went on to the king's scribes at [[set:152|Tanchethi]]. The clay had gone pale, the colour of a dry riverbed. One edge was rubbed soft from being held.

"I, A'menide," she read, with her finger under the signs, "set out from Wathshi in the 29th year of Ine."

"Sent by the king to the far countries," said Aselo, who could not read a sign of it and knew every word. "These are the cities I saw."

Wal'e came first. A'menide read it and did not need to. She could see the place better than the room she sat in: the low mud walls, the old men who still talked about the siege twenty years gone as if it had been last season, and the well. She had walked ten days out of a Wathshi where the water was handed round by the cup, and at Wal'e a woman had let a bucket down on a rope and brought it up full and cold, and A'menide had drunk three cups standing up, and wept, and the woman had laughed at her, not unkindly. That night she pressed into the clay the line that was still the truest thing on the tablet.

"The water is good," said Aselo.

"The water is good."

They went on. [[set:104|Iethithi]], where the same gods were honoured. [[set:205|Pivish]], a small town. Aselo bounced on the bench, because Pivish had her favourite line.

"Beware the money-changers!"

"Beware the money-changers," A'menide agreed.

"What did they do?"

"Weighed my silver with a thumb on the beam."

"Did you hit him?"

"I wrote him down."

Aselo thought about this, and seemed to find it worse. "Why didn't you write the people? The woman at the well?"

"The king didn't want the people. He wanted to know who ruled, and which gods were kept, and what grain cost, and where the water was good."

"Why?"

"So that whoever went next would know."

Aselo frowned at the tablet, as if whoever went next might be written on it somewhere. "Who went next?"

A'menide looked toward the doorway. The light was going grey, the rain had started again on the eaves, and somewhere past the eaves the road went down toward Wal'e. Before she could answer, the child coughed: a small dry cough, a nothing. A'menide put the back of her hand to the girl's forehead, and left it there longer than she meant to.

* * *

By dark Aselo's eyes were running.

Wil'ak sat on the bench with the child's head in her lap and stroked the hair back from her temples, over and over, as she used to stroke A'menide's when they were girls and A'menide had a fever. A'menide fed the hearth. She put a pinch of barley on the coals for [[dei:7|Yof]], and a drop of oil, and said the hearth-words their mother had said, and the fire took the offering with a little hiss and gave back nothing.

"She's crying," Wil'ak said.

"It's the cough. She isn't crying. Look at her face."

"I know what crying is." Her sister's own face was wet. "I know what it is."

Across the lane the felt-maker's house had gone quiet. A'menide had not counted that silence. She did not want to know its number.

"The priest says the wheel turns," Wil'ak said after a while. "What ends begins again."

"He said it to me too."

"Then this will end. It has to."

"It will end," A'menide said. "Everything ends."

She did not say the rest of it. She lay down beside the two of them and listened to the child breathe, and said the cities over to herself in the order she had walked them, the way she had said them a thousand nights since: Wal'e, Iethithi, Pivish, Namos'ash with its ferry, Achishithyi around its well, [[set:127|Los]] where the water was good, [[set:122|Ameyith'i]] where the road was paved. She had said the list so often that it had worn smooth, like a prayer. Tonight it would not stay what it had been, a list of places she had seen. It kept turning over in her mouth into a list of places that would see.

* * *

The carts went out by the Wal'e road at first light.

Aselo was asleep when A'menide left, breathing through her mouth, her lashes stuck together. Wil'ak had not slept at all. Neither of them said anything at the door.

Mim'ede was on the lead cart with the goad across his knees. His eyes were streaming. He saw her coming and wiped them with his sleeve before she could speak.

"Smoke from the shrine," he said.

She walked beside the oxen down the lane, past the last houses, past the sky-shrine where the brazier was out and steaming in the rain, to the place where the paving gave way to mud. She could tell him to turn back. She had told him already, and he had heard her, and here he was. He had silver weighed and beams promised and a wife somewhere who needed both, and a cough that was dust, only dust.

So she did what an envoy does. She gave him the road.

"At the second cairn the left-hand track is shorter, but there's no water on it until evening. Drink from your own jar. Don't share the cup. Sleep upwind of the others." She put her hand on the rail of the cart. "At Wal'e the water is good."

"I know." He was laughing, and his eyes were running. "You wrote it."

He lifted the goad. The oxen leaned into the yoke, and the wheels came unstuck from the mud with a sound like a mouth opening, and the carts went one after another down the road between the dripping oaks until the bend took them.

* * *

At home she took the tablet down again.

She wet her thumb in the water jar and held it above the first line. The clay was old and hard, but she knew its surface would still soften under a wet thumb and slide. She could rub out Wal'e. She could rub out all of them, every city, every price and god and well, and press the clay smooth, and it would be as if she had never gone anywhere and seen anything and told anyone the way.

She put her thumb down instead on the blank edge beside the first line, and held it there until the clay went slick, and lifted it. The whorl it left was the only mark on the tablet that said nothing at all.

"Do the cities," said Aselo from the bench. Her voice was a thread.

So A'menide did the cities. She read from the beginning, with her finger under the signs, and the child said the words with her, and when they came to Wal'e they said it together.

"The water is good," said Aselo. Her eyes were streaming. She was not crying.

Outside it was raining on the Wal'e road. Half a day gone, A'menide counted. Nine and a half to go.

**Author's note.** A'menide's tablet (PAL·TV·00012) was dug up at Wathshi in AY 4892; the catalogue calls it complete, with minor losses, and I have imagined one of them. The drought over Wathshi (AY 1608–1630) never reached Wal'e or Los; that, I think, is why she wrote that the water there was good. The cough began at Wathshi in AY 1633, and the record names the places it reached: five of the seven cities on her road. It does not say how. The annals kept at Wal'e (PAL·AN·00262) give it one line: "The dead lay unburied." Her sister, niece and carter are mine.
