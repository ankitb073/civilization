title: Nobody Digs in Ash
---
Tolorath heard it before she saw it: a street going quiet from the gate inward, the way a barley field goes still when a hawk is over it.

She was sitting on the wall-steps by the upper gate, where children were not allowed to sit, with a barley cake in a cloth for her father. She had brought one every evening since the levy marched out against [[pol:773|Tonnga]], and every evening she had eaten it herself on the walk home, slowly, so that it would still count as waiting. Now she stood up. The cake rolled down two steps and she let it go.

The men came up the Diviminnge road in no order at all. Not in ranks, not even in companies: in twos and threes and alone, a long frayed rope of them, some with spears and most with nothing, one carried on a door by four others who did not look down at him. They were grey to the knee with the dust of the steppe.

‘Who won?’ a boy shouted from the gate-tower. Somebody laughed, a short terrible sound, and nobody answered him.

Tolorath went down among them with the one question she had. Toran, son of Poftovan, from the street of the bronze-smiths. A spearman. Tall, with a crucible burn on the back of his left hand. A man with a split lip looked at her for a long while, as if she had asked him the name of a star.

‘There were three of us,’ he said at last, ‘for every one of them. Do you understand? They were a third of us.’ He held up his hand with the fingers spread, as though a third were a thing you could show on fingers. ‘And the general’s dead. Shemenen’s dead. I saw his horse come back with nobody on it.’

‘And Toran?’

But he had gone on past her into the city, still holding up his hand.

* * *

Poftovan did not go to the gate. His knees would not have got him there before dark, and he had seen beaten men come home before, in the old wars, when the city still answered to Yennga. The first ones back, he told his granddaughter when she came in white and silent, were always the ones who had run first. The slow ones were not the dead ones. Not always. She nodded. He did not know whether either of them believed it. At the quern by the door Eloth went on grinding, though grain had been dear all year and there was flour enough in the house for a week and no one in it who could have eaten, and under her breath she gave [[dei:17|Nedhekhte]] the same three words over and over, as if the grain goddess were hard of hearing.

He could not stay in the house with that sound. He went to the temple of Eba.

It was a year old, the newest roof in [[set:151|Nodoruegeremo]]. The queen had built it in the sixth year of her reign, for the god of fire, and the pine of its posts was still pale where hands had not yet darkened it. The walls were limed white. In the middle of the floor was the hearth, a ring of dressed stones, where the priests fed the god’s fire and never let it die; beside the hearth was the pit where they raked his ash, as deep as a man’s forearm and soft as flour. And on the right-hand doorpost, at the height of a tall man’s eyes, was Poftovan’s bronze.

He had made it the summer before. The queen’s scribe had come to the street with the words scratched into a tablet of wet clay and asked who had the steadiest hand, and the street, after an argument, had said Poftovan. He could not read. Hardly anyone in the street could. He had pressed the marks into the wax line by line, the way he copied the leaf-border on a lamp, without knowing which mark was a god and which a queen, and he had cast the plate and filed it and burnished it with ash and oil until the letters stood up from it like the veins on the back of an old hand. He was paid in silver, weighed out on the temple’s own scales, and in grain. On the day it was hung the scribe read it aloud, and Poftovan made him read it twice, so as to keep it:

"[[per:17515|Talenornenath]], queen of [[pol:799|Nodoruegeremo]], built this temple for [[dei:18|Eba]], her lord. May [[dei:18|Eba]] protect the queen."

He had liked that it was short. A prayer for a queen should be short, he thought. The gods were busy, and a queen could always build another temple.

The old priest was at the hearth, feeding the fire a stick at a time. He did not look up.

‘Is it true about the general?’

‘It’s true.’

‘Twenty-six,’ Poftovan said. He had watched Shemenen ride out at the head of the levy, a boy with a good seat and a seriousness that looked borrowed for the day. Younger than Toran.

‘[[dei:14|Bolme]] was not with us,’ the priest said.

‘She was with them, then.’

‘Bolme is with no one.’ The priest laid on another stick. ‘She is the goddess of war, not the goddess of this city. People forget it.’

He said what priests said: that prosperity was the sign of the gods’ favour, and its loss the sign of something owed, and that the city would learn what it owed and pay it. Poftovan had paid for things all his life. He wondered what the price would be, and in what, since the gods did not take their silver by weight.

* * *

Tonnga came up the road behind the last of the beaten men, the way a dog comes behind a cart. Their horsemen showed first along the ridge, unhurried, and then the rest, and at night their fires lay across the hills under a thin paring of [[dei:19|Yenan]], like a second town that someone had dropped and broken. Poftovan could have counted the fires from the roof. He did not.

The gates were shut. No one in the street of the bronze-smiths knew where the queen was. Some said she was in the palace with her guard, some that she had gone out by night to find another army. The priest said the gods knew where the queen was, which was what priests said when they did not.

Poftovan lay awake. He thought about his son. He thought about the boy-general. Then, and he was ashamed of it, he thought about his bronze. He knew what soldiers took from a taken city, because he had made most of it: lamps and bowls, hinges, the rings of harness. Bronze is worth what it weighs. They would prise his letters off the doorpost and throw them in a sack with the temple lamps, and somewhere on the steppe a smith of Tonnga would melt them down for bridle rings, and the queen’s prayer would end in a horse’s mouth.

He got up. Tolorath was awake too; he could see the wet shine of her eyes in the dark. He meant to go alone. She came anyway, carrying the lamp, and he let her, because his hands would need to be free and because, he told himself afterwards, someone ought to know.

The priest was still at the hearth. He looked at the pry-bar and the tongs, and at the doorpost, and did not pretend not to understand.

‘They’ll take the vessels,’ he said. ‘They’ll kick the fire apart to see if there’s gold under it. The god’s fire goes out tomorrow, or the day after, whatever we do.’ He was quiet a while. ‘Nobody digs in ash. Ash is what’s left when there’s nothing worth taking.’

The pins came out hard. In a single year the pine had closed around them as if it meant to keep them. Then the plate was in Poftovan’s hands, heavier than he remembered, heavier, he thought, than words had any right to be.

They knelt at the pit. The ash was still warm a hand’s depth down. He laid the bronze in face up, the letters to the roof, and Tolorath held the lamp over it, and every raised mark threw its own small shadow.

‘What does it say?’ she said. ‘Say it properly.’

He said it properly, all of it, the queen and the temple and the lord and the prayer.

‘Will he? Now it’s in the ash?’

‘He’s had a year to read it,’ Poftovan said.

She did not laugh. She scooped up a double handful of ash and held it over the plate, and before she let it fall she bent close and said something into her cupped hands, so low he would not have heard it if he had not been listening for exactly that. Her father’s name. Only the name, after the queen’s.

He pretended he had not heard. They covered the bronze until the pit looked like any ash pit, and the priest raked the top smooth with the god’s own rake and put another stick on the fire, and that was all.

* * *

He never learned how the gate was opened. Afterwards everyone in the street had a version, and in every version it was someone else’s fault. By the middle of the morning there were men of [[pol:773|Tonnga]] in the street of the bronze-smiths, and by noon there was smoke over the lower town, and a sound from the next street that Eloth put her hands over Tolorath’s ears against, and kept them there.

Three of them came into the workshop. They were smaller than he had imagined, and dusty, and one had a cold. They took the copper in its cakes and the tin in its leather bag. They took the good tongs, and the crucibles, and the iron knife Toran had bought him, which Poftovan had never liked. They took Eloth’s flour, and the jar it was in. They looked at the old man and the woman and the girl the way you look at furniture you cannot carry, and went out.

The last one stopped in the doorway. He pointed at the street, at the roofs, at the ground under his feet, and said a word to the others, pleased with it, and then said it again: *Nuthuruekhrim*. It took Poftovan a moment to understand that it was the name of his city, said the way they said it, as if the man were telling the city what it was called.

Across the square, the house where the scribes kept their tablets stood with its door kicked in. Two soldiers came out of it with nothing, scuffing through the broken clay on the threshold. They did not want clay. Nobody set fire to it.

When the Tonnga men had gone, with their carts, and the city had begun to count what was left of itself, Poftovan went back to the temple. The bronze had been stripped from the doors. The lamps were gone, and the bowls. The hearth had been kicked apart, as the priest had said it would be, and the god’s fire was out, and the stones were cold. Feet had trampled the ash pit flat. But no one had dug.

On the doorpost were four holes where the pins had been. Around each one the pale wood was gouged with a knife-point, as though someone had stood here a while, puzzled, and dug a little at the place where bronze ought to be, and given it up.

Poftovan put his thumb into one of the holes. It fitted.

* * *

Word came that the queen was alive. Nobody could say who had brought it.

The priests made new fire when the city had been a day without soldiers. They made it the old way, the way it is made only when a god’s fire has died, with a bow and a drill and a nest of dry moss, and when the smoke came the old priest said the oldest words of [[mov:2|Jenro]]: that all things turn on the wheel, and what ends will begin again. They built the fire on the same hearth, beside the same ash.

Tolorath was not there. Men had been seen again on the Diviminnge road, a few, the slow ones, and she was gone before anyone could tell her not to hope.

Poftovan stayed. His knees. He held his hands out to the new fire, a stride from the place where the bronze lay in the dark with the queen’s prayer standing up from it and the girl’s breath pressed somewhere into the ash above. He found that he wanted to pray. He had cut a prayer once, and carried one, and buried one, and in all his life he had never made one of his own. He did not know the letters for it.

He said it anyway.
