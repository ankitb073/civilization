To the magistrate of the camp, from a soldier of the levy of [[set:76|Nimith]], taken on the road without his shield: this confession, written in my own hand, since you have been kind enough to leave me one.

I do not deny the charge. I left the standard on the third night after the town fell, and walked by the stars until your horsemen found me asleep in a sheepfold. I ask only that you read what follows before you decide what to do with the hand that wrote it.

I enlisted in the year this war began, the [[evt:24626|Tsiy–Mii War]], and enlisted gladly. My father had fought for [[pol:949|Tsiy]] at the [[evt:24521|Battle of Zhaalakhazhash]] and came home with a scar he showed at every feast, and I wanted a scar of my own to show. The criers said that [[pol:1065|Mii]] was weak and its lands were rich, and that the emperor [[per:23971|Yithnim]], who has reigned since before I was born, would lead us himself. All of this proved true. That is the difficulty.

We came into [[reg:3|Mii]] in the spring, a host so great that the dust of it stood over the road all day like a second sky. I am told there were five and twenty thousand of us. I believe it; I saw the end of the column only once, from a hill, and it was still coming. Before us stood the walls of [[set:192|Miwimai]]. Its commander, a man named [[per:24451|Ithuina'e]], had with him, we learned afterwards, eleven thousand men. Eleven thousand, against more than twice their number.

You will know the official account: that the [[evt:24647|Battle of Mivimäkhi]] was a great victory, and so it was, for the town is ours. You will perhaps not know what it cost, because no stele will record it. Nearly a thousand of our men are dead. Some fell at the wall, for those eleven thousand fought like men who knew they would not be spared. Some died of the camp fever, which came in the second week. Some were crushed at the breach by our own ranks pressing behind. One man of my tent drowned fetching water. The defenders lost twice as many, and the officers call it a cheap victory. I stood at the burial pits and did the sum: three thousand men, ours and theirs, for one town, and it is called cheap.

The philosophers tell us that courage is the mean between rashness and cowardice. I have thought about this a great deal in the sheepfold. A man who runs from the walls is a coward. But what is an army of five and twenty thousand that buries a thousand of its own and calls the bargain cheap? I do not think the philosophers have a word for it, and I did not wish to stay and learn one.

I do not blame the emperor. Emperors do not count as soldiers count. And I do not ask to be excused. I took the oath before the altar of [[dei:87|Hutlhi]], lady of the dead, and I have broken it, and she will have her due of me whatever you decide.

I ask two things. First, that my pay, which stands at eleven [[cur:542|Hazhni]] owed and unpaid since the spring, be sent to my mother in Nimith, who must buy grain at nearly two Hazhni the measure and has no other son. Second, that if I am to be made an example, it be made quickly, and at dawn, which is when I am least afraid.

I have heard the army marches again within the month. The war is not over; the criers say Mii has other walls. I hope they open their gates before the ladders go up, for their sake and for ours.

Written in the camp, in the year 233 of Tsiy.
