title: Who Drinks First
---
The woman was lowering a cord into the well.

Bithunen saw her as he came round the end of the grain-sellers' row with his jar on his shoulder, and for a moment he did not understand what she was doing, because nothing else in the market of [[set:70|Nivanga]] was being done the way it was done on other mornings. The awnings were down. A loom lay in the gutter with its warp cut. Over the roofs of the upper town a brown smoke stood straight up in the windless air, and from somewhere behind it came the sound of a door being broken with a beam, slow and uneven, like a drum in the hands of a man who could not keep time.

But the woman paid out her cord hand under hand, a knot at every forearm's length, until it went slack. She waited. She drew it up and ran her thumb down the knots, and where the wet began she stopped, and said one word aloud to nobody, and coiled the cord over her arm.

She was not young. Her hair was grey at the parting and her feet were bare. Beside her on the curb-stones stood a wooden chest, its lid blackened at one corner, as if she had carried it out of a fire.

Bithunen had been thirsty for three days. The thirst had begun on the field outside the walls, where nearly twenty-eight thousand of the queen's men had stood in the sun against a little over three thousand three hundred of the city's, and nothing had answered it since but the warm last mouthfuls of the skins. He had not, so far as he knew, killed anyone in the [[evt:23416|Battle of Nivanga]]. He had stood in the fourth rank and gone forward when the rank in front went forward, and by the time the sun was high it was over.

Afterwards they had put him on the burial detail, because he had been on it after the [[evt:23384|Battle of Nuvun]] two years before, and the army's scribe remembered him. The scribe could write and Bithunen could not, but the scribe could not look at faces, and Bithunen could. So Bithunen went among them and called each one aloud, and the scribe pressed a mark into his tablet without lifting his eyes. Five hundred and thirty-one of theirs. A hundred and thirty-nine of ours. He knew both numbers now the way he knew his mother's name. They had gone into him through the arms.

He set his jar down on the curb. The woman looked at the jar, and then at his spear, and then at his face. She held up one finger.

* * *

His file came up behind him a little later: Lavnan, who was the oldest of them, and five others with jars and skins, and the borderer, who had been born in one of the districts the old queen took from Nivanga before Bithunen was born, and who spoke both tongues and was trusted in neither.

The woman held up her finger to them too, and said something.

"One jar," said the borderer. "One for each of you. Another when the sun goes down."

Lavnan laughed. He had a blue cloak over his arm that had not been his the day before. "Tell her whose city it is."

The borderer told her. She answered him at some length, without raising her voice, and he listened with his eyes on the ground.

"She says it was the king's city the day before yesterday and the queen's city today, and the well was the well on both days."

Nobody at the well knew where the king of Nivanga was that morning. Nobody asked.

Lavnan dug in his belt and brought out a coin and held it up between two fingers so that it caught the light: a [[cur:503|Yuenu]], good gold, enough at home for nearly a measure of grain.

"I can pay," he said.

The borderer did not need to be told what to say. He said it, and the woman answered at once, as if the answer had been waiting in her mouth for years, and the borderer turned it into their tongue slowly, as though he were hearing it from a long way off.

"So can the well, and it will not take your coin."

Lavnan's face went dark under the dust. He put out his hand for the cord on her arm.

She did not pull it away. She stooped instead and lifted the lid of the chest, and took out a second cord, older, the colour of a dry riverbed, and let it fall open between her hands so that they could all see it. It had thirty knots. The last ten were stained darker than the rest, almost black, and at the tenth knot someone had tied a thread.

She talked, and the borderer talked after her.

"Her grandfather's. There was a drought here, before any of us. Fourteen years. The water stood down there" — he pointed at the black knots — "all those years, and people died at that depth. This morning it's at the ninth." He listened again. "She says she doesn't know how many of us came in through the gate. She says she can see it's more than rain. She says an army is a dry year that walks."

Nobody laughed, not even Lavnan. They were thirsty men, and a thirsty man can be made to listen to anything about water.

Bithunen picked up his jar and held it out. "One," he said, in his own tongue, and held up a finger as she had.

She let the bucket down on its chain, brought it up dripping, and filled his jar to the neck. He drank until his teeth ached. Then, because his mother had taught him that the gods give and must be given to, he tipped a little onto the curb-stone for [[dei:141|Navthin]], who keeps the rivers and so, he supposed, the water under the ground. He stopped, and after a moment tipped out a little more. The priests of [[mov:21|Khunewmu]] said that many gods share the world, each with their place and their due, and this was a stranger's well. Whatever goddess lived in it, she was not his, and she was owed.

The woman watched him do it. Her face did not change.

Lavnan filled his jar, one, and spat beside the well, and said there was silver in the upper town that did not argue. The others went with him. The borderer stayed long enough to drink, and then he went too.

* * *

Bithunen did not decide to stay. He found, when the borderer had gone, that he had not gone with him.

He laid his spear across the mouth of the trough, the way a gate-guard lays one across a gate, and stood beside it. When men came down the grain-sellers' row with jars and skins and helmets, he held up one finger. Most of them stood in line without being told twice; a soldier will stand in any line if it ends in water. Some cursed him. One, a big man from the hill levies, put a hand on his chest and pushed, and Bithunen pushed back, and the big man looked at the spear and at the woman with her cord and at the boy with one finger in the air, and decided that the morning was too hot for it.

Around noon a girl of the city came out of a doorway with a jar on her hip, saw the soldiers, and stopped as if she had walked into a wall. The woman called something to her. Bithunen took a step back from the curb. The girl came, filled her jar, one, and went, very fast, without looking at anybody. A man in the line said something about the girl, and Bithunen said, "One," and the man let it go.

At noon the woman sounded the well again. Bithunen counted the dry knots with her as they came up through her hands, moving his lips. Eight, nine, ten. The wet began at the eleventh. She said her word for it. He said his.

She looked at him then, for the first time, as if he were a person and not a weather.

He thought, in the long glare of the afternoon, about the stone at Nuvun. He had been at the raising of it. Queen Duyirith had set it up for [[dei:136|Dueminu]], her lord, and a priest had stood in front of the army and read the signs aloud, and Bithunen remembered the sentence exactly, because he had been waiting all through the reading for the number: "She killed 4,400 soldiers."

He had counted seven hundred and twenty-six. He could not read, but he could take one number from another, and he had stood in the ranks that day and taken it: three thousand six hundred and seventy-four men who had never been born, standing in stone under the storm god's name, where no one would ever have to bury them.

His own name meant victory stone. His mother had chosen it for luck. She had taught him what the priests taught, that prosperity is the sign of the gods' favour, that they show their love by what they give; and on the walk into Nivanga he had seen men coming down from the upper town under bundles of cloth and bronze, and he had thought, Then the gods love us very much today. He wondered whether a stone would be raised for this field too, and what number would be cut on it, and whether anyone who had carried the five hundred and thirty-one would be there to hear it read.

Late in the day four horsemen came down the row leading a string of remounts, and turned toward the trough.

"Not the horses," Bithunen said. "The river's outside the gate."

The first rider looked down at him. He was not much older than Bithunen, and he had a whip in his fist and the dust of the whole campaign in his beard. "Out of the way, spearman."

Bithunen did not move. He did not quite know why. Afterwards he thought it was the thread on the tenth knot.

The whip opened his cheek from the ear to the corner of his mouth. He went down on the curb-stones, and while he was down they watered the horses, bucket after bucket, a horse to drink what four households drink, and the woman stood by with her cord and said nothing at all. When they had gone she knelt, wet the corner of her sleeve in the bucket, and held it to his face. She said something. There was no borderer to tell him what. He found he did not need one.

* * *

At dusk she let the cord down for the last time that day, and he counted with her.

Ten. Eleven. Twelve. The wet began at the thirteenth knot.

She said her word for it, and he said his, and then she said hers again, more slowly, the way you speak to a child, and he tried to say it after her and said it badly, and she nodded once. Then she took a thread from the chest and tied it at the thirteenth knot of her own cord, so that the day would be kept.

He filled his jar, one, and walked back up the grain-sellers' row through the dark with the water slopping cold against his shoulder. The smoke over the upper town had turned red underneath. Someone was singing, and someone else was weeping, in two languages, and he could not have said which was which.

Years afterwards, at the well of his own village on the road to [[set:16|Turamtu]], when the old soldiers told one another what they had carried away from Nivanga — a cloak, a silver lamp, a girl's bronze mirror — Bithunen said that he had carried away a jar of water and a number. Nobody asked him what the number was. It was thirteen. It was the only number he brought home from that war that nobody, afterwards, made any larger.

**Author's note.** The keeper's cords and her answer to the man with silver come from the anonymous well-keeper's day-book of AY 2638 (PAL·DI·00032), excavated at [[set:70|Nivanga]]; I have given its writer a daughter. The stone is Duyirith's stele for Nuvun (PAL·IN·01722), set against the record's 726 dead. The record holds the [[evt:23417|Sack of Nivanga]], the [[evt:23418|Conquest of Nivanga]] that followed, and the fact that the city's archives were not burned. It does not say who drank. Duyirith died the next year; her son [[per:23226|Litathen]] was eleven (PAL·SP·00067).
