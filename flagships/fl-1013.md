title: The Grain of My Lady
---
The children of [[set:31|Waga]] learned to write by telling a queen that all was well.

"To Yoni Ash, my lady, say: thus says your servant," Baalshima read from the doorway, and nine reed pens went down into the ink and came up and began to scratch. He waited the length of the line. Waiting the length of a line was most of what teaching was. "The grain of my lady is safe in the granary."

From the back a boy asked how many letters there were in *granary*. Every autumn for forty years a boy at the back had asked it.

"Look at yesterday's," Baalshima said.

The granary itself stood across the lane, the queen's granary, long and windowless and buttressed against its own weight, and in this season it was full. You could smell it from the school, dust and sweetness, and hear the sparrows quarrelling at its vents. "The boat of my lady has arrived at the quay." That line they all knew. The quay was at the foot of the lane, and the boats that came to it came for the granary; on still mornings the pupils could hear the boatmen counting sacks aloud, and sometimes, to his despair, they wrote the numbers down.

He had known the letter with five names in it. He had learned it himself, a boy with ink to the wrists, addressed to the lord of Ana. Ana fell when he was twelve, and he copied it next to a king of Shetie, and then, a young man with pupils of his own, taught it to a queen of Shetie. Then Shetie came apart like a dropped jar, and the piece with Waga in it was called [[pol:1228|Yisamashtsi]] and given a king the city called the Lame, and Baalshima scraped one name off the model sherd and painted on another. When the Lame died his daughter was sixteen. He scraped the sherd again and turned *my lord* into *my lady*, and that was fifteen years ago. The kingdom was thirty-two years old. He was sixty-one. The city held more than twenty thousand, though no one in it could have told you so: it had always been there, on its river, in its grass, and the names in the letter passed over it like weather.

* * *

The runner came up from the quay a little before noon. Baalshima did not hear what he shouted, only how the lane went quiet after it, and then how it went loud.

The queen's army had been broken at Faamni.

The record would one day call it the [[evt:28669|Battle of Faamni]] and set it down plainly: two thousand four hundred and ninety-three of Yisamashtsi under the queen, nine thousand and forty of [[pol:1166|Tamisitsiyi]] against them; three hundred and forty-six of the first lost, fifty-nine of the second. Waga had none of these numbers that noon. It had a boatman leaning on the granary wall to get his breath, saying the queen was dead, the queen was taken, the queen was on the road home with what was left of them — saying all three, because he had heard all three on the river.

Baalshima sent the children home. He made them wash their pens first, since a pen put away wet will split, and a split pen writes nothing but blots; they stood at the trough in a row and did it, and he understood that they did it because he had told them in his ordinary voice. The last to leave was Yi Yueve, who was ten and wrote better already than the boy at the back ever would.

"Should I come tomorrow?"

"Come when there's school," he said.

That night his wife took up the floor.

It was Pieluesha's idea, as most of the good ideas in the house had been. The school's fees were in a jar in the niche behind the beer — six years of them, since the queen began to strike her own silver, so that coin after coin carried her head, diademed, facing right, and on the back a horseman and the mint mark of Waga. Grain had gone to nearly two Ia the measure since the war began. Everyone in the lane had a jar somewhere, and everyone in the lane was burying it tonight.

"Not all of it," Pieluesha said, and counted a few back into the old purse, and hung the purse on its peg. "If they find nothing, they keep looking. If they find something, they stop."

They dug in the schoolroom, where the smallest children sat, because the floor there was beaten hard as pot by small heels and nobody would think a school worth digging. He set the jar in and tamped the earth down with his heel, and sprinkled the floor, and swept it. Then he stood a long time holding the broom.

"In the spring," he said.

"In the spring," said Pieluesha.

* * *

They came some days later, on a morning that smelled of rain. Baalshima never learned whether the river gate had been opened to them or broken; by the time the noise reached the lane it was the noise of a city that already had them inside it. He went up on the roof with Pieluesha and watched the smoke climb from the direction of the market — not so very much smoke — and then he watched the granary.

They broke its doors with a beam. They did not burn it; it was what they had come for. All morning men carried the queen's grain down the lane in the queen's own sacks, past the school door, to the quay, where every boat in Waga had been made to wait, and Baalshima found his lips moving and knew what they were saying. *The grain of my lady is safe in the granary. The boat of my lady has arrived at the quay.* Forty years of children had written it, and it had been true each time, and now it was happening in front of him exactly as written, only backwards.

In the afternoon three of them came into the courtyard. One had a crossbow slung on his back with the string let down, and he was young, younger than some of the fathers who had once sat on that floor. They spoke the queen's tongue with the vowels a little flattened, and one of them, cracking his head on the lintel, swore by the same gods Baalshima would have sworn by. They took the lamp and the blankets and the beer. They found the purse on its peg.

The crossbowman tipped it into his palm and weighed it, and held one coin up to the doorway light, the queen's small head.

"Light," he said. He worked a coin of his own out of his belt and laid it beside hers: a [[cur:639|Tluitlsi]] of his own country, with half as much silver again in it. "Your queen's are light."

Baalshima said nothing. It was true.

Then the young man saw the shelf. There were perhaps twenty sherds on it, the ones worth keeping, stacked by year. He lifted one down and turned it to the door and read it aloud, slowly, a finger under the line, the way somebody somewhere had taught him.

"To Yoni Ash, my lady, say: thus says your servant. The grain of my lady is safe in the granary. The boat of my lady has arrived at the quay."

One of the others laughed. The crossbowman did not.

"We had this one," he said. "Near enough. Ours goes to [[per:27970|Shoe Nyime]], and the boat comes to the quay the same." He looked at the foot of the sherd, at the other hand. "Better than yesterday," he read. "Who wrote that?"

"I did."

"And Poabe?" The name was scratched along the edge.

"A pupil. Two autumns ago. The first time without a mistake."

The young man considered this. "Mine wrote," he said. "*Write it again, ten times.*" He shrugged. "Every day." He set the sherd back on the shelf, squared to the others, as if it were the one thing in the room that was not his to take. The third soldier had put his foot against the washing trough, and the crossbowman said, "Leave it," and the soldier left it.

At the door he turned, shaking the purse so that it rang, and with a kind of smile said what the priests of the [[mov:25|Myitsiyishio]] said in both their countries: "Wealth is a chain of gold; the wise go without." Baalshima could not tell whether it was a joke. Afterwards he decided it had been meant as a kindness, and that was worse.

* * *

On the third day they were gone, with the grain and the boats and whatever else could be carried. They had not burned the house of tablets by the palace — they had no use for it, people said, or no time; Baalshima thought that perhaps one of them could read. The queen was alive, people said. No one said where.

The next morning Yi Yueve was at the door with her pen washed and a sherd under her arm.

"Is there school?"

"There is now," he said.

She sat where the smallest children sat, cross-legged on the swept floor, and he thought of what lay a forearm's depth beneath her: the queen in silver, over and over, facing right in the dark. In the spring, he thought.

"To Yoni Ash, my lady, say," he read, "thus says your servant." Scratch. He waited the length of the line. "The grain of my lady is safe in the granary."

Her pen stopped.

"It isn't," she said. "They took it. I watched from our roof."

He could have told her a great many things. That the letter was older than the queen and older than the kingdom. That it had been true under five names and would be true again under another, and that a crossbowman had already told him which. That she was not learning what to say to a queen, only how to make the marks, and that the marks would outlast them all — on that he would have staked the jar beneath her. He said none of it.

"Write it as it is written," he said.

She wrote it. She wrote the boat. She handed up the sherd, and he held it to the light and saw that she had made two mistakes, which was one fewer than the last time: she had put the grain at the quay and the boat at the granary. He looked at that for a while. Then he struck the wrong words through, one line each, and wrote the right ones after them, small, the way it was done.

Under the letter, in his own hand, he wrote what he always wrote, and this time it was even true.

*Better than yesterday.*

**Author's note.** The record files this as the [[evt:28670|Sack of Bakh]], in the [[evt:28643|Tamesitsiyi–Yisamashtsi War]], and names no one in the lane. The letter is Poabe's sherd (PAL·SX·00498); the crossbowman's teacher speaks from a sherd of Tamisitsiyi (PAL·SX·00390), his saying from Eshievashie (PAL·SC·00002). The queen's first silver (PAL·NU·00171) is known from a hoard; whose, the record does not say. Shoe Nyime's stele (PAL·IN·02138) claims 1,300 dead at Faamni; the record counts 346, and gives that day's command to Esie Foyatl. The next year brought the [[evt:28683|Siege of Bakh]], the [[evt:28684|Conquest of Yisamashtsi]], and Yoni Ash's execution.
