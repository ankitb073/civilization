title: Six Fires Where There Was One (AY 750–1000)

In the third year of her reign, which was also the last year of her kingdom, Queen Makhshishenmumath of [[pol:61|Tallin]] dictated a decree to whatever priests and scribes were still in her palace. The governors had stopped sending the tribute, and in her own city of [[set:0|Taulin]] another man called himself lord. Her orders were practical. The palace grain was to go to the widows. The guard was to go home: "Let no man die at the gate for a seat that is broken." And the tablets of the kings were to be "sealed in jars and set beneath the floor of the house of the gods, so that the names of the kings of Tallin are not lost" (PAL·DE·00920, *Last Decree of Queen Makhshishenmumath*, AY 825).

The court of Mii gave much the same order in 974, and the court of Luthe in 989. As an archaeologist I cannot read these decrees without picturing the jars, and they explain much of what we know. The years from AY 750 to 1000 are the age in which the great houses of the previous chapter came apart, one after another, and we mostly hear their endings in the voices of the courts that lost.

The overall numbers do not look like an age of ruin. The people living under states rose from 51.4 million in AY 750 to 58.0 million in 870 and 58.9 million in 990. The number of states grew from 27 to 36, and the land that no state claimed shrank from a third of the map in AY 620 to a tenth by 870. Yet six great states broke apart: [[pol:40|Navnga]] in 819, Tallin in 825, [[pol:80|Nelo]] in 833, Egu in 840, [[pol:93|Mii]] in 973 and Luthe in 987. Almost every one broke at or near its greatest size. These were not collapses brought on by hunger. What failed was the loyalty of the governors.

## The spearman's victory

The old order of chapter 3 ended quickly. The [[evt:3602|Secession of Piviyo]] in 750 and the [[evt:3608|Secession of Bimunga]] in 752 helped bring down Va'mi and Duvegobuvinga by 770. The [[evt:3653|Secession of Khie'a]] in 758 ended the Kingdom of Yi'otl two years later.

The great war of the early decades was fought between two survivors. In 761 Queen [[per:3573|Shijilialath]] of Tallin sent her army to take [[reg:16|Yidhe]] from Navnga, and so began the [[evt:3676|Tallin–Navnga War]]. One of her spearmen set down his memories in old age, around AY 795 (PAL·CH·08435, *What the Spearman Saw in the Navnga War*). At Rile in 763, he writes, "the men of Navnga were as many as locusts". The record agrees: about 20,000 against 9,600, and Tallin lost. At Shivange in 764, under the new king [[per:4011|Evenin]], "the dead of Navnga lay in the field like sheaves after the sickle. Seven thousand, the scribes said. I believe it. I walked among them." Here the scribes were honest: Navnga lost 7,634 men that day. The defeats he blames on soft ground and a theft from a shrine.

The war ended in 770 at Vuvu with the status quo and a ten-year truce. Over the nine years Tallin had lost some 10,900 men and Navnga 16,400. "Is that not a victory?" the old man asks. "The young men laugh. Let them laugh." He says Navnga had taken tribute from "our fathers", but the record dates that war to 547–550, two centuries before his birth. Memory had folded the generations together.

A worse killer than either army travelled with them. [[evt:3665|The Marsh Pestilence]] broke out at Taulin in 765, in the middle of the war. It spread to 31 more cities, among them Rile, Shivange, Nilinge and Vuvu, the very places where the armies had fought, and killed about 1,480,000 people, close to a third of those it reached. Navnga's army fell by half within a decade, from about 47,700 men to 23,400. Then Mount I'amikhil erupted in 770, and the 770s became the coldest decade of the period.

## A wall falls in the rain

For a generation afterwards Navnga looked as strong as ever. [[per:3868|Dulomin]] the Conqueror reigned from 743 to 796, and in AY 810 Navnga reached its peak of 8.4 million people and 372 districts. But the record's measure of Navnga's internal order had fallen to nothing in the 770s and hardly rose again. After Dulomin, men of the sword held the throne. [[per:4247|Beroyramathen]], called the Usurper, held it until 807, and in 810 [[per:4324|Bünin I]], also called the Usurper, seized it. Nine years later the realm dissolved without a battle. A boy of twelve watched it happen and wrote it down at thirty-six (PAL·CH·08440, *How the House of Navnga Was Divided*, AY 843):

> In the spring the messengers from the provinces did not come. In the summer the tribute did not come. In the autumn there were six banners where there had been one.

The [[evt:4038|Collapse of the Navnga State]] left six heirs. [[pol:165|Yida]] took [[set:10|Navnge]] itself, and [[pol:166|Noni]], Tivintusa, Rele, Bäni and Nothinga took the rest. The writer blames the usurper, the pestilence and the men of Tallin, which are the three causes the record lists. His image for the fall is the best we have: it fell "as an old wall falls in the rain: first a crack, then a stone, then the whole."

Tallin fell six years later. Queen Makhshishenmumath blamed governors "who ate bread at my table". She also blamed the gods for Rile, Shivange, Vuvu and the pestilence, all more than fifty years past. That sounds like a stale excuse, yet the record names exactly those battles and that plague as causes of the [[evt:4077|Collapse of the Tallin State]]. Five heirs divided a realm of 6.8 million people. One kept the name, a new [[pol:171|Tallin]] at Taulin, and beside it stood [[pol:172|Duegowinga]], Tlü'ti, Noltennga and the [[pol:175|Yinnga]] empire, the largest of the five.

Nelo followed in 833. A temple scribe at [[set:5|Nilu]] counted four evils (PAL·CH·08441, *Tablet of the Breaking of Nelo*, AY 851). The first was a usurpation in 770. The second was the White Pestilence of 798, which killed about 460,000. The third was the [[evt:3937|Secession of Kagene]] in 803, and the fourth the seizure of the throne by [[per:4382|Nashenjè]] in 816. Between the two usurpers had reigned [[per:4169|Nagovo II]], called the Wise. When the new year of 833 came, the governors did not: "The roads were empty." The Nelo that survived at Nilu began as little more than the city and its fields, with [[pol:178|Puvu]] and [[pol:180|Kagüne]] among its new neighbours.

Egu fell in 840. Its deposed king Bepimttumin wrote from exile to beg a foreign king for men (PAL·LT·01689): "Not the enemy. The enemy I had beaten. Not hunger. The storehouses were full. Treachery did it." He had seized the throne himself in 826, and the record counts that usurpation, with old defeats at Yinishme and Nuwkennga, among the causes of the [[evt:4213|Collapse of the Egu State]]. One of Egu's heirs was a small city-state, [[pol:185|Tomözel]], a name later chapters will meet again.

The four collapses share a pattern. None followed a famine. Through Tallin's decline, grain at Taulin stayed between 6.7 and 8.8 grams of silver a measure, close to the long-run norm of 8 grams. All four had old wars or plague among their recorded causes, and three had a usurpation or secession as well. In each case a successor kept the capital, and usually the name. What the old state lost was its provinces.

## Dry decades and the price of barley

The climate was mild but dry. After the cold 770s the world warmed to about the long-run mean by AY 900, but rain grew scarcer, and the precipitation index was at its lowest from the 890s to the 950s. The record dates great droughts to Nis from 783, Luthe from 831 and Nivi from 983, when 506 districts went dry. The [[evt:4416|Onset of the Great Drought in Mikhukhizhi (AY 868)]], across 384 districts, began a dry spell that lasted until 964. The temple of Atlie at Khie'a noted in 868: "Year after year the rains failed. Grain stood at 3.4 shekels of silver the measure. The canals stood dry and cracked" (PAL·RE·00015). In Khie'a's market a measure of grain cost 27 to 39 grams of silver from the 850s to the 880s, three to five times the norm.

The worst fell on Mana. The [[evt:4804|Famine of AY 903 in Mana]] killed or drove out about 512,800 people, a quarter of its population. A farmer of Tivintusa kept a diary on clay, which survives, water-stained, as the *Tablet of the Hard Year in Man'e* (PAL·DI·00007). He gives barley at "near four law'e" the measure. At about 9.4 grams of silver to the law'e, that is some 37 grams, and the record's price for the decade is 35. He watched the king's carts leave full of grain for a war against Tonnga: "The granary of the king is open for the host. It is not open for us." His mother "gave her bread to my children and she died". His daughter "does not cry any more, and that frightens me more than the crying."

Plague returned with the River Fever of 911, which killed about 253,000, and famine struck Khishkhih in 942 and 987. Money, at least, did not make things worse. The great houses reckoned in silver by weight: the menmu of Tallin at 8.3 grams, the lash'a of Mii at 10.1, the ikhal of I'ith at 10.7. In the record none of them was debased, so when prices doubled the cause was the harvest or the army, not the mint. Thirty-six merchant houses were founded in these years, and trade went on through the droughts and collapses alike.

## Mii at the summit

No state rose higher than Mii, or fell from a greater height. [[per:3853|E'a Mue'a III]] (739–772) had sworn the treaty that ended the First Yiutl Trade War of chapter 3 in a draw. Under his successor [[per:4052|A'insimen II]] (772–814) the court commissioned the *Song of the Twelve Summers in Yiutl* (PAL·PO·00001, AY 804), which ends battle after battle of that war with "The field was Mii's." Court poetry was not written to be checked.

But in the wars that followed, Mii's soldiers really did win. The record lists eighteen wars for Mii between 765 and 964, and Mii won every one. It grew from 182 districts to 382, and from 5.2 million people to a peak of 9.4 million by AY 970. The victories were costly. At Itlishithyi in 924, nearly 30,000 men of Mii attacked fewer than 2,000 of the small kingdom of Val'a. They were beaten and lost 11,397 men. Mii won the war anyway.

The trouble came from within. In 947 the province of [[pol:225|Miezanitie]] withheld its silver, and the [[evt:5220|First Mii Civil War]] began. The queen of Mii was [[per:5249|Mishfa'i]], called the Conqueror. In the set phrases of royal letters she calls herself "king of Mii" and the rebel Shimenshimen her "brother", and she threatens to "come and fetch" the enemies he sheltered (PAL·LT·00930). Her basalt stele in [[reg:5|Yiutl]] reads: "Mishfa'i, queen of Mii, defeated Miezanitie in Yi'otl. He killed 100 soldiers." The "he" is the mason's: the formula was made for kings, and nobody changed it for a queen. The 100 is the court's. In the record, the Battle of Yiutl in 949 set 23,150 royal troops against a rebel band of 27. Mii lost 1,490 men, the rebels 10. The next year the rebels beat Mii at Nyinsitlasa, killing 5,287, and again at Onithie. Not until 952–954 did the royal army retake the province, which was annexed under an amnesty. In that decade grain at [[set:7|Ane]] cost 20.5 grams a measure, two and a half times the norm.

Mishfa'i died in 955, and La'im rose at Namos'ash in 961. In its last decade Mii kept the largest army in its history, some 56,500 men, while its internal order fell to nothing. In 973 the seal was "broken into six pieces" (PAL·DE·00921, *The Last Decree of the Court of Mii*). The [[evt:5441|Collapse of the Mii State]] left a new [[pol:229|Ana]] empire at Ane, alongside [[pol:230|Miezanitia]], Yiutl, Namos'ash, Fa'amni and Mwehosh. The decree orders the seal smashed with a bronze hammer and frees the soldiers from their oath. It also asks protection for the last queen, Eshueshilie'an: "she has done no wrong but to live in the last year."

## Tribute, and those who paid it

Later wars were often fought for tribute, and several ended with the attacker paying it. The empire of [[pol:155|I'ith]], founded in 762 with its seat at [[set:152|Tanshedi]], took up [[mov:1|Ya'cuam]] in 786 under [[per:4038|Hish'o Mish]], called the Devout. For a century and a half it beat its small neighbours almost every time. In 873 its queen A'mapayal wrote to a defeated vassal about a young man of his house, whom she held as a hostage (PAL·LT·01676). The boy ate at her table, she said, but "if the silver is mixed with lead, then the bread will be taken from him." In 926 I'ith attacked [[pol:119|Tonnga]] for tribute, and the [[evt:5022|I'ith–Tonnga War]] went the other way. The king of I'ith fell in battle in [[reg:4|Ayshi]] in 933, and by the Treaty of Tanshedi I'ith paid Tonnga 85 talents of silver a year for thirty-five years (PAL·TR·00311). [[per:5400|Iminpayal]], called the Victorious, restored the realm's fortunes between 940 and 989.

Nelo learned the same lesson in the [[evt:5314|Holy War of Nelo against Puvu]] (956–963). It ended with Nelo paying 1,367 talents and opening its roads to Puvu's merchants without tolls. Then it was Nelo's turn to be attacked. In the [[evt:5395|Luthe–Nelo War]] (967–975), [[pol:134|Luthe]], the old realm of [[per:3654|Natyim]] with its seat at [[set:9|Dagafu]], marched on Nelo for tribute. Luthe won the first two battles and lost the next five. The Treaty of Dadavli bound Luthe to pay 114 talents a year for thirty-four years (PAL·TR·00343). Its treasury, about 246 tonnes of silver in the 970s, then shrank by some 36 tonnes, close to ten years of the tribute. The [[evt:5573|Collapse of the Luthe State]] followed in 987. Its last court wrote that "the tribute went out from Luthe year upon year, silver upon silver, and the granaries grew thin." It counted "six fires where there was one hearth" (PAL·DE·00922). The largest of the six was the [[pol:237|Tagath]] empire at Dagafu.

Where the two sides' accounts survive, we can measure the lies. In the [[evt:4383|Kadhdazhile–Kagüne War]] (857–866) over [[reg:21|Daadüdhih]], the Kagüne king Nangashu claimed 13,200 enemy dead at Dyizhih on a limestone stele (PAL·IN·00285). A Kagüne chronicle of 915 says 11,936 (PAL·CH·00710). The record says 4,066, and the war ended where it began. In the [[evt:5555|Second Nelo–Kadhdazhile War]] (984–993) the Nelo scribe claims 6,283 enemy dead for 90 of his own at Luyügih (PAL·CH·00949), where the record gives 1,880 and 375. The Kadhdazhile scribe (PAL·CH·00950) skips from his side's victories of 986–988 to the peace of 993, when "some villages … which had always been poor" were given up. In fact the treaty gave up two regions, [[reg:24|Pyivi]] and [[reg:20|Khishkhih]], two towns and thirty-three years of tribute. In that decade of war and drought together, grain at [[set:65|Adhizhili]] reached 44 grams a measure, five and a half times the norm.

## The wheel turns

Faith mostly spread by royal choice. The [[evt:3820|Conversion of Uem'o to Ya'cuam]] in 775 and the [[evt:3874|Conversion of Bäni to Ya'cuam]] in 785 came just before I'ith's. By 870 Ya'cuam held 8 percent of the land, behind La'im's 12. New cults rose where disorder was worst. [[per:4214|Hish'o Losh'a]] founded [[mov:29|Alshana'a]] at Iemi in 785. [[per:4469|Lukhkathren]] founded [[mov:30|Lovanu]] at Navnge in 826, seven years after the house of Navnga fell. [[per:4770|Todovin]] founded [[mov:31|Mvivituthpo]] in 856 and [[per:4771|Bedonin]] founded [[mov:32|Wishdoriru]] in 857. Yet the dying courts, whatever their gods, used the same words of comfort. The Navnga chronicler, the Nelo scribe, the courts of Tallin, Mii and Luthe and the exiled king of Egu all write that the wheel turns and "what ends will begin again."

Writing changed too. The Ngayakune syllabary was adopted at Kangushe in 842 and the Khave at Khukunji in 877. Within three-quarters of a century Khave carried Mishfa'i's victory formula, and kings would go on cutting their victories in it for centuries. Six languages became distinct from their parents, from Lukhmu and I'tlim by 799 to Sevim and Hanalholim by 999.

What survives shapes this chapter. The archive holds 531 everyday documents from these years: 226 inscriptions, 120 chronicles and 60 treaties, but only 17 ledgers and 17 letters. Victory stones outlast account books. The annals that survive belong mostly to heirs such as Nothinga, Yade, Egu, Tomözel and Birnadu, and each begins in the year its state was founded (PAL·AN·00097, 00107, 00108, 00111, 00112). So we see each collapse twice, through the courts that buried their tablets and through the governors who began new annals, and hardly at all through the farmers who paid for both.

By the 990s the map was crowded and new. Tallin had recovered under [[per:4463|Kodhakhin]] and [[per:5098|Niluyin]], and since 981 it had been ruled by [[per:5808|Kalidath]], called the Great. Yida had been ruled since 983 by [[per:5678|Beroybethun II]], called the Brave, and [[per:5884|Ma'shi Li]] took the throne of I'ith in 991. Ana and Tagath had risen from the wreckage of Mii and Luthe, and [[pol:153|Waga]], founded in 760, was growing toward its long life. In 999 Tallin went to war with Yida for Yidhe, the same land Shijilialath's spearmen had marched on in 761. That war, the [[evt:5693|First Tallin–Yida War]], opens the next chapter, beginning with a dispatch to the Great Queen from the field at Virathkanu.
