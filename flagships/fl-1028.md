title: Forfeit to the Queen
---
Good lime has to lie in the pit for a year at the least, under a hand's depth of water, until it goes soft as new cheese and white as the inside of a shell. Najige kept two pits in the yard behind her house. In the near one was the last lime her husband ever burned, slaked the spring he marched to Kamvamvil and three years down now, so smooth she could have spread it on bread. In the far one was this year's, still grey at the edges, still muttering to itself when she lifted the boards. Neither was for sale. Nobody would pay for the new, and she would not sell the old.

The night before the army went out she took a pot from the near pit, thinned it with water, and limed her son's shield. She gave it two coats, the way she would a doorpost, and stood it by the hearth to dry, and in the lamplight it was the whitest thing in the house.

"So your friends can find you in the trees," she said.

Nanjijim turned it over in his hands. He was nineteen and had his father's way of looking at finished work, which was to find the one place that was wrong. "So everybody can find me in the trees," he said. But he did not ask her to wash it off.

* * *

In the morning she stood under the customs gate of [[set:34|Tatunde]] and watched them go out. They went in fours under the arch, spears held low so as not to strike the stone, and the street smelled of sweat and oiled leather and the bread the women were pushing into their hands as they passed.

The stone was set into the gate at the height of a rider's head, where no carter coming in with a load could miss it: the dues of the market in rows, grain and salt and horses and spices, and under them the rules. Queen Nakuthu had put it up in the last war with the [[pol:1132|Tambakh]], when Najige was young. The queen had been dead eighteen years. Her younger son was king now, since the Luthe had killed the elder the year before. Najige could not read the stone and did not need to. She had whitened that gate every spring since she was nineteen, working round the carved face with a small brush, and every spring the collector had read the rules aloud to the street as if it might have forgotten them. The fourth she could have said in her sleep, Dambakhu and all: "Merchants of Dambakhu shall not pass the gate; their goods are forfeit to the queen."

Nanjijim went by in the second hundred, the shield on his arm like a moon come down early. He did not look for her. She had told him not to.

* * *

From the wall you could see the rise south of the city, where the wood began, and the open ground beside it where the Tambakh had camped. The night before, their fires had come out across that ground one after another, until it looked as though a second city had been built in an afternoon beside the first, bigger than it, and lit.

[[per:27043|Navande]] had four thousand two hundred and fifty-eight men to put among those trees. He was fifty years old, and three years before he had commanded at the [[evt:27476|Battle of Kamvamvil]] and lost it, and Najige's husband with it. Against him, under a general called [[per:27075|Namumbiv]] whom nobody in the city had heard of until that year, Tambakh had brought sixty thousand four hundred and forty.

Nobody on the wall knew the figure. They knew the fires. They knew that the chariots drawn up on the open ground at first light went on and on, wheel beyond wheel, and that the dust when the Tambakh began to move went up all along the front of the wood at once, as if the whole hillside had been struck with a stick.

Then there was nothing to see. That was the thing Najige had not expected. The wood took the men in, both sides of them, and gave back only sound: a long dry roar that came and went like threshing heard from another farm, and horns, and once a scream so clear that every woman on the wall turned her head the same way. Rooks went up out of the trees and did not come down. She looked for white between the trunks, and twice she thought she saw it.

She did not pray. The priests of [[mov:24|Uthunkale]] said there was one power and the old gods were only its servants, and she had always found it was no use asking a servant for what the master had not decided, and less use asking the master. She stood.

Somewhere about noon it came to her that she had limed the shield so it could be seen, and she understood, all at once and far too late, who else had been looking.

* * *

They came back out of the wood at dusk, and came back in order, which the old men on the wall said was something, though they did not say what. The Tambakh did not follow them to the walls. The gate was opened and the street filled with them, men walking, men carried, men holding up other men, and the women went down into it with their lamps and their names.

She saw the shield before she saw him. It was still white, mostly. Something had taken three bites out of the rim and laid the leather open to the wood in a long gash across the face, and the lime there had come away in flakes like old paint. Nanjijim's forearm was bound with a strip of somebody's shirt. He let her hold him the way he had let her pare his nails when he was small: patiently, looking past her, waiting for it to be over.

Three hundred and seventy of Navande's men did not come back from the wood alive. The record gives the other side too: four hundred and twenty-eight of theirs. In the market that week it was a thousand, and by the end of the week it was two, and a man who sold rope stood on his own counter and shouted that Navande had killed more of them than they had of us, as if that were a thing a city could eat.

Nanjijim sat by the hearth with his arm across his knees and did not go out to hear it.

* * *

The treaty was sworn at Tatunde before the rains. The king, who was twenty-seven, put his name to it, and so did the new queen of Tambakh, [[per:27073|Nandithim]], who was twenty-eight and had lost her father that same year. [[pol:1127|Pyivi]] gave up six districts to the south, the nearest of them hard by the rise where the battle had been. It paid silver. It swore fifteen years of truce. And the merchants of Tambakh were to have privileges in the markets of Pyivi, which meant here, which meant this gate.

The last time, Najige had been twenty-one. The Tambakh had sat down outside the walls that year, and when it was over the queen's envoys had gone out and come back with a treaty, and nine districts had gone, and Dadavli with them. She remembered thinking then that it could not happen twice. She was older now and knew that it could happen any number of times. It was the one thing, it seemed to her, that the world was really good at.

The collector came to her door on the third day after the swearing. He was an old man with a cough and a wax tablet on a cord round his neck, and he did not sit down.

"The fourth line," he said. "It comes off before their merchants come through. Palace says."

"Get a mason."

"I've had a mason. He says the letters are cut a finger deep and the stone's carrying the arch, and if he takes a finger off the face of it he'll have the gate down on the street. Cover it, he says. So." He coughed. "Cover it."

"And the second?" said Najige. The second rule raised every due but the grain by half, for as long as the war lasted.

"The second takes care of itself. That's the beauty of the second." He looked at the white on her hands. "They'll come with [[cur:608|Mimbu]]," he said. "Heavier than ours. We'll be weighing their silver and thanking them for the privilege."

She said, before she could stop herself, "Forfeit to the queen."

"Well," said the collector. "There's a queen at both ends of it now."

* * *

She went out to the yard before light and lifted the boards off the far pit.

New lime is never quite done. There are grains in it that have not taken their water, small and hard as grit, and a grain like that in a wall is a seed. It sits quiet through the dry season. Then the first rains find it, and it swells, and blows a crater the size of a lentil out of the face of the plaster. Put in enough of them, with too much sand and no hair to bind it, and by the end of the wet the face goes to pocks and crumbs, and whatever was under it shows through.

She filled the board from the far pit. She put in sand until the stuff went short and grainy under the trowel. She did not put in hair.

Nanjijim was waiting at the gate with the ladder and a lamp, though she had not asked him to come. He held the ladder while she climbed, and lifted the lamp so the light lay across the stone and filled every letter of the fourth line with its own small shadow. She wet the stone. She took up the first trowelful.

"That's the far pit," he said.

She did not answer.

"It'll blow by spring."

"It might."

He was quiet for long enough that she thought he had let it go. The lamp did not move.

"There was one in the wood," he said. "He came round a tree at me. They all came at me, because of the shield, you were right about that, I had them on me all morning. This one I got under the arm. He sat down against the tree and started saying the words. Uncle's words for Father, when there was nothing to bury. The line from the first morning to the judgement, and that he had kept to it, and the rest. All of it." He shifted the lamp to his other hand. "He said them better than I do. He didn't miss any."

"They took the faith twenty years ago," Najige said. "He'd have been a boy."

"He said them like he'd had them all his life." The lamp moved, finally, a little. "If you're going to cover it, Mother, cover it."

She stood on the ladder with the trowel in her hand and the grey stuff on it going dull in the air. Below her the street was empty, and the arch was cold, and somewhere up in the town a cockerel had started and stopped.

Then she climbed down and carried the board back across the city and scraped it into the far pit, and lifted the boards off the near one, which she had opened only once since the spring her husband went to Kamvamvil, for a shield, and took out what she needed. She beat goat hair into it, and sharp sand, not too much. By the time she was back on the ladder the sky over the wood had gone the colour of the lime.

She laid it in three coats, each one pressed hard into the letters so that nothing would lie hollow behind it. Nanjijim held the lamp until the daylight made it useless, and then went on holding it.

When she had done, the gate of Tatunde said what was due on grain and salt and spices, and that no one should carry horses, iron, bronze or timber out of the realm. Then it said nothing for the width of two hands, white and smooth. Then it said that whoever bribed a collector would lose his hand.

Good lime goes on hardening for years after it is laid, long after the hand that laid it has been washed and folded, drawing something out of the air that no one has ever seen, until it is very nearly stone again. Fifteen years, the treaty said. Najige came down the ladder and stood back in the street to look, beside her son, and thought that her husband's lime would see out fifteen years without trouble. She thought it might see out a good deal more than that. And whatever was under it would keep.
