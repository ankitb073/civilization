title: The Scribe Who Counted True: Namyili and the Last Tablets of Puvu
---
Six tablets are credited to [[per:47914|Namyili]]; four carry her name. The excavator Nanjil took them from the ground at five sites between AY 4890 and 4926: two at [[set:12|Kabaf]], the capital where she worked, and one each at Kavu, Adhgumgi, Lamlivzhe and Nzhinduh. Kabaf lies today inside my own Khishkhis, and the record traces my state's line of predecessors back to the Khishkhizi that took thirty-seven of her country's districts in 1835. I owe her a fair hearing.

The record of her person is thin. She was born in 1773, a woman of the Kumbumbi people, and served as scribe of [[pol:506|Puvu]] from 1793, when she was twenty, until she died of natural causes in 1846, aged seventy-three. It names no parents, husband or children, and is silent on her training and face. It names no other scribe of Puvu between the death of Nabule in 1796 and her own, and only one other woman among the empire's scribes, who served from 1544 to 1594. Of the twelve documents the record assigns to Puvu between 1793 and 1846, six are hers.

## The king's voice

She took office a year after [[per:13888|Nashyidi]], a year her junior, came to the throne. Her first surviving tablet, written at thirty, ended the [[evt:13946|Puvu–Kavu War]] in 1803. It speaks, as treaties did, in the king's voice: "The storm-god of Puvu went before me, and Kavu bowed down." Kavu gave up the land of Lude, became Puvu's "servant", and was to bring "12 talents of silver" a year for thirty-three years. The record is less grand. Puvu lost the first two battles before Kavu, the second under Nashyidi himself, and won the war having lost 2,990 men to Kavu's 1,424. Kavu was annexed by Nilu in 1817, fourteen years into the tribute. The tablet's edges are scorched by fire. Puvu had sacked Kavu the year before and burned its archives, too early to touch a tablet of 1803; Nilu burned them again in 1816. The record does not say which fire marked her clay.

Her two other treaties also close wars Puvu won: Lamlivzhe in 1828, which made Kashunji a "servant" after the [[evt:14176|First Kashunji–Puvu War]], and Nzhinduh in 1836, a silver tablet whose single clause is a ten-year truce ending the [[evt:14269|Holy War of Nambe against Puvu]]. The defeats were written by others: the Treaty of Yave (1829) by Natsunge, a scribe of Luthe, and the Treaty of Shyih (1835) by Nakadhash, a scribe of Khishkhizi. The victor's scribe held the stylus.

## The count at Adhgumgi

Her muster roll for the Battle of Adhgumgi in 1814 lists eight towns. Kabave, as the roll spells Kabaf, sent 4,717 men in chariot crews; the total is 12,257, exactly the strength the record gives for Puvu that day. One line concerns ordinary men: "Deserters to be branded and returned to their towns."

Nambude, a historian born in the same year as she and in service from the same year, wrote in 1823 that the enemy had gathered "1,330 men" and that "The scribes counted 773 enemy dead; we lost 26." The record gives Khanji 642 men and 234 losses, and Puvu 51. At the next battle there, in 1815, Nashyidi's stele boasts "He killed 800 soldiers"; the record counts 122 losses. That stele and her roll came out of the same ground in 4890. Her own annals give the 1814 battle one line and no figure: "There was a battle at Adugumgi; the gods gave the victory."

Her lawsuit of 1823 shows what the peace of 1815 meant on the ground. Nankashu, son of Nambide, a man of Khanji, asked for his father's "275 measures of sown land". The steward answered: "No private claim survives the treaty." The claim was dismissed, and he paid the court's costs. No text of that treaty survives in the record; Nambude put the cession at 13 districts, the record at 6.

## Annals of a shrinking house

Her last work, the Annals of Puvu, AY 1800–1841, was finished when she was sixty-eight. In those years Puvu fought 24 battles; she mentions six. Of Nashyidi's reign, over by then, she wrote defeat plainly: at Tadüfu in 1823 and again in 1824, "the day was lost." Of Nakagum III, the king she served as she wrote, she records only victories. The six defeats of his reign are absent, among them his own at Dadifi in 1828 and his general Nandigume's at Yave in 1829, where Puvu lost 2,616 men to 522 and Nandigume fell. At Divnüzith Puvu lost in 1829 and won in 1835; only the victory is entered. In 1841, the year she finished, the priest Natadtsu, copying omens for Nakagum himself, named "Nakagum, whose army Lude scattered near Tadüfu." A loss could be spoken at that court. In her annals it was not.

The treaties fare likewise. Yave, which bound Puvu to 95 talents of silver a year for twenty-three years, becomes "Peace was sworn". Shyih, which ceded thirty-seven districts and added 96 talents a year for thirty-six years, "was sworn, and the land had rest." In 1803 her tablet had promised Kabaf 12 talents a year; now Kabaf owed 191. The survey of the 1820s counted 263 districts and 4,744,430 people; that of the 1830s, 145 districts and 2,918,799, after Shyih and the secession of Yizhe in 1839, which took 81 more. Her last entry reads: "Yizhe came up against the land."

## After the house fell

In 1842 the empire broke into three. Of the eight towns on her muster roll, Adunambi, Shavi and Kalunzhe had been ceded at Shyih; four more passed to the new kingdoms of Ndulamlule and Tadudüvi; only Kabave stayed with Puvu. The record keeps her in office until her death, four years after her state ended, but no tablet of hers from those years survives.

She was not a free witness. She wrote in her kings' voices and kept their silences. Yet where she counted, she counted true, and when a man was refused his father's fields, she set it down without softening. Among Puvu's last voices, hers is the one I would trust with a number.
