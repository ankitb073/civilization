title: Mercy Stone

She had counted them out, and so she counted them back.

Tothiluesh'a stood where she had stood for most of her life, under the reed awning at the north gate of [[set:78|Bayitl]], where the farmers brought their sacks to be weighed. Her scale was folded in its box at her feet. In her hands was a cord of knotted wool, the kind she used when a caravan was too long to hold in her head: a large knot for every ten, a small knot for every one left over. Twelve days before, she had tied that cord as the army marched out behind the young general Uetho, and it had come to fifteen hundred and nineteen. She had not meant anything by the counting. Things went past her in a line, and her hands counted them.

Now the line came back the other way, and it was not a line. It was men in clumps of four and five, men leaning on spears, men carried between two others on cloaks. Three chariots came in with nobody standing in them, the horses led by boys. Uetho walked beside his own chariot, which carried two of the wounded, and he did not look at the gate or at the women along the wall. He was twenty-seven. The general of the Mii, they would hear later, was a man called Ilshamov, twenty-nine; the two of them together had not lived as long as Tothiluesh'a had.

She tied her knots. Her fingers were steady. Her eyes went from face to face looking for one, and her fingers did not wait for her eyes.

When the last of them had come in, a boy with a bandaged hand walking by himself and saying sorry to nobody in particular, she ran the cord twice through her fingers, the way she would weigh a doubtful sack twice. Thirteen hundred and twenty-nine.

She did the sum standing there, because the sum was easier than the other thing. A hundred and ninety had not come back.

Her son Esiesh Tlie had not come back.

* * *

By evening the market had decided that the hired men had broken first. The salt-seller said so, and the potter's wife, and a man who had not been within a day's walk of the steppe said it loudest of all. Tothiluesh'a had counted the men who went out and the men who came home, and nothing in the cord said who had run. A count tells you how many. It is not made to tell you why.

What the men who came home did say, every one of them, was that the enemy had been more than four to one. It was true, though nobody in the city knew the figure yet: the king of [[pol:694|Mii]] had put six thousand six hundred and thirty-nine men on the steppe against their fifteen hundred.

"Ten Rain has come for Three Reed," said the salt-seller, who liked a saying better than a fact. The king of Mii was Ma'sha To'a, Ten Rain. The king of [[pol:653|Bayitl]] was [[per:15574|Yi Foyatl]], Three Reed, who had been set on his father's throne at seven years old. Tothiluesh'a remembered it. She had been nineteen, and had stood in this same market to watch them carry the boy past, and had thought him very small for so much gold. This was the fortieth year of his reign. He had a grown son of his own now, and the rain had come.

"Reeds lie down in rain," she said. "Afterwards they stand up."

She did not know why she had said it. It was a thing her mother used to say, about the canal reeds after a storm, and about widows.

That night she did not sleep. She took her stones out of their box and set them on the floor in their order: the grains, the quarter, the half, the one [[cur:253|Ievi]], the five, the ten, the thirty, black and smooth and notched on their bellies by her father's knife. Before you weigh anything, her father had said, weigh your weights. She hung the beam from the roof-pole and tried them against each other by lamplight, the one against the two halves, the five against five ones, the ten against the two fives, until the oil was nearly gone. They were true. They had always been true.

What came to her of Esiesh Tlie was not his face. His face would not come. What came was the ring she had given him on the day he married: plain silver, the thickness of a reed-stem. She had weighed it before she gave it. Two Ievi and a quarter, less a grain. His father's ring had been that weight, and she had pared the new one at the edge with her shears until the beam stood level, because it seemed to her that a son should begin exactly where his father had.

* * *

When the Mii came into the city five days later, she did not see how. Nobody she knew saw how. There was shouting at the far end of the lanes, and then smoke, and then the shouting was everywhere and the smoke was the sky.

The roofs of Bayitl were reed and palm-rib laid over beams, and they burned the way a dry field burns, fast and loud, the sparks lifting in sheets. When the beams went, the mud-brick walls beneath them leaned and folded down into the lanes. Tothiluesh'a sat in her house with the box of stones on her knees and listened to her street come down. The priests of [[mov:25|Mie'to'ieshio]] had never had much to say about the end of the world, and in the temple precinct, she heard later, they did not begin now. They stood at the doors of the shrines and let the soldiers past, each god in its place and with its due, and the soldiers took the lamps.

In the afternoon three men came to her door. They wore iron at their belts and their faces were black from the fires, and when they spoke it was her own tongue, the vowels pulled a little flatter than hers, the same jokes underneath.

"Who weighs here?" said the one in front. "They said the weigher of the north gate lives here."

She did not ask who had said so. She had a fair idea, and found that she did not blame him.

"I weigh," she said.

* * *

They had cleared the square inside the north gate, where her awning had been. The awning was ash now, and so was the old thorn-tree that had shaded it. In the middle of the square, on cloaks spread over the paving, the silver of Bayitl was coming in.

The man in front was called Mozhan. He was perhaps twenty-five, with a burn across the back of one hand that he kept looking at as if it belonged to someone else. "Our weigher's dead," he said. "On the steppe. The men won't have the captains weigh it, and they won't have each other weigh it. A town weigher they'll take." He looked at her box. "You weigh in Ievi."

"I weigh in Ievi."

"We count in Pi'a."

"Twenty-nine of mine," said Tothiluesh'a, "make twenty-eight of yours."

He frowned. "So your Ievi is light."

"Your Pi'a is heavy," she said. "It comes out the same in the pan."

For a moment, against everything, he laughed.

She hung her beam from a spear-shaft laid across two broken posts, and began.

Cups. Hair-rings. Coils of silver wire that had been somebody's savings, rubbed bright where somebody had counted them in the dark. A child's anklet with the bells still on it: a Ievi and a half, which was what a measure of grain cost at her gate that year. Earrings. A temple lamp with the soot still in it. She called every weight aloud in Ievi and then in Pi'a, and Mozhan tied knots in a cord exactly like hers, and she wished she had not noticed that. When a piece looked doubtful she drew it across her touchstone and read the streak. All afternoon the silver came, and all afternoon the beam swung and slowed and stood, and the numbers left her mouth as clean as they had ever left it.

A bracelet came onto the cloak that she knew. It was Imas's, from three doors down. Imas had worn it to the grain gate on every market day since her wedding, as if to make a point, and Imas herself was standing now at the edge of the square among the women who had come to watch their things go, and she was looking at Tothiluesh'a.

The streak on the touchstone was clean and bright. One word would do it. Lead under a silver skin, Tothiluesh'a could say; she had seen enough of it in her time. They would throw the thing in the dust, and Imas would have it back by dark.

Her name meant mercy stone. Her father had laughed about that all his life. A stone that shows mercy, he used to say, is a false stone. I named you for the stone.

"Good silver," she said. "Four Ievi and a half. In Pi'a, four and a third."

She did not look at the edge of the square again.

Toward evening an old priest came in from the temple precinct with two soldiers behind him carrying the last of the shrine silver in a basket. He watched it go into the pan.

"We have taught for a long time that the wise withdraw from wealth," he said to her quietly, while the beam settled. "It seems the gods mean us to be very wise this year."

Then the men who had been on the steppe came forward with their pouches, the things they had taken off the field before they ever saw the city, and she weighed those too. Belt-fittings. A knife with silver wire wound round the hilt. A ring.

She knew it before the beam stopped moving. She knew it the way you know a step on the stair. Plain silver, the thickness of a reed-stem, with a bright pared place at the edge where the shears had been.

"Two Ievi and a quarter," she said, "less a grain." Her voice did not change. She had been doing this all her life. "In Pi'a, two and a sixth."

Mozhan tied his knots. The ring went onto the heap with the rest.

* * *

At dusk they divided it. A heap for the king of Mii went off first under guard, and then the men's shares were weighed out one by one against her stones, every man watching every other man's pan. It was dark before the last share was done, and the smoke over the city had turned from black to brown to a dull red where the lanes still burning lit it from below.

Mozhan held out a piece of cut silver. "For the weighing."

"I'll take the ring instead," she said. "The plain one. From the steppe."

He had to go through his own share to find it, and untie it from the rest, and then he stood looking from the ring to her.

"This is more than the fee."

"By a quarter Ievi and a little," she said. "Put them in the pans."

He set the ring in one pan and the cut silver in the other, and the beam leaned toward the ring, as she had known it would. She turned back the hem of her skirt, where five nights before she had sewn a sliver of silver into the cloth, the last of her own. She bit through the thread and laid the sliver beside the cut silver. Now the beam leaned the other way. She took her shears from the box and pared the sliver, once, and again, a curl so thin it lifted in the heat off the burning lanes, until the beam stood level and still.

He could have told her to keep her sliver. She watched him think of it. Then she watched him look at her face and think better of it, and he took the silver, all of it, and put the ring into her hand.

"Now we're quits," she said.

He nodded. He turned to go, and turned back. "Our weigher was my mother's brother," he said. "A hundred and forty-six of ours, on the steppe."

"A hundred and ninety of ours."

"I know," Mozhan said. "We counted yours too."

It was her number. Of everything that had happened since the gate, it was the one thing the two of them had weighed alike.

* * *

She walked home through the north quarter in the dark, with the box of stones under one arm and the ring shut in her other hand. Where her street had been there was a long low bank of ash with the ends of beams standing out of it, still ticking with heat. Later people would say that the fire had never reached the tablet rooms of the palace, as though that were a comfort, and perhaps to someone it was.

She sat down on what had been her doorstep. She did not need the stones. She had known what the ring weighed since before her son was married. She opened the box anyway, and hung the scale from the end of a timber standing up out of the ash, and set the ring and one grain in the first pan, and the two Ievi and the quarter in the other.

The beam swung, and slowed, and stood. Two Ievi and a quarter, less a grain. It was not much silver. She knew exactly how much it was.
