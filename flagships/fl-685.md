In AY 4873 the Assembly of [[pol:1674|Mbilu]] voted 259 to 139 against a peace its president had signed with his own hand. No surviving Assembly debate in the archive records a heavier defeat for a motion. The editors of the proceedings (PAL·PD·00052) note only that the government proceeded anyway. The president was [[per:46223|Nakhumbèn]], and it was his second peace in three years with the small kingdom of [[pol:1974|Mana]], which ceased to exist the following year.

Of the man himself the record keeps little. He was born in 4822, a Khumbivi; his name means "drought". It names no parent, wife or child, and no office before the presidency, which he won at forty-eight in 4870 and gave up at the regular election of 4876. He died a natural death in 4895, aged seventy-three. Nearly everything that survives of him comes from two of his six years in office. A translator's warning: his own side's papers call the battlefield Ini and Mana's capital Shivindua; Mana's writers say Ene and Tivintukhe, and call him "Nakhomden of Mdel". Same places, same man.

## A peace that lasted a year

He inherited a war. His predecessor [[per:46152|Nakhange]] had carried the declaration against Mana in 4867 by 227 votes to 66, and by 4870, when the general Nakhièvi beat Mana's army at Ene, the [[evt:47105|Mbilu–Mana War]] was won. The [[evt:47182|Treaty of Tivintukhe (AY 4870)]] is the earliest surviving document to carry Nakhumbèn's name (PAL·TR·02890). Mana gave up six districts, agreed to pay 1,847,703 Da "in ten annual instalments", placed "its foreign relations under the protection of Mbilu", and both parties undertook "to refrain from any act of force against one another for a period of 20 years." Mana's signatory was its king, [[per:45955|Mezhe Yof]], called the Unfortunate, who had already signed away twelve districts to Mbilu at Ene in 4847.

The twenty years ran out the next year. In 4871 the record has Mbilu lodge a colonial claim against Mana, its new protectorate, and the [[evt:47189|Second Mbilu Colonial War in Mana]] followed. Each side's historian blames the other in the same words. Mbilu's Nagième (PAL·CH·08235, 4877): "Mèzhè Yufu of Mane broke the peace and sent raiders across the border of Mbilu. Our president could not suffer the insult." The insulted president was Nakhumbèn, a year into his term. Mana's Ye Ash (PAL·CH·08236, 4879) turns it round: "Nakhomden of Mdel broke the peace and sent raiders across the border of Mana." The president was more candid than his chronicler. His public letter of 4873 (PAL·LT·01775) says plainly that "your Government laid before the world the Mbilu Claim against Mana", which he calls "a claim moderate in its terms and just in its foundations". On that much the record agrees with him: the war began with Mbilu's claim, not with raiders.

## Twice at Ini

Nakhumbèn then did what no president of Mbilu before him had done: he took the field himself. In 4872, aged fifty, he led 362,655 men against 9,786 under Mana's general [[per:46204|Vaelmoga]], a year his junior. At the [[evt:47195|Battle of Ene (AY 4872)]] Mbilu lost 431 and Mana 1,014. The next year he came back with 705,096, more than half the men Mbilu counted under arms at the end of the 4870s, against 10,193. At the [[evt:47202|Battle of Ene (AY 4873)]] Mbilu lost 130, Mana 563, and Vaelmoga was killed, at fifty. No other president of Mbilu has commanded twice.

The paper they left is richer than the battles. His own wires survive. In 4872 (PAL·TG·00145): "OUR LOSSES ABOUT 300 STOP ENEMY LOSSES ESTIMATED 1,300 STOP REQUEST REINFORCEMENTS TO EXPLOIT". In 4873 (PAL·TG·00146): "OUR LOSSES ABOUT 100 STOP ENEMY LOSSES ESTIMATED 800 STOP MORALE EXCELLENT". Both trim his own dead and swell the enemy's, as such wires do, but not by much. The farther the story travelled from the field, the more it grew. The Mbilu Herald (PAL·NP·00676) gave the second battle as 1,105 enemy dead to 91. Nagième's chronicle gave Vaelmoga nearly twice the men he had, 18,985 and 19,774, and claimed 2,499 enemy dead against 234, then 1,388 against 70. Ye Ash wrote that at the first Ene, "after a night of rain that bogged the guns, our men could not hold the field. It cost us 269 men, and them 1,144"; at the second, "through the blunders of the general staff, our army withdrew in good order. We lost 150; the enemy, 345." Of all who counted the dead at Ini, the man with most reason to boast came closest to the record.

His letter is another matter. There Vaelmoga's "army ceased to exist as an army". The record has him lose one man in eighteen, and scores the day as all but indecisive; Ye Ash's "withdrew in good order" is nearer the mark. Yet the same letter holds the most generous sentence in the file, written of the general he had just killed: "I record that he did not run. It is the only good thing I can record of the cause he served."

## The terms of order

[[set:13|Tivintukhe]] was sacked in 4873, for the eleventh time, and its archives burned, as they had in the sack of 4869. The letter blames the defenders: "their incendiaries were in the record office before ours were in the street." The record says only that the archives burned while Mbilu's army took the city. Whose torch it was, it does not say; the president was hardly a disinterested witness.

The [[evt:47204|Treaty of Tivintukhe (AY 4873)]] (PAL·TR·02892), signed for Mana by Mezhe Yof's son [[per:46024|Eh Tle]], king since the year before, took two districts and exactly the same 1,847,703 Da, again over ten years, while the instalments of the first still had years to run. The record reckons each indemnity at 27,346 kilograms of silver. Mana's last survey, taken as the kingdom ended, finds 32,254 in its treasury.

The Assembly still had to ratify it. The president opened: "The terms are hard, but the alternative is ruin." A member, Nabizhi, answered, "Mane will rebuild, and our sons will fight this war again", and the house laughed. The president came back: "Greed now would only make a new war." Another, Nagu, asked: "Who will govern the new provinces, and at whose cost?" So the ruin he feared was the next war, and Nabizhi feared the same war from the other side: one thought a harder peace would breed it, the other a softer one. Nagu doubted that even two districts were worth their keep. Nabizhi wanted more, not less, and Nagième's chronicle later supplied it on paper, turning two districts into four. The letter had already answered them: "These are not the terms of revenge. They are the terms of order." I find this the most interesting moment of his life: a victorious commander defending a small peace against a parliament that thought it too small or not worth the cost, and losing by 120 votes.

The wars had costs at home. Grain stood at 2.16 Dathu the measure when war was declared in 4867 (PAL·NP·00660); the Herald of 4873 gives 5.91. The president knew it: "I am told it is near six [[cur:1094|Dathu]] the measure, and that is a scandal in a victorious country." They had costs for the conquered too. In 4880 Èshu Da, son of Gègu, "formerly a subject of Mane", asked a court at Mbilu for his father's 175 acres, taken under the treaty (PAL·LS·00780). Counsel for the Crown answered: "The plaintiff did not swear allegiance within the year." He kept the fields as a tenant, paying 55.4 Dathu a year.

## After Mana

The small peace was overtaken within a year, though not by war. In 4874 the Kingdom of Mana collapsed, thirty-four years after its founding, and by 4876 its last ten districts, Tivintukhe and Ene among them, had passed to Mbilu. The record logs them as expansion: no battle, no treaty, no signature. Eh Tle died in 4878, aged thirty-eight. Whatever Nakhumbèn meant by order, by the end of his term it was simply Mbilu's.

He left office in 4876, at fifty-four, to another [[per:46251|Nakhumbèn]], seven years younger, who fought the [[evt:47217|Holy War of Fuvu against Mbilu]]. Of his remaining nineteen years the record says nothing.

In 4909 the archaeologist Nambili found eleven destruction layers at Tivintukhe. The uppermost, dated to 4873, is "burnt timbers and scattered arrowheads" (PAL·EX·00003). That layer belongs to his presidency. So do the most honest wires from Ini, the kindest words about an enemy in this file, and a peace his Assembly would not ratify, which his republic overtook within three years. He was no peacemaker: he ended a war his own government had begun. But he counted the dead more honestly than his chroniclers did, and he asked for less than the Nabizhis of his Assembly wanted.
