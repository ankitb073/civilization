title: Jamfin Shimin of I'ith
---
The lower town of [[set:176|Azha]] was still burning when Inashmi came up through the gate, and the smoke lay along the streets at the height of a man's chest, so that the soldiers coming down past her with their sacks seemed to be wading.

She had been a scribe for twenty-nine years. She had written down cities taken and cities handed back, ransoms, dowries, the weight of tribute in silver and in grain, but she had never walked into a city on the day it was being emptied, and she found that she saw it the way a scribe would. The men carried silver, cloth, bronze pots, a bolt of red wool, a door. A boy no older than her sister's son Lilanmi went by with an unlit lamp in each hand, holding them away from his body as if they might still be hot. Nobody carried a tablet.

Three nights before, in the camp under the walls, she had written the day's figures by lamplight while the surgeons worked behind the next screen. Of the king's men under Yi Ta'a, seven hundred and seventy-nine lost. Of the [[pol:1120|Iti]], four thousand one hundred and eighty-one. The Iti had come out with more than thirteen thousand men against fewer than ten thousand, and had broken anyway, and she had sat with the reed above the clay for a long moment because the two numbers did not look as if they belonged to the same afternoon. Then she had written them down. That was the work.

The dust was worse than the smoke. It was the third dry year in this country. The canals she had crossed on the way up were cracked mud, and the fires had nothing to fight them; the hooves of the [[pol:1116|Witti]] horses lifted a grey powder of ash and earth that got into the creases of her knuckles and would not wash out, because there was no water to wash in.

* * *

A man of the king's household told her to find a room with a door. She found the record-house by its smell, which was the smell of every record-house she had ever worked in, dry clay and lamp-soot and old reed, and she stood in the doorway breathing it, because it was the first thing in Azha that smelled of anything but burning.

The door had been broken in. Two shelves were down and the floor was a field of shards. An old man sat cross-legged in the middle of it with a lapful of pieces, fitting one broken edge to another.

—They were looking for silver, he said, without looking up. He said it in her own tongue, with the flat vowels of the old imperial offices, from the years before the empire broke and its provinces became kingdoms with kings of their own, his and hers. —They pulled down two shelves and found clay. Then they went and burned the potters' street.

—Nobody burns what nobody wants, Inashmi said.

—Nobody wants what nobody can read.

He had four pieces together. She could read his letters, slowly, upside down; she had learned them as a girl, when there was one empire and one set of shelves. "The wells ran dry and the river shrank to a stream. Grain stood at 0.872 Iscuax the measure." A priest's record of last year. He set it down on the floor with great care, as if the sky might come and check it.

—What are you here to write?

—The peace.

He looked at what she held under her arm, wrapped in wool, the weight of it plain in the way she carried it.

—On bronze.

—On bronze.

He got up, slowly, and cleared the table with his sleeve, and poured water for her from a jar in the corner: a finger's depth in the bottom of a cup. She drank half and gave the rest back, and he looked at her then with the first interest he had shown.

—Moderation in all things, he said. —It's what our priests teach. This sky has taken it to heart.

* * *

The hall on the citadel had been restored ten years before by the man whose city this was. The plaster was still pale, the arches were sound, and only the eastern end had taken the smoke. Witti officers sat on the Iti benches with their boots on the painted floor.

Isakha was thirty-nine. Inashmi had written for him since he came to the throne at twenty-seven, and three years ago she had stood behind him when he went over to Kievi, and had gone over with him, as far as her mouth. He dictated walking, the way he always had.

—The land of I'ith, with Bathshi. Seventeen districts.

She wrote it. Five times in six years the two armies had met at Bathshi, and now the town was a word on her clay. The kingdom had taken its name from that land. They were taking the land, and leaving the kingdom its name, and its capital, and its smoke.

One of his men read the sum for the costs of the war off a tablet of his own: two million, one hundred and thirty-seven thousand, seven hundred and sixty, in the Iti coin. Inashmi asked him to say it again, and he did, and she wrote it, and checked it against his tablet, which was also the work. The merchants of her country to trade in their markets free of all tolls. A truce of fifteen years. Raiders handed over for judgement.

Then the opening, for which she had left a space. Peace is granted by Isakha, king of Iethithi, to — and she wrote the name the soldiers used, and after the name, from habit, as she had written it in other peaces for other kings, she began the sign for king.

—No, said Isakha.

She lifted the reed.

—Five years ago at Bathshi he beat me, he said. He did not say it loudly. —I lay in my tent that night and heard his men singing across the river. He is a king in this hall. On my bronze he is a man of his country.

She smoothed the clay with her thumb and wrote "of I'ith".

* * *

That night she cut the bronze in the record-house by two lamps, and the old man held a third when she needed it nearer. The iron graver had to be held low and drawn, never pushed, and every letter cost a breath. She began with the gods, as the form required: "In the name of Kievi and of Mie'to'ieshio." The old cult, as her letters spelled it, which her people had kept until three years ago and his king kept still.

He read over her shoulder.

—That isn't his name.

—It's what our soldiers call him.

—Tlamfin, he said.

She tried. The sound at the start of it was a breath pushed past the sides of the tongue, and her tongue would not make it, and her alphabet had no letter for it. She had cut the nearest thing, which was not near.

He took a coin from the cloth at his waist and laid it on the table in the lamplight: a silver [[cur:604|Iscuax]], the king's head facing left, and over the head a veil. On the other side an eagle spread its wings above the mark of Azha's mint. He set his thumbnail under the first letter of the legend.

—That. And this, at the end, is king.

It was nearly her own word for it. She took from her purse the coin she had been paid in at home, the new silver of her own king, struck only last year, and laid it beside his. The same eagle on the back, wings spread. On the front, Isakha in a wreath of leaves, looking left, as if the two kings had turned their faces the same way to avoid each other. Hers was lighter by nearly half.

—Why is he veiled?

—The wise withdraw from wealth and appetite, he said, the way a child says a lesson. —A king who puts his face on silver puts a veil over it first. So the silver knows he isn't looking at it.

—What does his name mean?

—Nine Storm.

—In my tongue it would be Lampin Imin, she said, and heard it, and was surprised. —It's the same nine. Worn down differently.

—Then write that.

—Then it would be another man. Every child in my city would know what it meant, and nobody would know who it was.

So she left Jamfin in the bronze, which was as near as her letters could come to the sound and nowhere near the man, and the first line stood as it stands still: "Peace is granted by Isakha, king of Iethithi, to Jamfin Shimin of I'ith, on these conditions."

When she came to the second article he asked how many it was, and she told him. Then, because she had worked beside treasurers for twenty-nine years and could not help it, she reckoned it aloud against the priest's figure on the floor: at a little under one of these coins the measure, the sum came to nearly two and a half million measures of grain, in the third dry year.

At home two years ago they had sung a new hymn in the square, and she had stood in the crowd and sung it with them. "Take less than you are owed; the wise man asks for less, and wins." She did not say it aloud. She cut the figure, every sign of it, and blew the bronze dust out of the strokes.

The old man picked up his coin and turned it to the veiled side.

—Every one of them with his face, he said. —Well. At least he won't have to watch them go.

* * *

On the twenty-sixth day of Shi the two kings swore in the hall before Kievi and [[mov:25|Miyiteyixio]]. [[per:25489|Tlamfin Ximin]] came veiled, as he was on his silver. He was forty-eight, a year younger than Inashmi, and his voice behind the cloth was dry and quite steady. He did not look at the bronze; or if he looked, she could not tell, which she supposed was the purpose of a veil.

When the oaths were done she cut the last lines while the hall emptied around her: the place, the day, the year of her king's reign. "Let it be read aloud in the markets of both lands." Under that, because the form required a witness, her own name. In twenty-nine years she had cut a great many names in bronze and had never once cut her own, and her hand, which was steady for kings, was not quite steady for it. "Witnessed and written by Inashmi."

* * *

The market lay below the citadel at the end of the burnt street, and there was nothing in it to sell. A few women stood with empty baskets because it was where they had always stood. A dog lay in the shade of a fallen awning. One of Yi Ta'a's captains had brought twenty horsemen to make a crowd, and a crowd came, slowly, out of the doorways.

Inashmi read the bronze in her own tongue, as it was written, in her reading voice, which carried. She watched the faces not understand it. The captain watched them too.

—Tell it to them in theirs, he said to the old man, who had come down with her carrying the bronze in its wool.

The old man held the tablet up to the light and read it slowly, putting her words into his own tongue as he went. She found she could follow him; she had kept more of it than she knew. Kievi and Miyiteyixio. Isakha, king of the Witti. Then a breath, and the name as it ought to be said, with the sound she had no letter for, and after the name the word for king.

She heard it. A woman with an empty basket heard it and shut her eyes. The captain heard a name, and nothing in it he knew.

The old man read the rest exactly. The land and Bathshi and the seventeen districts. The two million and more of the coins with the veiled face, at which nobody made a sound. The merchants of the Witti free of all tolls, at which a woman spat into the dust and the captain pretended not to see. Fifteen years. Raiders handed over. Her own name, which in his mouth came out softer than she had ever heard it.

The captain turned to her.

—Did he read it as it's written?

The old man was folding the wool back over the bronze, corner by corner, the way you wrap a thing that has a long way to go.

—Word for word, said Inashmi.

It was the only untrue thing she said in [[set:176|Azha]], and she did not write it down.
