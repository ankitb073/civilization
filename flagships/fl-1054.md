title: Less Butter

By the third day of mourning the kitchen of the palace at [[set:68|Poyonga]] had worked out who the heir was.

"It's you," Evsheth said. She was fourteen and had been crying on and off since the wailing began upstairs, and she had come out the far side of it into the country where everything is funny. "Think. The king's gone. His father's gone, his mother's gone, the cousins are gone. Old Lady Eloth went last year. So who's left who's been in this house the longest? Who's the eldest of the house?"

"Peel," said Ongolath.

"Queen Ongolath." The girl bowed to the onions. "Queen of the long spoon. Lady of the—"

Ongolath rapped her knuckles with the spoon, not hard, and Evsheth laughed and wept at once and went on peeling. Down the table Efpen, who carried the water, laughed too, and then looked at the stair door in fright, as if upstairs could hear a kitchen laugh. Nobody from upstairs had come down in three days except to fetch trays.

It was a bad joke, and Ongolath did not like how true it was. She had come down from the hill pastures at twelve, in the reign of the old queen Neneth, with a cough and a bundle and her mother's knife, and she had been in this kitchen forty-nine years. She had cooked for a queen and four kings. She had learned their deaths the way a kitchen learns them, which is by the trays. Bemjen's had come back half-eaten for a season, then barely touched, then not at all. Naruen's had come back clean to the end, because Naruen ate the way he did everything, quickly and without remark, until one morning there was nothing to come back. Last year it was Eloth, who took her millet with honey and sent it back every time for more honey, until she did not.

And then the boy.

She still called him that in her head, though [[per:16660|Nemnan]] had been thirty-one, and king of [[pol:669|Tobejenga]] for five years, and had beaten the Yarel men at [[evt:16724|Yaaril]] and come home with [[evt:16725|that whole country under the crown]]. When he was six he had a fever, and his nurse brought him down to the kitchen because it was the warmest room in the palace, and Ongolath made him what her mother made in the hills for a sick child: road broth. You crumbled hard curd, the grey cakes that herders dry on flat stones all summer, into boiling water. You added a fistful of barley and a pinch of salt, and laid a lump of butter on top to melt into a yellow eye. It was food for a cold night on the move. He drank two bowls and fell asleep on the flour sacks. After that, whenever he was ill or miserable or merely nine years old, he came down for it, and when he was a man, and then a king, he sent down for it. It was the one dish of her hearth that the rest of the palace had never learned to want.

This summer the fever came back and sat in his chest for eleven days. Every evening the broth went up, and every morning it came down with a skin on it. The queen, who was twenty-six and had no child, sent down for nothing at all. On the twelfth morning there was no tray.

Since then Ongolath had made the broth each evening anyway. It was not a rite any priest had taught her. The priests of [[mov:2|Jenro]] had their own business with the dead, up at the royal tombs, where they sang the wheel over him and argued, the steward said, about which god to put on his stone. Ongolath's business was smaller. When the kitchen had gone to bed she poured the boy's bowl into the hearth, a spoonful at a time, so that [[dei:18|Eva]], who takes whatever is given to the fire, might carry it wherever it was wanted now.

Upstairs the hall was full of men. The steward came down twice a day to say how many to feed and to complain about the cost of feeding them. Grain stood at over two [[cur:356|Nalshe]] of silver the measure, near twice what it had been when the boy's father took the throne, and the storerooms had never been made good since the Yarel men [[evt:16699|sacked the city]] seven years ago and burned the archives. There were generals in the hall, and priests, and the old officers of the household, and every one of them, said the steward, knew exactly who ought to be king. That was the trouble. Not one of them was of the house. There was no house left.

* * *

On the third night she was alone with the fire and the bowl when a man spoke from the yard door.

"Is that going in?"

He had come past the woodstack, the way the carters came, and she had not heard him. He was not a carter. He wore a soldier's cloak the colour of mud and no mourning ash, and he was somewhere in his middle thirties, with a long jaw and the wind-cut look that the hill men had and the palace men lost. He was not looking at her. He was looking at the bowl.

"It's the king's," Ongolath said.

"The king isn't hungry." He came and held his hands out to the hearth, though the night was warm. "I am. I've been in that hall since first light. Your meat goes past me on trays all day to men who talk with their mouths full, and none of it stops."

"This isn't for eating."

"Everything's for eating." He bent and breathed over the bowl, and something in his face came loose. "Road broth. I haven't had road broth since I came down out of the hills." He looked at her for the first time. "How many nights have you been giving it to the fire?"

"This is the third."

"Then Eva's had two," he said. "Give him his share of this one, and give me the rest."

She should have sent him off. She was nobody, a cook, and he was by the look of him nobody either, and the hall upstairs, which was somebody, had been arguing for three days about what belonged to whom. Perhaps that was why she did it. Somebody in this house had to decide something. She took a spoonful from the top and gave it to the fire, and the butter spat and flared blue, and she handed him the bowl.

He did not take it to the bench. He squatted on his heels by the hearth the way herdsmen sit at a night fire, held the bowl in both hands and drank from the rim, fast, as if somebody might take it from him. She had seen men eat like that. They were men who had been hungry a long time and had not yet believed it was over.

He came up for breath. "Too much butter."

"He liked it so."

"He had butter to spare." He drank again. "My mother put in a thumbnail's worth. The rest was for selling."

In the hills, when a stranger came up to your fire, you did not ask him where he was from. You asked who had fed him, and the answer was always a woman's name. Ongolath had not asked it of anyone in forty-nine years, and the question rose in her as if it had been waiting all that time.

"Whose son are you?"

He laughed, without much in it. "You're the first today to ask it like that. Upstairs they've been asking all day whose son I am, and they mean my father, and his father, and so on back to the gods. I tell them I'm my own. They don't like it."

So he was one of the hall. She looked at the cloak again, and at his hands, which had held reins and something heavier than reins, and knew she ought to be afraid of him, and found she was too tired.

"I didn't ask for your father," she said.

He was quiet so long that she thought he would not answer. The fire settled. Across the yard a horse stamped in the chariot sheds. Then he told her his mother's name.

It was a hill name, a plain one, the kind given to a girl who would be out with the goats before light all her life. He said it carefully, like a man setting down a full jar.

"Is she living?"

"She was at the thaw."

"Will you send for her?"

"No." He turned the empty bowl in his hands. "If they know her name, they'll go up there. Some of them will want to make a great lady of her and sit her on a chair in this place and stare at her. Some will want to cut her throat to spite me. She wants neither. She wants her goats." He looked up. "So now you have it, and nobody else in this city does."

"Why give it to me?"

"You asked properly."

She took the bowl from him. It was clean; he had run his finger round it. "And what do they call you?"

"[[per:16675|Balothberoyen]]."

Swift wolf. "What sort of hill woman names her son for the wolf?"

"One who lost a lot of goats to one." For the first time he smiled. "She said if she couldn't keep it out of the fold, she'd keep it in the house."

At the yard door he stopped. "Same tomorrow," he said. "Less butter."

* * *

Nobody in the kitchen saw how it was done. The steward came down at midday, white about the mouth, and said there would be a king at supper, and the king was Balothberoyen, and if anybody had an opinion about that they could have it in the yard, quietly, where the walls were thick. Within the hour all of [[set:68|Poyonga]] knew that [[evt:16733|the throne had been taken]], and no one could say whose son had taken it.

In the afternoon the young scribe Lorlashan came down, as he often did, because the kitchen was warm and he liked to eat standing up. He was twenty-six and had been scribe of the palace since the year before the boy was crowned, and he carried a tablet wrapped in a wet cloth to keep the clay soft.

"They've given me the words for the stone," he said. "For the tomb. Do you want to hear them?"

She did not, but she nodded, and he unwrapped the tablet and read: "Nemnan, king of Tobejenga, son of Naruen, dwells in this tomb. May Volme protect his soul."

"Volme," said Ongolath. "He was frightened of the dark till he was ten. It was [[dei:19|Yenan]] he talked to, at the kitchen door, when the moon was up. He put Yenan on his own stone at Yarel. You told me so yourself."

"For Yenan, his lord," Lorlashan said. He had it by heart. "I know. The king says [[dei:14|Volme]]." He lowered his voice on *king*, the way everyone would for a while yet, as if testing its weight. "He says she has kept him alive this long, and she's the best he has to give, and he won't send the boy down there with no one at his side."

Ongolath thought about that. It was a soldier's gift, from a man who had very little else to give, and she found, to her surprise, that she could let it stand.

Lorlashan wrapped the tablet again. "One day I'll have to write his," he said. "Balothberoyen, king of Tobejenga, son of — son of what? Every stone says son of. I asked him this morning. He laughed at me." He looked at her sideways. "The guards say he was down here last night. Did he say anything? Where he comes from, who his people are? Anything I could put down?"

Ongolath thought of the name. It lay in her somewhere under the forty-nine years, small and plain, like a curd cake drying on a stone.

"He said the broth wanted less butter," she said.

The scribe waited. Then he sighed, and took the bowl of millet she pushed across the table, and ate it standing, and went back up the stair with nothing to put down.

* * *

That evening she made road broth, as she had every evening since the boy died. When it was ready she took a spoonful from the top and gave it to the fire, for Eva, and for the boy. The fat flared and went out.

The priests said that all things turn on a great wheel, and that what ends will begin again. Ongolath had never been sure what they meant by it. In a kitchen it meant that tomorrow somebody would be hungry.

She looked at the butter for a while. Then she cut a thumbnail of it, no more, the way a woman in the hills does who means to sell the rest, and sent the bowl up to the king.

**Author's note.** The record gives Balothberoyen no father and no mother, and his house begins and ends with him. The words on Nemnan's tomb are those of his epitaph, PAL·IN·04607; who chose its goddess the record does not say, but Nemnan's own stele at Yarel, PAL·IN·01216, was raised to Yenan, and Balothberoyen's of AY 2104, PAL·IN·01221, to "Volme, his lady." Lorlashan, who wrote PAL·CH·03094 in the ninth year of Balothberoyen, is real. The cook, the broth and the mother's name are mine. No epitaph of Balothberoyen survives; he outlived his kingdom by eighteen years.
