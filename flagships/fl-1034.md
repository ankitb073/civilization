title: Divide by Seven

On the last night the boy counted fires.

He lay at the lip of the heights with his greatcoat bunched under his chest and his rifle under the greatcoat, to keep the frost off the breech, and he counted the enemy's fires in the valley by tens, setting a pebble aside for each ten. When the pebbles ran out he started on the stars, to see which there were more of. Yidimin let him. He had done the same himself a year before, on the night before the [[evt:47603|Battle of Myixim]], and had learned only that a man who counts fires does not sleep.

"The fires win," the boy said.

"They usually do."

Everyone on the heights had a number, and most of the numbers were near enough. [[per:46522|Luwathkhu]] had fifty-seven thousand men on the stony ground above [[set:158|Shaathi]]. Below them, filling the valley and the plain beyond it with smoke, lay six times as many, and a new name had come up the road at their head: [[per:46557|Maapufnen]]. The men said the name over to one another the way you test a plank before you put your weight on it. Nobody knew anything about him except that he was not the general they had already met.

Yidimin's own village was an hour's climb behind them. His wife had barred the door and taken the child into the back room, and he had told her that the guns would not reach so far, which might even be true. Round the shoulder of the mountain the city kept its lamps burning all night. He could not see them from where he lay, only the yellow they made on the underside of the cloud.

"You dug there," the boy said. "In the city. My mother told me."

"Nine years ago."

"Did you find gold?"

"Ash," said Yidimin.

* * *

He had been twenty-five, a quarryman from the high country with a quarryman's back, and the excavator had hired forty men like him to take the citadel mound of Shaathi apart a basket at a time. In the hills it was the year 2522. The hills counted by the wheel, as his mother had taught him to count: all things turn, and what ends will begin again. Down in the city, where they had kept the faith of the line for three hundred years, the same year was 2648, and the clerk who paid the diggers wrote it that way on the wage sheets. Yidimin had found that funny for about a week, two years for one summer, and then he had got used to it, the way he got used to the city's bread.

They cut down through the cellars of the town into the old city, and through the old city into ash. There were four floors of it in the mound, one over another, each a hand or two thick, each with fallen brick in it and here and there a bead, a black seed of grain, a tooth. The excavator taught them to tell one burning from the next by its colour, and to letter a paper tag for every basket, trench and depth and layer, and that was how Yidimin learned to write: *burnt, fourth floor*, in his own square hand, on tags tied with string.

Then the stones came up.

They came out of the ash and out of the floors between, whole and broken, and on the flank of the mound there was a face of living rock, carved, that nobody could carry away, so they cleared the earth from it where it stood. A reader of dead scripts came up on the train to see them all, a thin old man who kept his coat buttoned to the throat at noon, and because Yidimin's trench was nearest, Yidimin held the lamp.

It was a strange thing to listen to. Each stone was cut in a different hand, some in letters the old man said nobody had written for thousands of years, but read aloud they all said the same thing. A king, or now and then a queen, had beaten somebody at Shaathi, and had killed so many, and asked a god to destroy the name of the enemy. Only the name of the place changed a little from stone to stone, as if feet had worn it down. The old man read them in the order they came out of the ground, so that every king was older than the one before: in Shate, in Shedi, in Shadi, in Shati, which was the oldest way of saying it and had meant the place of fish.

The rock face was the one Yidimin remembered. The old man set his finger under a line of it and read: "He killed 13,000 soldiers."

Then he laughed, not unkindly, and wiped the finger on his coat.

"Kings," he said. "Divide by six. Divide by seven. Nobody has ever killed thirteen thousand men at Shaathi."

* * *

"So it wasn't true," the boy said.

"It was a king's number." Yidimin turned onto his back. The cold came up out of the ground into his shoulder blades. "Go to sleep."

"What will they cut for us?"

"Nobody cuts words in stone any more. They print them."

"What will they print, then?"

Yidimin thought about it for so long that the boy began counting again.

"Whatever they like," he said.

* * *

It was the twentieth of Bishmi, in the year 2531 by the wheel. The guns began before there was light enough to see what they were firing at: a flat slapping down in the valley and then, a long breath later, the heights jumping into the air. On that ground a shell threw stone as well as iron. Yidimin's company lay behind a wall the herdsmen had built for sheep, and the wall came apart over them in pieces the size of a fist, and men who had not been touched by anything sent from [[pol:1963|Biniynge]] were killed by the high country itself.

He fired when he was told to fire. The valley was full of smoke and the smoke was full of men, and they came up the slope the way the tide comes up a beach, never all together and never all stopping. Some time in the morning the general rode along behind the wall on a grey horse with frost still in its mane. [[per:46522|Luwathkhu]] was a year younger than Yidimin; the whole army knew his age and was proud of it, the way a village is proud of a clever son. He had beaten the enemy at Myixim three years ago and been beaten by them every time since.

He made no speech. He leaned down to the captain and said, so that the nearest men heard it, "When the order comes, by companies. Nobody goes before his turn." Then he rode on along the wall, and the wall went on coming apart.

The order came in the early afternoon. They went back by companies as he had said, over the shoulder of the mountain, each company lying down to fire while the one behind it went, then getting up and going while the next lay down. It was the slowest thing Yidimin had ever done, and the loudest. He was the last of his file across the ridge. He looked back once and saw the sheep wall, and the stones of it scattered over the slope, and among the stones the greatcoats, more than he could count, and he did not try.

The boy was not at the roll that night.

* * *

At the railhead they found out that Yidimin could write, and set him in a goods shed with three clerks and a lamp to copy the company rolls into the army's book. Name, company, village. He lettered them as he had once lettered tags for baskets of ash, small and square, so that a stranger could read them. The names went round and round. There were only so many names in the hills, Eshueth and Nisluth and Kaünith, Shiiyuf, Lamfi, Iminak, and he wrote each of them so many times that by the second night it seemed to him the same few men had died over and over, a wheel of them, coming round.

He came to the boy's name on the third night, in another man's hand, and copied it out like the rest.

On the fourth night the senior clerk ran his pencil down the last column and added it, and added it again, and sat back.

"Thirteen thousand and thirty-nine," he said. "Lost."

Somebody whistled through his teeth. "And theirs?"

"Three or four thousand, they say." The clerk rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands. "Over there they'll make it more. Here they'll make it less."

Yidimin said nothing. He was thinking of a finger under a line of rock, and of a laugh, and he found that his own hand had stopped on the page and would not go on for a while.

* * *

The [[evt:47612|Treaty of Xath]] was sworn at Shaathi on the twelfth of Lashle. By it twenty-two districts of [[pol:1935|Niwidhithdhu]] passed to the Emperor [[per:46189|Shikhshin]], the city and the high country among them; there was to be a truce of twenty years; and the People's State itself, what was left of it, was to take the faith of the line. Yidimin heard the terms read out beside the well of his own village by a clerk from the city, a soft-voiced man who had worked for the People's State until that month and now worked for the Emperor, and who spoke the hill speech the way city people do, as if every word were a little too heavy to lift.

The clerk set up a table, and a ledger on the table. Everyone in the high country was to swear to the Emperor within the year, he said, or their fields would go to someone who had. He said it without pleasure and without regret, as you might read out the price of grain.

"Your Chairman signed it herself," he told an old woman who asked. "There is nothing to be done."

They stood in a line. When Yidimin came to the table the clerk asked his name and his village, and then the year of his birth.

"Two thousand four hundred and ninety-seven."

The clerk did not look up. There was a little table of figures pinned inside the ledger's cover, two columns in a neat hand, but he did not need it; he had done this too many times that week. "Two thousand six hundred and twenty-three," he said, and wrote it.

"That isn't my year."

"It is now. Your count started late, a hundred and twenty-six years after ours, so you add a hundred and twenty-six." He blotted the line. "The [[mov:58|Vishithmikh]] counts in a line, from the first year to the judgement at the end. Nothing comes round twice."

Yidimin swore. His wife swore after him. The clerk asked the child's year, and his wife said two thousand five hundred and twenty-five, and the clerk said two thousand six hundred and fifty-one and wrote it down, and the child, who was six, looked up at her father to see whether she had been made older and whether it was going to hurt.

"So we're old," his wife said that night. "We were born a hundred years ago."

"We're the same age," Yidimin said. "It's the years that got older."

She laughed, which he had not expected, and then she cried, which he had.

* * *

Before the year was out he walked down to [[set:158|Shaathi]].

The city had not burned. That surprised him more than anything, though he could not have said why. He had dug through four Shaathis and every one of them had ended in ash, and this one had simply been signed over at a table: the shops open, the trains running, a new flag over the station and the same pigeons under it.

The trenches on the citadel mound had been filled in, but the rock could not be carried off, and it was where he had left it, with an iron rail around it now and a boy selling roast nuts beside the rail. Yidimin could not read it. He had never been able to read it. But he had held the lamp, and he knew the line, and he put his hand flat on the cold rock beneath the place where the old man's finger had been.

*He killed 13,000 soldiers.*

Divide by six, the old man had said; divide by seven. For nearly four thousand years he had been right. Yidimin stood with his palm on the stone and thought of the goods shed and the lamp and the clerk's pencil going down the column, and of a boy on his stomach in the frost, counting fires.

It had not been a lie, he thought. It had only been early.

All things turn, his mother used to say. What ends will begin again. He had always thought she meant the spring.
