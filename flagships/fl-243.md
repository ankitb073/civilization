title: Test It as You Test Silver: The Historical I'a Lo

"Do not believe [...] a priest has said it. Test it as you test silver." The line is the most quoted in the Sayings of I'a Lo, and it is good advice for anyone writing about the man himself. The [[mov:69|Kievi]] faith he founded now counts some 160 million adherents. Its founder has accordingly acquired the biography a faith of that size seems to require. This essay tests that biography against the record and the one early text, and little of it rings true.

## The record

[[per:19924|I'a Lo]] was born in AY 2344, a man of the Nishmam people and a subject of the Kingdom of Pivish, whose state cult at the time was the Ya'cuam religion. The [[evt:20057|Founding of the Kievi Faith]] took place at Fivix in 2382, when he was about thirty-eight. He died of natural causes in 2396, at fifty-two, after some fourteen years of teaching. The record attaches to him no office, no family, no conflict with the court and no journey. The courts that kept the record had no reason to notice a teacher in Fivix.

## The text

The Sayings of I'a Lo (PAL·SC·00038) survive on papyrus, an unusual medium among the early collections, most of which are clay. As an object it is fragile and damaged: three of its seven lines have lacunae, including the famous one. Its date is AY 2433, thirty-seven years after the founder's death. Its compiler, the priest Im'amiof, was born in 2403. The heading speaks of sayings "gathered by those who heard him", but the man who gathered them was born seven years after the teacher died. At best the collection is the memory of a community in its second generation.

The contents raise a harder problem. Every one of the seven lines has a close parallel in older collections. "There is one above all the gods" and the test of silver both stand already in the Sayings of Nakavzi (PAL·SC·00001) of AY 106, as does "Be just in the market and gentle in the house". The turning wheel and the advice to speak little are centuries older than I'a Lo. These were the common formulae of the sayings literature. What belongs to the Kievi is the selection: the wheel, scepticism of priests, justice in dealings, restraint in wealth and speech. It is the profile of a moralist rather than a prophet. Whether the selection was his or his community's, the papyrus cannot tell us.

## The legend

The popular picture of I'a Lo as an ascetic who gave away his wealth comes from the doctrine, not from evidence: the faith holds that "the wise withdraw from wealth and appetite", and the Sayings duly say that "the wise go without". Biography deduced from doctrine is the oldest temptation in this field. We do not know whether I'a Lo was rich or poor, married or alone, or what work he did in Fivix. The doctrine shows what his followers valued. It cannot show how he lived.

The second element of the legend is the image of a faith that swept the world. Here the record is decisive. At the founding the Kievi held only a handful of districts, and a century later scarcely more. Its great growth came between the thirty-third and thirty-seventh centuries. The first ruler to adopt it was the king of Uem'o in AY 2696, three centuries after Fivix, and the earliest edict of establishment in the archive, the Edict establishing the Kievi (PAL·DE·00527), dates from 3764. The faith of 160 million was built over a millennium by people who never heard its founder.

That long gestation also produced divisions, each of which needed the founder's authority. The [[mov:133|Oze]] separated in 3746, among the Tlisyim people, and the [[mov:135|Tuthtu]] in 3787. The [[mov:138|Tutpothtu]] followed in 3985 and the [[mov:146|Khaleneth]] in 4256. Of these, the Tuthtu died out in the 47th century and the Tutpothtu only a generation ago. Each, we may be sure, quoted the Sayings. None had a better witness to I'a Lo than a papyrus compiled by a man who never met him.

The historical [[per:19924|I'a Lo]] is therefore a Nishmam moralist of Fivix whose community remembered him as teaching that the world turns, that priests should be tested and that the just man is gentle at home. The rest belongs to the history of the [[mov:69|Kievi]], which is a larger and a different subject.
