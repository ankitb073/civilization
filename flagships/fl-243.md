title: Test It as You Test Silver: The Historical I'a Lo

"Do not believe [...] a priest has said it. Test it as you test silver." The line is the most quoted in the Sayings of I'a Lo, and it is good advice for anyone writing about the man himself. The [[mov:69|Kievi]] faith he founded counted, at its height, some 245 million adherents. Its founder has accordingly acquired the biography a faith of that size seems to require. This essay tests that biography against the record and the one early text, and finds that little of it rings true.

## The record

[[per:19924|I'a Lo]] was born in AY 2344, a man of the Nishmam people and a subject of the Kingdom of Pivish, whose state cult at the time was the Ya'cuam religion. The [[evt:20057|Founding of the Kievi Faith]] took place at Fivix in 2382, when he was about thirty-eight. He died of natural causes in 2396, at fifty-two, after some fourteen years of teaching. The record attaches to him no office, no family, no conflict with the court and no journey. The courts that kept the record had no reason to notice a teacher in Fivix.

## The text

The Sayings of I'a Lo (PAL·SC·00038) survive on papyrus, an unusual medium among the early collections, most of which are clay. As an object it is fragile and damaged: three of its seven lines have lacunae, including the famous one. Its date is AY 2433, thirty-seven years after the founder's death. Its compiler, the priest Im'amiof, was born in 2403. The heading speaks of sayings "gathered by those who heard him", but the man who gathered them was born seven years after the teacher died. At best the collection is the memory of a community in its second generation.

Within it, the stock formulae must first be set aside. "There is one above all the gods, and the gods are its servants" and "This teaching is not for one people but for every nation" recur across the sayings literature of several unrelated faiths. They tell us nothing about I'a Lo in particular. What remains is distinctive. There is the wheel, "do not grieve at the turning of the wheel". There is the test of silver. There is "Be just in the market and gentle in the house". And there is the advice to speak little and truly. It is the voice of a moralist rather than a prophet: practical, sceptical of priests, attentive to markets and households. If any part of the collection preserves the teacher, it is this part.

## The legend

The popular picture of I'a Lo as an ascetic who gave away his wealth comes from the doctrine, not from evidence: the faith holds that "the wise withdraw from wealth and appetite", and the line "the wise man goes without" is in the Sayings. Biography deduced from doctrine is the oldest temptation in this field. We do not know whether I'a Lo was rich or poor, married or alone, or what work he did in Fivix. The doctrine shows what his followers valued. It cannot show how he lived.

The second element of the legend is the image of a faith that swept the world. Here the record is decisive. At the founding the Kievi held only a handful of districts, and a century later scarcely more. Its great growth came between the thirty-third and thirty-seventh centuries. No state established it until the Edict establishing the Kievi of AY 3764 (PAL·DE·00527), almost fourteen centuries after Fivix. The faith of 245 million was built over a millennium by people who never heard its founder.

That long gestation also produced divisions, each of which needed the founder's authority. The [[mov:133|Oze]] separated in 3746, among the Tlisyim people, and the [[mov:135|Tuthtu]] in 3787. The [[mov:138|Tutpothtu]] followed in 3985 and the [[mov:146|Khaleneth]] in 4256. Of these, the Tuthtu died out in the 47th century and the Tutpothtu only a generation ago. Each, we may be sure, quoted the Sayings. None had a better witness to I'a Lo than a papyrus compiled by a man who never met him.

The historical [[per:19924|I'a Lo]] is therefore a Nishmam moralist of Fivix who taught for fourteen years that the world turns, that priests should be tested and that the just man is gentle at home. The rest belongs to the history of the [[mov:69|Kievi]], which is a larger and a different subject.
