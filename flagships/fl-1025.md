title: Eat Slowly, Eva

The clamp had been burning for two days when the smith came up through the oaks to ask for it.

Veltongath heard him a long way off. Nobody who lived in the hills above [[set:161|Lenedagoshal]] walked like that, breaking every stick, stopping to blow and starting again before he had blown enough. She was kneeling on the uphill side of the mound, going over the turf with the flat of her hand, feeling for heat where there should be none, and she finished the side before she looked round.

He was a heavy man going grey, with the small white scars of his trade all up both forearms, and behind him came two boys with empty carrying-baskets. He looked at her clamp the way a hungry man looks at a pot.

"Oak?"

"Oak."

"How long?"

"Three days. Four, if the wind goes round."

He swore by [[dei:18|Eva]], mildly, which was the right god for a smith to swear by in a burner's clearing, and came and stood downwind, where the smoke rolled off the turf in a thick white fleece and lay along the ground instead of rising. He sniffed it.

"Still sweating."

"Still sweating. Open it now and you'll get brown sticks. You can't make iron with brown sticks."

"I know what I can make iron with." But he said it without heat. His name was Ralnen, and he was smith to the axemen of Nelo, four thousand two hundred and six axes to keep sharp on a forge of three stones and a goatskin, on a steppe with nothing on it that would burn but dung.

"Yesterday," he said, "they put a scribe at a table down there with a lump of wet clay, and the whole army walked past him town by town, and he called us out as we came. Ego, archers. Vernadho, shield-bearers. Yennga, archers. On and on. Nelo, axemen: that's us." He held up his two hands as if the scribe were still counting them. "Thirty-four thousand six hundred and seventy-nine. Then he read us the last line. 'The captains of hundreds answer for the absent.' I'd like to see the captain who'd answer for me."

Veltongath had listened for one name and not heard it. "You didn't say Lenedagoshal."

"No." He looked down through the trees at the roofs of the town, grey stone and grey shingle, and past them at the steppe, flat and the colour of a loaf's crust all the way to the edge of the sky. "Too small. Or too high up. Or somebody forgot you. Thank whoever you thank."

On the far side of the steppe, low down, there were three smudges that were not clouds.

"That's them," said Ralnen. "Burning as they come."

"And you sit and watch."

"[[per:16472|Mapofnen]] says we sit." He shrugged. "He's had the springs under your town fenced with shields since the day we got here. Those men have walked up that steppe on what's in their skins. He says let them come and look at our water for a day or two. He says a thirsty man is half beaten before you've touched him." He hitched up his belt. "Three days. Don't sell it to anyone else."

"Who else is there?"

He jerked his chin at the smoke on the horizon, and laughed, and went off down through the oaks, breaking every stick.

* * *

By morning the smudges had become a line of dust, and by evening the dust had become an army, sitting down on the far side of the steppe behind its own fires. For two days it sat there, and the army below the town sat too, and the fires over there grew fewer each night. On the third morning the men of [[pol:664|Yeda]] came on.

From the lip of the clearing it was very quiet. They spread across the steppe the way spilt water spreads across a floor, slowly, finding the low places, and here and there in the brown of them something caught the sun and let it go: bronze, iron, the polished rail of a chariot. Below her the army of [[pol:580|Yennga]] had stopped being a camp and become a long dark bank between the town and the steppe, and it did not move at all.

Then the wind got up, and she stopped watching.

It came down off the snow behind her the way it did on some afternoons in that season, dry and hard and all at once, and it fell on the clamp like a dog on a bone. Her hurdles of woven brush were up on the uphill side already, but the wind found the gaps between them and leaned on the turf until the turf breathed, and where the turf breathed, the fire under it woke and began to eat. She knew the sound before she saw anything: a small deep cough from inside the mound. Then, near the crown, the turf sagged and fell in, and a tongue of yellow flame stood up out of the hole and bent away downwind toward the steppe.

A clamp is a fire you starve. Let the air in and it stops being charcoal and becomes a bonfire, and two summers of cutting go up in one afternoon and leave you a ring of white ash.

She went up the side of it with the basket on her hip. She stamped the hole in, which you must never do and must always do, and threw the earth on, and threw more, and lay on her stomach on the hot turf with her face in the smoke and pressed it down with both arms, as if she were holding someone under a blanket. The wind pulled her hair out across her eyes. Under the wind a sound was coming up the hill that she had never heard before: like hail on a far roof, then like a market, then a long sound that was like nothing at all. She did not look. When the crown held, she slid down and went round the windward side on her knees, packing the base, and when that held, she went round again.

It went on all afternoon, the wind and the noise under it. Twice more the turf fell in. Twice more she put it back.

When the wind dropped at last, the light was long and red and the noise had stopped. On the steppe the dust was moving away, all of it, back the way it had come. The dark bank had broken into hundreds of small knots spread over the ground where the men of Yeda had stood, and between the knots the ground was strewn with something she could not make out and did not try to.

Over her head, from the peaks, the ravens were coming down in a long unhurried line, as if somebody had called them.

* * *

He came after dark, following the smoke.

She heard him far down the slope, as she had heard the smith, but this one stopped when he broke a stick and waited a long while before he moved again. She took up the long rake and stood on the far side of the clamp, where the glow from the vents would be in his eyes and not in hers.

He was a boy, sixteen or seventeen. He had no spear and no shield, one sandal, a leather cap pushed back off his forehead, and he came into the edge of the light with both hands open and held away from his sides.

"Water," he said.

It was her word, but worn into another shape, the way a doorstep is worn by other people's feet. He was from the other side. She had known it before he spoke.

She gave him the skin. He drank too fast and choked and drank again, and she took it back before he could make himself sick and gave him bread instead, the heel of the loaf with an onion pressed into it.

"Eat slowly," she said.

He sat by the clamp with his knees up and ate slowly, as he was told, with his eyes shut. Then he looked at the mound for a long time, and said something she did not catch, and said it again more carefully, as though to a child.

"Your back's gone blue."

She turned. On the far side of the clamp the smoke had thinned and changed: not the white fleece any longer, not the sharp yellow-grey that comes after, but a clean thin blue that hardly showed against the night, the colour of a vein in a wrist. The charcoal on that side was made, and if the vents under it stayed open it would burn on to ash by morning. She had been watching the field. She had missed it.

The boy was already on his knees at the base, scooping earth with both hands into the vents she would have closed, in the order she would have closed them.

"My father burns," he said, not looking up. "For the smiths at [[set:0|Tallen]]."

They worked through the night. She opened new vents further round to draw the fire on into the green side, and he closed the spent ones behind her, and they went round and round the mound like two oxen on a threshing floor. When they stopped at the crown to fill a hollow, he laid both palms flat on the turf, the way her husband used to, and said under his breath what every burner says.

"Eat slowly, Eva. [[dei:15|Venba]], hold him down."

He said it in his worn-over way and she said it in hers, and they were the same words.

In Lenedagoshal they kept the old teaching of the hill towns, which had little use for the gods of [[mov:2|Jenro]] that the army prayed to, though it did not deny them. "There is one above all the gods, and the gods are its servants," the teaching said, so there was no shame in speaking to Eva, any more than in speaking to the doorkeeper of a great house. Her husband had spoken to Eva every night of his life, and so did she.

Toward morning the boy talked, a little, in pieces. They had not drunk since the day before the fighting. [[per:16526|Nokhkoshan]] had told them the springs under the town would be theirs by the second day, and they had believed him, because he was a man you believed. Then the wind came down at them full of grit, and they could not see, and the arrows came out of the grit, and the men on both sides of him turned, and he turned. He did not know whether his captain was alive. He did not know whether anyone from his village was.

"They'll count us," he said, "when we get back." He seemed more afraid of that than of anything that had happened on the field.

She thought of the scribe at his table and the captains who answered for the absent, and wondered whether they did it the same way in the south. Probably. People were people.

Before he lay down to sleep a little, he broke a crumb from the last of the bread and pushed it into a vent with one finger, for Eva. She let him. "Whoever feeds the hungry stranger has fed the god," the teaching said too, and watching him, she was not sure which of them had fed which god that night.

* * *

Voices woke them, spread out across the slope below, and the crack of sticks as men beat the bracken, calling to each other in her own shape of words.

The boy was on his feet. She took him by the sleeve and walked him, not ran him, across the clearing to where the woodcutters' path went up over the shoulder of the ridge to the west, and pointed down the far side.

"It comes out on the steppe a day from here, a long way from the field. Keep the mountains on your right hand. Walk at night."

She hung the water-skin round his neck and pushed the rest of the bread inside his tunic. He fumbled at his belt and held something out to her on his palm: a twist of silver wire, bent double.

"It's a full [[cur:364|Kerel]]," he said. "By our weight."

She closed his fingers over it. "Buy water with it."

He looked at her as if there were something else to say, a name perhaps, his or hers, and then the voices below came closer, and he went up the path and over the shoulder of the ridge and was gone. She did not know his name. She found afterwards that she had not wanted to.

* * *

It was Ralnen who came out of the trees, with his two boys and a dozen axemen fanned out behind him, swiping at the bracken with their hafts. He had a cut over one eye and a rag round his left hand, and he was happier than she had ever seen a man.

"Did you see it?" he said. "Did you see it from up here?"

"I saw the wind."

"[[dei:16|Domamnol]] sent it. Every man down there will tell you. Straight into their faces from first to last." He looked at the clamp, sealed and quiet now, with only a few threads of blue going up from the crown. "Done?"

"Done. It wants a day to cool. Open it hot and it catches."

"A day." He sat down heavily on her woodpile. "Some of them have gone up into these woods. Mapofnen wants them brought in. Did any come by?"

"No."

He nodded, not really listening, looking out over the steppe, where the cookfires of Yennga went straight up into the still air, and the ravens rose and settled, rose and settled.

"Thirty thousand, they're saying in the camp," he said. "Thirty thousand of theirs lying down there, and hardly a man of ours." He turned the bound hand over and looked at it. "I put two from Nelo in the ground this morning myself, so I don't know about hardly. But thirty thousand, they're saying. The queen will want it on a stone."

Veltongath looked west, to the shoulder of the ridge where the path went over. Somewhere beyond it a boy with one sandal was going down toward the steppe with the mountains on his right hand, and if he was careful, and lucky, and drank slowly, he might see Tallen again.

She said nothing. She let the number have him.

**Author's note.** The muster of Yennga for the [[evt:16595|Battle of Linidhagshal]] (PAL·MU·00217) names eight towns and 34,679 men; Lenedagoshal is not among them. The wind and the thirst come from the Yennga chronicle (PAL·CH·03088), which gives Nokhkoshan 78,012 men; the record gives him 26,682, and counts 7,630 of them lost against 1,926 of Yennga. Lorarponath's stele (PAL·IN·01204) claims 37,300 dead, more than Nokhkoshan brought. Yeda's diviners later filed the day under a raven (PAL·OM·00185). The sayings are Ramadhshefin's (PAL·SC·00004); the record keeps his teaching at Lenedagoshal, and nothing of its burners.
