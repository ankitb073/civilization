title: Leave the Name Light
---
Nyimenyime had been singing the king's name for twenty-seven years, and she was halfway through it when the bells began.

She was on the high note, the one the whole of *Stone upon Stone* climbs toward — *Luisha*, and then the drop, *luisha*, like a man setting down one stone and then its twin — when the bell of the [[mov:25|Myithiyishiu]] shrine above the grain steps came down on the note like a lid on a pot. Then the bell in the dyers' quarter. Then one across the river, thin with distance, and another beyond that, until the air over [[set:15|Myizinithie]] was so full of bronze that her grandson stopped drumming and clapped his hands over his ears, the drumsticks sticking out on either side of his head like a beetle's feelers.

Nobody noticed that she had stopped. They were not her crowd. They had their backs to her and were packed round the post at the foot of the steps, where a printed sheet had been pasted up that morning, still dark with paste at the corners, and a young man inky to the wrists was reading it aloud for the third time, louder each time, because the bells kept eating it.

"To the people of [[pol:1966|Myizinithie]]: The old order is ended."

He waited for a bell to finish.

"No man shall again rule by birth alone."

Some of the crowd had heard it twice already and were moving their lips with him, the way people mouth a song they have only begun to learn.

"The republic is established, and [[per:46131|Yuiveshima]] shall lead the provisional government. Let the bells ring in every town."

'They are,' somebody said. 'Listen to them. They are.'

'Who?' a woman near Nyimenyime asked her husband. 'Who's to lead?'

'Fire Eagle,' he said, which was only the name turned into plain words, the way you turn a stranger's face to the light. It told her nothing, and she nodded as if it had.

Yinush took his hands off his ears. He was ten, and serious about the drum. 'Do we finish it?'

Nyimenyime looked at the backs of the crowd, and past them, up the hill, at the long roofs where the king had lived for as long as there had been a king. She had never once stood near enough to him to see the colour of his eyes, and she had sung his name more often, she supposed, than anyone alive, himself included.

'No,' she said. 'We don't finish it.'

* * *

Mashe Muie's cookshop was a fire, a counter and four benches under an awning at the bottom of the tanners' lane, and Nyimenyime had sung there at the noon meal for so many years that her bowl was set down before she sat. Today Mashe Muie set the bowl down and kept her hand on the rim.

'Not that one,' she said.

'I haven't sung anything.'

'You've the look of somebody about to sing that one.'

Barley and onions and a knuckle of mutton, the same as yesterday. The grain was the same as yesterday too. Mashe Muie had sent her girl to the grain steps at the first bell, expecting the worst, and the girl had come back with the usual sack at the usual price, a little under an [[cur:1101|Isui]] the measure. 'Whatever's happened up the hill,' Mashe Muie said, 'it hasn't got down as far as the barley.'

Nyimenyime ate and went through her songs, the way another woman might go through a chest after a death.

Nine songs paid. There was *Stone upon Stone*, which she had made in the year the kingdom began, when she was twenty-four and he was twenty-nine and the whole city went out to look at him. There was *The Goose*, which children asked for, in which the king's goose eats the king's dinner and the king, being a just man, goes hungry. There was *Tlilisyi*, for the city taken eleven years ago, in which the king rides in at the gate with his own sword out; she sang it for soldiers, and once a soldier had told her, laughing, that the king had come to Tlilisyi after the fighting, to sign, and that a general had done the rest. She had thanked him and gone on singing it the way it paid. There was *The Walls*, for the year the enemy came up to the city fourteen years ago — the [[evt:46873|Battle of Myizinithie]], the clerks called it, as if it had been a thing that happened to the city and not in every lane of it — which ended the next year with the king putting his hand to the [[evt:46890|Treaty of Myizinithie]] in his own hall and giving away towns for twenty years of quiet. People cried at *The Walls* and paid well for it. And there were three songs about love, in which the beloved was as handsome as the king on his horse, as stubborn as the king, as rich as the king.

That was seven. Of the other two, one was a lullaby and the other was about a one-eyed ox.

'Sing the ox,' said Mashe Muie.

'At noon? Today?'

'Today of all days. Nobody ever got into trouble over an ox.'

But Nyimenyime was not thinking about the ox. She was thinking about the tenth song, the one that had not paid in twenty-seven years because she had not sung it in twenty-seven years.

When she was a girl the high seat had been somewhere far off, in a city she never saw, and the men who sat in it changed every five summers. Nobody she knew had ever set eyes on one. Her mother had a song about them, with a hole in the chorus where the name went, and on market days she would stop in the middle and call out, 'Who is it now?' and somebody always knew, and shouted it, and she sang it in. Nyimenyime had learned the song at her knee. She had learned the hole, too.

'Leave the name light, Nyime,' her mother had said. 'Don't lean on it. It isn't staying.'

Then the far-off country came apart, and a young man stood on the steps of the hall up the hill, and Nyimenyime, twenty-four and with her mother not a year in the ground, made *Stone upon Stone* and leaned on his name with her whole chest, because this one was staying. He stayed. She had leaned on that name so long that the song had grown round it the way bark grows round a nail.

Her mother's song she had put away. *Five summers, and then he's through* was not a line you sang about a man who meant to stay.

* * *

In the afternoon she and Yinush walked up toward the great square, because everyone was walking up toward the great square.

In the potters' lane a gang of children had a king already. He was a fat boy enthroned on an upturned basket, and as they passed the others dragged him off it by the ankles, all of them shrieking 'Not by birth alone! Not by birth alone!' which they had got from somewhere and did not understand and loved. Then the basket stood empty and there was a fight about it. A girl with her hair in two knots settled it. They would count out for it, she said, and they did, standing in a ring and slapping each chest in turn to the chorus of *The Goose*. Nyimenyime heard her own words go round the ring — *and the king went hungry, and the king went hungry* — and land on a small boy with a running nose, who climbed onto the basket looking frightened.

Yinush watched them over his shoulder until the lane turned.

At the corner of the square there were soldiers, a dozen of them, muskets grounded, faces careful. Nobody had yet said whose soldiers they were, and they did not look as if they knew themselves. The youngest of them was humming. It took Nyimenyime a moment to place the tune, because he was humming it so softly, and then she heard *Tlilisyi*, the bit where the gate comes down. He saw her looking and stopped and went red to the ears.

She nodded to him, one working person to another. Habit was habit. You could not pull a tune out of a man's mouth by proclamation.

* * *

By evening the bells had stopped, and the quiet they left was worse. The square filled anyway. People brought lamps and bread and jars of beer and stood about as if waiting for a procession nobody had announced. The printed sheet had been pasted up here too, on the wall of the shrine, and people kept going to look at it, even people who could not read, the way you go back to look at a newborn.

What they wanted, Nyimenyime saw, was to sing. A crowd that size, on a night like that, has to make a noise with its throat or burst. And they had nothing to sing. Somebody near the shrine started *Stone upon Stone* out of pure habit, and a hundred voices took it up, and it rolled across the square, big and warm and dreadful, right up to the high note — and stopped dead, all at once, as if the whole square had walked into a door. In the silence somebody laughed. Then everyone was laughing, ashamed.

'Flower Flower!' A man had seen her. 'Flower Flower! You made this mess. Give us something else!'

There was a mounting block by the shrine wall. Yinush got onto it first and pulled her up after him by both hands. From up there the square was all faces turned yellow by the lamps.

She sang the first verse alone, the way her mother had, plain and quick:

*The rain comes down when it likes,*
*the river goes where it goes,*
*the goat eats whatever it finds —*
*so who's in the high seat now?*

And she stopped, and shouted into the stop, 'Who is it now?'

They did not understand. They looked at one another. Then, from somewhere on her left, a voice she knew — the young man with the inky wrists, who had read the sheet three times that morning and had not stopped, it seemed, all day — shouted, 'Yuiveshima!'

She sang it in.

*Yuiveshima's in the high seat now.*
*Give him his bread and give him his due —*
*five summers, and then he's through.*

The first time round only the front rows had it. The second time round half the square had it. It was a song made to be learned by a crowd in two hearings, made by people who had needed to learn a new name every five summers, and it went into the crowd like water into dry ground. By the third chorus they were shouting the name back at her before she reached the hole, and Yinush was beating the drum so hard he had his tongue between his teeth.

Then, in a gap, a voice from the back. Not jeering — curious, almost shy.

'And the old one? What do we sing for Stone Stone?'

Some laughed. Somebody made an ugly sound. Nyimenyime thought of the young man on the steps twenty-seven years ago, whose eyes she had never been close enough to see, who was fifty-six tonight and somewhere up the hill, if he was still up the hill, hearing all this or not hearing it. She had eaten for twenty-seven years on his name. You give a thing its due. The priests of the shrine behind her said as much, in their way, when they were sober.

She made it up as she went, to the same tune:

*Stone Stone has gone in to his supper,*
*twenty-seven summers, and now he's through.*
*Let the soup be hot and the bread be new —*
*give him his bread and give him his due.*

There was a moment in which it might have gone either way. Then a woman near the front laughed, a big loose laugh with no edge to it, and sang the last line back, and the square sang it after her, and it was done. [[per:45883|Luishaluisha]] had his verse. Nobody would ever sing it again, she thought, and nobody needed to.

'Again,' said Yinush, flushed, sticks up. 'The new one. Again.'

So she sang it again, the high seat and the river and the goat, and when she came to the hole the whole square filled it for her. And she sang the new name the way her mother had taught her to sing all of them: lightly, without leaning on it, so that it could be lifted out.

**Author's note.** The words read aloud at the post are from the proclamation itself, PAL·DE·00847, dated 14 Nizö; the crowds in the squares are the *Myizinithie Clarion*'s (PAL·NP·00639), and the quiet, tense capital is the *Ane Intelligencer*'s (PAL·NP·00640). The singer, her grandson and her mother's song are mine; the record keeps no songs. It keeps what the story only hints at: that [[per:46131|Yuiveshima]] died in AY 4863, two years after the bells, and that [[per:45883|Luishaluisha]], first and only king of [[pol:1966|Myizinithie]], outlived him by twenty-seven years, dying a natural death in AY 4890.
