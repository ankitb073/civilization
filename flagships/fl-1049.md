title: Carry Him Into the Cold
---
The boy was barking like a fox, the kitchen woman said, and nobody up the hill knew what to do about it.

She said it on the doorstep with the cold coming in around her skirts, in the court's tongue first and then, remembering whose door it was, in the town's. She carried her lamp inside a pot to keep the wind off it. Behind her the lane ran down to the weirs, black, and the smell of the smoking-sheds lay along it, alder and fish, as it had every night of the nurse's life. In the town's own tongue [[set:158|Shati]] meant *the place of fish*. The kings on the hill had never troubled to learn it.

"Whose boy?" the nurse said, though she knew.

"The new one's." The kitchen woman did not say the name. Nobody in the lower town had said it aloud yet; it had only been a day. "His wife has the priests in. They're burning leaves over him, and he's worse."

The nurse looked back into the room. Her son was asleep by the hearth with a net-needle still in his fist. He was twenty-one years old, and as far as she knew he was the only milk-brother a king of [[pol:55|Yimola]] had ever had who mended weirs for his bread. She put her outdoor cloak on over the one she slept in.

"I haven't been up the hill in eleven years," she said.

"I know. That's why I came for you."

* * *

[[per:1994|Ulobyiruyin]] had died in his bed the night before, of nothing worse, the fishwives said, than being fifty-two. By noon there had been other men on the palace gate. By dark the town knew whose men they were, and had barred its doors and gone on turning its fish, because it had seen a throne change hands before and the fish had needed turning then as well.

The lane steepened between the woodstacks. At the upper gate two young men stood in sheepskins with bronze-headed spears, and their faces were new. The older one lifted his lamp to the nurse's town cloak, and her town face, and the kitchen woman said, "For the boy," and he stood aside. As they passed she heard him mutter something to [[dei:64|Nidi]], the way men on a door at night always do, as if the goddess of war kept the hours.

In the long hall the lamps were lit for the dead king's soul, more lamps than she could have counted, and through the doorway she saw the shape of him on the bier and the women around it, and heard his widow's voice going up and coming down. Priests of [[dei:66|Shyidartsi]] moved among the lamps with their sleeves held back from the flames. Below the wall, in the tomb court, there was more light: a long stone lay on trestles there, and three cutters stood around it with their mallets hanging, and a priest held a stick of charcoal and waited, like a man who has been told he will be told.

She had come down this hill eleven years ago with nothing in her hands. She had come up it ten years before that with her own baby on one arm and her milk just in, to feed the son of the king's son. Old Pupraman had died, and then his son Pupraman within the year, and the child she had nursed was king of Yimola at eight, with his mother ruling for him. His name was Utun. At ten, Ulobyiruyin took the throne from him and left him in his nursery, with his sling and his nurse, and that winter a fever went down into his chest.

She had sat with him four nights. On the last one he asked whether the fish were running.

The town said poison. The town still said it. She had told them what she had seen, and they had looked at her kindly, the way you look at someone who stood too close to a thing; a poisoned boy is a better story than a sick one, and it gives people a face to hate. Utun's mother was still alive. The two women did not visit each other. There was only one thing to talk about.

The kitchen woman did not take her to a new room. She took her to that one.

* * *

It was full of smoke. Someone had set a brazier by the bed and heaped it with bitter leaves, and two priests stood in the haze saying the names of gods. On the bed a young woman sat bolt upright with a child on her lap, a boy of two in a good wool shirt, and as the nurse came in he barked: a hard, hollow, astonishing sound, too big for him, a fox on a hillside. Between barks, on every in-breath, his throat made a thin high crowing, like wind in a split reed. His eyes were round with it. So were his mother's.

In the corner, wrapped in a blanket and wide awake, sat a girl of about eight.

"You're the nurse," the young woman said. She was from the court, and she spoke the court's tongue, and she had been crying in it. "The other boy's nurse. The one who died."

"Yes."

"In this room."

"In this room."

The mother's arms went tight around her son. "I didn't send for you."

"I did, lady," said the kitchen woman from the door.

"Then you can both—"

The boy barked, and crowed, and barked, and the crowing went on a little too long, and nobody in the room said anything until it stopped.

"Put that out," the nurse said to the priests. "It's choking him." They looked at the young woman, and the young woman, after a moment, looked away, and one of them took the brazier out by its handles, muttering. The nurse did not reach for the child. She sat on the edge of the bed and made the sound the women of the town make at a frightened baby, a sort of clicking hum, low in the chest, and the boy turned his head and looked at her.

"He's frightened," she said, "and fright shuts the throat. Yours too, lady. He can hear it." She held out her hands. "Where's his cloak?"

"You're not taking him out. It's freezing."

"Yes," said the nurse. "That's why."

* * *

The cold took him by the face, and he gasped, and for one long moment the nurse was afraid. Then he coughed, an ordinary cough, and leaned his hot head into her neck.

The sky over the courtyard was hard and clear, [[dei:63|Dvimamnul]]'s winter sky, every star sharp enough to cut on. The frost had come down on the flagstones. She walked him slowly along the wall and back, and along the wall and back, holding him upright so that his breath went out over her shoulder and smoked. Behind her came small steps. The girl had followed in her blanket and was putting her feet exactly into the nurse's prints in the frost, as if there were a rule about it.

"Are you a witch?" the girl said.

"No. I'm from the town."

"Is my father king now?"

"Ask him."

"He's busy." The girl hopped, landed in the next print. "Did your boy die of this?"

"No. Of something else."

"Will Tatbalutin die of this?"

It took the nurse a moment to understand that she meant her brother, who had their father's name.

"Not of this," she said.

They walked. The crowing came more seldom. Down in the tomb court the cutters had lit a fire and were standing at it, and the long stone was still white. After a while the boy lifted his head from her neck and pointed past her ear at the moon, low and enormous over the roofs, and said, "Tono," as the court's children are taught to, in a voice like a little frog.

"That's right," said the nurse. "There he is."

* * *

The man came out of the hall door without a lamp, so she saw him first as a darkness against the light, and then as a man of about thirty with his cloak pulled crooked and a day on his face that had gone on too long.

"Who has my son out in this?"

"She's making him better," said the girl, from inside a footprint.

He came closer, and stopped, and listened, as anyone would have, to the child's breath. It went in and out. It smoked. It did not crow. [[per:2088|Tatbalutin]] let out a breath of his own and stood beside them at the wall, close enough that the boy reached for his collar and held it.

"You were the old line's nurse," he said. "The boy-king's."

"Yes."

"They told me in the hall. As if it meant something." He looked down at the white stone in the court below. "In the town they say Ulobyiruyin had that boy killed."

"In the town they say a good many things."

"Did he?"

"No," she said. "It was his chest. Four nights." She shifted the boy higher. "On the last night Ulobyiruyin came to the door. He'd had the throne half a year by then. He came in and stood at the foot of the bed, where a man stands when he doesn't mean to stay, and asked me how old the boy was, and I said ten. And he said, *Mine was eleven*, and went out again."

Tatbalutin said nothing for a while. The cutters laughed at something by their fire and then stopped, as if they had remembered where they were.

"They're waiting on me," he said. "For the lintel. Half my men want it left blank. Let a thief go down to Shyidartsi without a name, they say. The priests want what's proper, and nobody can agree what's proper for a man who did what he did." He smiled with one side of his mouth. "What I did. This morning."

The nurse did not answer that. It was not a question for a woman from the town.

"Mine is two," said Tatbalutin, to nobody, and then, lower, "Mine was eleven," as if trying the words in his own mouth to see what they weighed.

The boy coughed then, a loose wet cough, and his father's hands came up fast.

"That's a good one," the nurse said. "That's the throat letting go. Hold him up. No, up, against you, so. Walk."

And the man who had taken Yimola that morning took his son and held him upright against his shoulder, the way he had been told, and walked along the wall and back, along the wall and back, while the girl hopped behind him from print to print. On the third turn he stopped at the head of the steps and called something down into the tomb court. The priest with the charcoal straightened up. The cutters turned from their fire.

* * *

Inside, with the smoke gone, the room was only a room. The young mother slept sitting up against the wall with her son across her lap, and his breath went in and out with a little catch at the top of it that the nurse did not mind. The girl had fallen asleep across the foot of the bed, where nobody would stand tonight.

The nurse did not sleep. She sat on the stool and sang, low, in the town's tongue, the song she had sung in this room before: that [[dei:65|Tono]] goes down every month into the house of Shyidartsi, and every month she gives him back.

The mother opened her eyes partway. "What does it say?"

The nurse told her.

"Every month?"

"Every month."

From the tomb court below came the sound of the mallets, patient, like someone at a door who knows it will be opened. In the morning the lintel would say, in the signs the priests kept, "[[per:1994|Ulobyiruyin]], king of [[pol:55|Yimola]], dwells in this tomb. May [[dei:66|Shyidartsi]] protect his soul." The nurse could not read, and never went to look. She listened to the other sound.

The mallets stopped before morning. The breathing did not.

**Author's note.** The record says only that [[per:2088|Tatbalutin]] seized the throne in AY 506 after Ulobyiruyin died, naturally, in office ([[evt:1757|Usurpation of Tatbalutin I]]); it does not say how. The lintel survives (PAL·IN·03423). The lamps for a soul come from the Shati merchant Ashiub's testament (PAL·WL·00013), which counts AY 523 as Tatbalutin's eighteenth year. Utun died at ten, of natural causes, in the year of the [[evt:1672|Usurpation of Ulobyiruyin]]; Ulobyiruyin's own son had died at eleven. The coughing boy reigned from AY 538 to 563.
