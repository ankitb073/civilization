title: Twice the Field Was Not Ours: Lwesh'ayüp and the Fifteen Years of Ya'cuam

In AY 1852 a clay tablet was set in the gate of [[set:240|Tiaiosh]], and its second stanza ends with a line I have never translated without losing something: "From this day and for all days, Ya'cuam is Ya'cuam." The kingdom it proclaimed lasted six more years. The man it praised, [[per:14350|Lwesh'ayüp]], outlived that kingdom by thirteen. This chapter measures the distance between the line and the life.

## Seven times our fathers stood up

The record gives Lwesh'ayüp a birth in AY 1811, a people, the Ya'cuam, and a name meaning *stone fire*. It names no father or mother. It knows a wife, Kha'im, born in 1809, who outlived him by seven years, but little else of her, and no children: the dynasty that bears his name begins and ends with him. Of his first thirty-two years it says nothing.

His world is better recorded. The record knows nine Ya'cuam wars of independence, from AY 449 to AY 2478, and his was the seventh, as the tablet knows: "Seven times our fathers stood up. Seven times the wheel turned." Five of the six before him ended in suppression, and when the sixth was crushed in 1447 the victors annexed four of the five districts that would rise with him. His is the only one of the nine that ended in a treaty granting independence.

He chose his moment well, or was lucky in it. The kingdom of [[pol:617|Waga]], ruled from Bakh, was failing: 372,237 people in 28 districts by the end of the 1810s, 133,266 in twelve by the end of the 1840s. Its real enemy was Tomözel, which sacked Bakh in 1831 and again in 1848. A Tomözel muster roll of 1847 (PAL·MU·00177) lists 15,674 men for a battle at which Waga fielded 397, and on a house wall in Bakh a Tomözel soldier wrote, "I, [...] the army of Tomözel, wish I were home" (PAL·GR·00079). Ya'cuam fought a kingdom already being dismantled from the other side.

The [[evt:14389|Revolt of Ya'cuam]] came in 1843, when Lwesh'ayüp was thirty-two. The tablet makes it one scene: he "stood up in the gate of Tü'a'i'osh" and said, "Enough." The record adds an irony. It dates the founding of Tiaiosh to that same year and credits it to Waga, and the excavator's lowest stratum there is "the foundation of the city, c. AY 1843" (PAL·EX·00182). The gate was new, and Waga had founded the town. The kingdom held five districts, and its state cult was the [[mov:9|Menibe]] pantheon, which the record assigns to the Mienibe people, not to the Ya'cuam's ancestral gods.

## Twice the field was not ours

On the battlefield the record is hard on him. The [[evt:14390|Seventh Ya'cuam War of Independence]] had seven battles. Lwesh'ayüp commanded five and lost four. At the [[evt:14419|Battle of Tiaiosh (AY 1845)]] he brought 559 men against 424 under Waga's king, [[per:14173|I'pil Foyatl II]], and lost 43 to their 11. In 1846 he had 485 to the 336 of the general [[per:14378|Sho'e Movi]] and lost 126, Ya'cuam's worst toll of the war, to their 84. Twice more he failed against Waga's new queen, [[per:14183|Ba'enieme]]: 430 against 358 in 1849, and in 1850, at nearly four to one, 577 against 148, he still lost 35 men to her 22.

The victories belonged to his general, Ilshazh'igw'a. In 1847, with 414 against 269, he lost nine, killed fifty, and killed I'pil Foyatl II on the field. In 1851, with 590 against 136, he lost four and killed 42, and Ya'cuam laid siege to Bakh. Only then did the king win in person, at the [[evt:14488|Battle of Tunavükhini (AY 1852)]], near a town Waga had already ceded to Tomözel: 662 men against 208 under [[per:14383|Yi Luesh'a]], 15 lost and 64 killed. Ba'enieme, Tomözel's vassal since 1848, signed the [[evt:14490|Treaty of Bakh (AY 1852)]]: independence and a ten-year truce. Ya'cuam lost 262 men in the war and Waga 292. Persistence and Waga's decay won it, not generalship; Waga collapsed the next year.

The tablet (PAL·DE·00952) is surprisingly candid: "Twice Lwesh'ayüp went out against her by Tü'a'i'osh. Twice the field was not ours." It credits Ilshazh'igw'a, and its count for Tunavükhini, "Sixty-four of Vakha lay down. Fifteen of ours lay down," is exactly the record's. Its one distortion flatters. For 1845 it sings that "Twenty-nine went first against four hundred." The four hundred is honest; it omits the 559 behind the twenty-nine.

The king's own stele (PAL·IN·01024), dated 1853 and broken at the foot, is cruder: "Lwesh'ayüp, king of Ya'cuam, defeated Vakha in Tü'a'i'osh. He killed 300 soldiers." It commemorates the battle of 1851, which he did not command, where Waga lost 42 of only 136 men. Kings take their generals' victories everywhere; what strikes me is the date. He raised it in the year Va'mi first besieged the city.

Igwatlish, born in the year of the revolt, wrote his account (PAL·CH·02649) in 1883, twelve years after the king's death. He omits 1845 and 1846 and says that I'pil Foyatl "broke the peace and sent raiders across the border of Ya'cuam," though the record makes the war a Ya'cuam rising. For 1850 he writes "our men could not hold the field. We lost 18; the enemy, 71." The record says Ya'cuam lost 35 and Waga 22. For 1851 he counts 136 enemy dead, the size of Ba'enieme's whole army.

## The wheel turns again

The tablet's closing lines already face another enemy: "We did not bow to Vakha. We will not bow to Va'mi." The [[evt:14487|Va'mi–Ya'cuam War]] had begun in the year of the treaty. Against a kingdom the record counts at 17,855 people, [[pol:529|Va'mi]] had some 3.5 million by the end of the 1840s, and Tomözel some 4.7 million. In 1853 [[per:14387|Anial I]] of Va'mi beat Ilshazh'igw'a near Tiaiosh in person, 8,595 men against 426. At the [[evt:14513|Battle of Tiaiosh (AY 1854)]] her general [[per:14445|Ilxazhfayatl]] brought 13,422 against Lwesh'ayüp's 415, and Tiaiosh was sacked. By the [[evt:14515|Treaty of Tiaiosh (AY 1854)]] the king ceded two districts of Yimla, the land "where our fathers are buried", and accepted a tribute of 36 kilograms of silver a year, by the record's reckoning, for nineteen years: longer than the kingdom had left. No document notices the irony that the record counts Anial I among the Ya'cuam people, and that her empire's state religion was the ancestral faith of the Ya'cuam.

That year [[pol:492|Tomözel]] began the [[evt:14508|Holy War of Tomözel against Ya'cuam]]. Khaswila of Ya'cuam (PAL·CH·02670, 1861) says Tomözel's king "broke the peace and sent raiders across the border", the stock words Igwatlish used of Waga; Simada of Tomözel (PAL·CH·02669, 1901) says Lwesh'ayüp "had gathered an army against Tomözel. Our king could not suffer the insult." The record makes Tomözel the attacker. [[per:14241|Shoe Yueve]], king since the age of three, was twenty-seven when he came in 1856 with 10,227 men to Lwesh'ayüp's 396; Ya'cuam lost 141, Tomözel 22. Ilshazh'igw'a died in 1857. At the [[evt:14554|Battle of Tiaiosh (AY 1858)]] 16,214 men came against 308, nearly one soldier for every subject of the kingdom. After a third siege Lwesh'ayüp, at forty-seven, signed away his five districts at Tiaiosh. Simada gives him 1,123 men in 1856, 873 in 1858, and "8 districts"; the record says 396, 308 and five. In his three wars Ya'cuam lost 661 men in battle.

## Who will now remember

He lived thirteen more years, and the record does not say where or how: no exile, no execution, no office. He died in 1871, at sixty, of natural causes.

Khaswila wrote while the king still lived: "the gates were opened to the enemy. Who will now remember the kings of old?" Yet he dated his tablet "in the 19th year of Ya'cuam", three years after it ended, and Igwatlish dated his to "the 41st year of Ya'cuam". For a generation the scribes of Tiaiosh kept counting the years of a dead kingdom. That, more than any stele, measures what Lwesh'ayüp made.

He was a poor commander who kept going out, a king with the sense to let a better general fight, and a rebel who rose while someone else destroyed his enemy. His kingdom was too small for its world. The Ya'cuam rose twice more, in 2216 and 2475, and were crushed both times. The tablet's promise at the war's end was the Menibe one: "What ended has begun again." For him it never did. For the scribes who went on counting, it was enough.
