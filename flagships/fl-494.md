**THE FIRE-ENGINE AT WORK: ITS INVENTOR DESCRIBES IT FOR OUR READERS**

*Our readers will have heard rumours of the engine now working at the foundry by the east gate of [[set:7|Ane]]. We asked its inventor, [[per:45136|Mr Shomiethe]], to describe it in his own words. He has done so below. We print his account unaltered, except that we have removed two sentences concerning a rival which our lawyers advise are actionable.*

---

Sir,

You ask me to explain to the public what I have made, and I shall try to do so without either false modesty or the other kind.

I have made an engine that turns heat into work. In plain terms: a fire boils water in a closed iron vessel; the steam is let into a cylinder, where it pushes a close-fitting piston; the steam is then cooled by a jet of cold water, so that it shrinks back to almost nothing and the air outside presses the piston home again. The piston is joined to a beam, and the beam to a pump, or a wheel, or a hammer, as the owner pleases. That is all. Every part of it was known to the smiths of [[pol:1656|Ane]] before I was born. What was not known was how to make them work together without the boiler bursting or the piston sticking, and that cost me eleven years and, I am sorry to say, the eyebrows of two apprentices.

I am aware that engines on some such principle have been reported abroad, and I have read the accounts. I did not copy them. I could not have; they are vague, and the drawings that reach us are wrong in every particular that matters. But I do not claim that heat had never moved a piston anywhere under the sky before this year. I claim that it had never done so in Ane, at a working foundry, ten hours a day, for a month together. It has now.

What will it do? At present it pumps the foundry's water and works its bellows, doing the labour of twelve horses without eating their oats. A horse must be fed whether it works or not; grain stands at five and a half [[cur:1038|Withe]] the measure, and does not seem inclined to stand lower. Coal does not eat, and does not lie down. I foresee engines draining the mines in the hills, so that seams now drowned can be worked; engines driving the looms; engines, one day, perhaps, moving themselves.

I will be honest about the war, because your readers will ask. The foundries of Ane have been worked very hard these three years. Our soldiers were beaten at [[evt:45870|Itlishithyi]] and at [[evt:45878|Faamni]], and every man in this city knows that it was not for want of courage. They have also won, at [[evt:45882|Nzhivi]] and, this year, at [[evt:45885|Namosash]]. I do not say my engine won those battles. I say that a city whose foundries can work without horses and without rest is a city that will not again be short of iron when its soldiers need it.

A word to the priests of [[mov:25|Myitiyishiu]], some of whom have preached that the engine is a thing of appetite, a hunger for more than the gods give. I answer respectfully that the engine has no appetite; it only has a firebox. The appetite, if there is any, is in the men who own it. Let them deal justly, as the faith commands, and moderately, and the engine will do them no harm.

A word, finally, to the government. I have applied for letters protecting the invention, and I am told the application lies on a desk at the President's office. I do not doubt that [[per:45180|President Shimap]] has weightier matters before him. I only observe that an art which is not protected is an art which every foreign maker may take for nothing, and that I should be sorry to see engines of Ane's invention sold back to Ane at a profit.

I remain, Sir, your obedient servant,

SHOMIETHE,  
*engineer, at the foundry by the east gate.*

*Ane, 9 Muie 4224 of the Myitiyishiu.*
