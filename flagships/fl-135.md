*Chapter the Third. How the Crown Fell.*

My friends have asked me, now that I have left the presidency and have nothing to do in the mornings but read the newspapers, to set down what I remember of the [[evt:45920|Shivange Revolution]]. I do so with some reluctance. A man who writes his own history is a witness in his own case, and the reader should weigh my evidence accordingly. But I was there, and many who were there are not here to write.

To understand the year 2495 one must begin with the war. In 2483 the king, [[per:45042|Shunun]], who had himself seized the throne by force and never forgot it, resolved to prove his title by conquest, and sent our army against Talilin. The newspapers called it a war of honour; the schoolbooks now call it the [[evt:45794|Shivange–Tallin War]]. I was a clerk in the Ministry of Roads and Canals in those days, and it was my duty to read the movement orders. I knew before the public did how many columns marched out, and how many came back.

At [[evt:45807|Navnge]] in 2484, at [[evt:45832|Nodhinge]] in 2485, and at [[evt:45837|Nilinge]] in 2486, our soldiers did everything that could be asked of them. They killed, by the enemy's own admission, five men for every one they lost. It did not matter. The enemy came on with ten and more for every one of ours, and our lines were broken three times in three years. Some sixty thousand of our sons did not come home. The official bulletins spoke of glorious resistance. The casualty lists were printed in type so small that mothers bought magnifying glasses to read them.

Then came the [[evt:45838|Treaty of Nilinge]]. The king set his name to it at Nilinge. We paid an indemnity that emptied the treasury, so that the government printed [[cur:1063|khiril]] notes faster than the presses could dry them; we bound ourselves to the victor as his vassal; and we swore a truce of twenty years. A free people had been signed away by one man's pen. That was the hour, I think, when the crown began to fall, though it took nine years to reach the ground.

I will not repeat here the history of those nine years, the reading societies, the banned pamphlets, the strikes in the foundries, the night I spent in the cells beneath the central police station. Others have written them better. I will say only this: we did not make the revolution. The treaty made it. We merely refused to let it be wasted.

On the last morning the post-riders and the signal-tower clerks of the capital walked out together at eight o'clock, and by noon no order of the palace could reach a single garrison. By evening the crowds in the squares of [[set:66|Shivange]] numbered, the police later reported, more than any they had ever counted. The guards at the palace gate laid down their muskets when they saw their own brothers in the front rank. No one fired. I have been proud of many things in my life; I am proudest of that.

The king was told that the absolute monarchy was ended and that [[pol:1863|Shivange]] was a republic. He received the news, I am told, in silence. I insisted that he be treated with courtesy, and he was. Shunun died in his bed two years later, a private man. I did not attend the funeral, but I sent a wreath.

The priests of the [[mov:58|Vishithmikh]] held services of thanksgiving, and one of them, an old man I had known since boyhood, said from the pulpit that a nation released from suffering must not inflict it in turn. I tried to govern by that sermon. I did not always succeed.

I served as president until 2501, when my term ended and the people chose [[per:45262|Bumepufnen]] in my place. I handed him the seals in the same hall where the king had once received foreign envoys, and walked home through the streets with my hat in my hand. No one stopped me. That, too, is the revolution.

I, [[per:45231|Raamadhdun]], write this on 25 Mikhku 2505 of the Vishithmikh, at my desk by the window, with the carriages going by.
