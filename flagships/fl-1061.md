title: Two Thousand Two Hundred and Thirty-Eight
---
The priest came to the house on the morning of the wedding to ask what year it was.

He stood just inside the courtyard gate with his hands pushed up his sleeves and watched Natithmi's grandmother beat the octopus against the doorstep. An octopus had to be beaten forty times or it ate like a sandal. The old woman was on thirty-one. She did not stop for priests.

'The forty-fifth year of Nangatsu,' the priest said. 'That is what I would have said. It is what I have said over every couple in this quarter since the peace. The twenty-eighth year, the twenty-ninth, the thirtieth.' He drew one hand out of its sleeve and let it fall. 'Then the old king went to his fate, and since the new moon I have been saying the first year of Nabathu, and I was getting used to it. And now there is this business up at the fort.'

'What business?' said Natithmi's mother, who knew perfectly well. The whole of [[set:71|Lamlivji]] had talked of nothing else since the land gate was shut the evening before and not opened again.

'I only want to know what the family wishes. It is the family's year. I am only the mouth.'

Natithmi sat on the bench under the vine with the honey jar between her knees, pressing barley cakes together, and kept her eyes down. She was seventeen. She had been born in the year of the peace, when two kings sat down in this town and agreed that it belonged to whichever of them had won. That was nearly all she knew about kings: that they took the year with them when they came, and that until now nobody had ever had to ask which one.

'Say what you have been saying,' said her mother. 'The empress is in Dagafu. Dagafu is where the oil goes.'

'Say what you like,' said the grandmother, on thirty-six. 'In my day we didn't ask anybody.'

'In your day,' said her mother, 'you counted from the founding of Kashunji, and it took all morning to get to the end of the year.'

'The year two thousand one hundred and eighty-three from the founding of Kashunji.' The octopus came down on the step. 'That's the year I was married in. It doesn't take all morning. It takes one breath, if you've got one.'

'I married people in that count when I was young,' said the priest, and for a moment he looked almost happy. 'It was a fine count. It went along by itself. You did not have to know anybody's name.'

'And I was married in the eighth year of Nagovo,' said Natithmi's mother, 'the year of the [[evt:18179|battle at Adhgumgi]], when our king beat the Luth army, and the priest said so at my wedding, twice, because he was proud of it.' She looked at him. 'It was you.'

The priest studied the octopus.

'Forty,' said the grandmother. She stood, holding the creature by its head like a wet rag, and the brine ran down her wrist into her sleeve. 'Now it'll be tender. Go home, priest. Come back when the lamps are lit. We'll tell you then.'

* * *

In the afternoon Natithmi was sent down to the harbour for more salt, because there is never enough salt at a wedding, and so she heard the herald.

He was standing on the harbour wall where the boats tied up, a young man in a clean tunic with a bronze horn. When he had blown it twice and the gulls had settled he said it straight out, with no flourish, like a man who has been made to say the same thing at every corner since noon: [[per:18331|Nadimbulu]] was emperor of [[pol:598|Luth]]. He said nothing about the empress in Dagafu. He blew the horn again and climbed down and went off to the next place, and the fishwives looked at one another over their baskets. One of them laughed. Nobody else did.

Up at the fort the drums had started. They did not stop.

Natili found her at the salt-seller's. He had brought his brother, which she had not wanted, and he had scrubbed his hands, which she had. He held them out to her, palms up, puckered from the water.

'Sand,' he said. 'Then ash. Then sand again. Smell.'

She smelled them. They smelled of fish.

'I did my best,' he said.

'They smell of you,' she said. In four months of being promised it was the nearest either of them had come to saying anything, and he went red to the ears and looked hard at the boats.

His brother Nanüthnan had his spear and the leather cap of the fort men, and the look of someone who had been up since before light, had sworn an oath, and would like to be asked about it. Their father had come down from Luth with the army in the year of the peace, when Natili was two, and stayed. Nanüthnan had gone into the fort at fifteen and liked it there. Their mother, who had named him War, said it was her own fault for putting ideas in his head.

'Well,' said Nanüthnan. 'That's settled, then. You'll tell your priest. The first year of Nadimbulu.'

'My mother wants the empress,' said Natithmi.

'Your mother wants her oil to go up the road.'

'Yes,' said Natithmi. 'She does.'

'Dagafu never sent this town anything but tax men. They sent thirty thousand men to Adhgumgi and got beaten by fifteen hundred, and nobody ever came down here to ask why.' He shifted the spear to his other shoulder. 'He was in the yard this morning when they hailed him. His boy was with him, fifteen, holding a spear as if somebody had lent it to him. He's here. He knows where the harbour is.'

'That's what my mother's afraid of,' said Natithmi.

Natili took the salt from her. 'I don't care,' he said, 'if it's the first year of the octopus.'

They walked back up through the lanes with the salt between them and his brother a little behind, and all the way up the hill the drums went with them, like somebody else's heart.

* * *

The grandmother did the veil herself. Natithmi's mother had the cakes to see to and the oil for the lamps, and besides, the old woman had pinned her own daughter's and said nobody else knew how to make it stay.

'In my mother's house,' she said, with a pin between her teeth, 'the sea had a beard.'

Natithmi had heard this before. She let her say it.

'He was a man, and you gave him the fish heads, and he left you alone or he didn't. Then I came down the coast to marry your grandfather, and his people kept the Luth gods, and in his house the sea was a woman, [[dei:75|Bumbi]], and you gave her barley and a drop of oil, and she left you alone or she didn't.' The pin went in. 'She took him all the same, in the end. Same water.'

'Did you mind? Coming here, and everything different?'

'I was eighteen. I minded everything.' The grandmother stood back to look. 'You don't mind for long. You mind the first year. After that it's just the sea.'

Below, someone was tuning a lyre, badly. From the fort, the drums.

'Do you still say it?' Natithmi asked. 'The count?'

Everyone in the family knew that the old woman went down to the harbour wall at the first new moon of every year and said a number to the water. Natithmi's mother called it the old woman's prayer and was a little ashamed of it. The kingdom of Kashunji had fallen when Natithmi's mother was eleven. The city of Kashunji was still up the coast where it had always been, smaller every year, with nobody's name over it. Nobody had told the grandmother to stop counting, and so she had not stopped.

'Two thousand two hundred and thirty-eight,' the grandmother said. 'Since the new year. Somebody has to. Stop counting a thing and it's gone.' She took the last pin out of her mouth. 'There. Now you won't blow away.'

* * *

By the time the lamps were lit there were more people in the courtyard than it could hold, which was as it should be. Natili's people had the gate side: his mother, three uncles, men from the boats, and half a dozen of Nanüthnan's friends from the fort in their leather caps, who had come straight down from the hailing smelling of the beer that had been handed out in the yard. Natithmi's people had the vine side: the neighbours, the oil-pressers, the old women of the street, most of whom had been born in the long count, married in it, lived through Nagovo's years and buried their husbands in the old king's. Her father, who had said nothing all day, which was his gift, stood beside her mother and held the lamp.

The priest bound their wrists with a red cord, Natili's left to her right. He gave them the salt and the cake, and she ate the cake and tasted her own honey on it, and Natili ate his in one bite like a boy, and somebody laughed. Then the priest lifted his hands and named the [[mov:11|Kyif]] gods who had to hear it: [[dei:74|Bingi]], who fills the granary, so that there would be bread; Bumbi of the sea, so that the boats would come home; [[dei:77|Ugash]], who sees whatever happens under him and would see this. He named Natili, son of his father, and Natithmi, daughter of her mother. Then he said, 'In the first year of—'

He stopped.

'Nadimbulu,' said Nanüthnan, from the gate.

'Nabathu,' said Natithmi's mother, at the same moment, and not quietly.

For a breath nobody moved. Then the fort men said *Nadimbulu*, all together, the way they had been saying it since morning, and the old women said *Nabathu*, though half of them could not have told you who she was. One of Natili's uncles said the old king was not cold yet and by rights it was still the forty-fifth year of Nangatsu, and somebody told him the old king had been cold for a month and he could go and lie down beside him. The lyre player put his lyre behind his back. In the middle of it the priest stood with his hands still raised and his mouth still open, the mouth of a man who had been the mouth for forty years and had never once been asked his opinion; and Natithmi, her wrist tied to Natili's, felt the realm come apart in her own courtyard, quietly, between the vine and the gate.

She looked at her grandmother.

The old woman was sitting on the bench with a cup in her lap. She did not get up. She did not need to. She used the voice she kept for gulls on the drying racks, and the courtyard turned to her as if she had thrown a stone.

'Two thousand two hundred and thirty-eight,' she said. 'From the founding of Kashunji.'

Nobody said anything.

'Kashunji's nobody's,' said Nanüthnan at last, uncertainly.

'That's right,' said the grandmother. 'Nobody's going to come down from a fort about it. Nobody's going to send a tax man up the road about it. It's the only year in this town that nobody can take off these two.' She drank. 'Go on, priest. You know how it goes.'

Natithmi's mother began to laugh. She put her hand over her mouth and laughed into it until her eyes ran, and after a moment Nanüthnan, who was twenty-four and had sworn an oath that morning and did not know what to do with his face, laughed too, and then the fort men laughed, because he had.

The priest lowered his hands and raised them again. When it came, it came out of him whole, in the old long rhythm, the way he must have said it over his first couples forty years before, when the count was all anyone asked of him: in the year two thousand two hundred and thirty-eight from the founding of Kashunji. Natithmi and Natili were married in it, before Bingi and Bumbi and the sun, in her mother's courtyard in [[set:71|Lamlivji]], while up the hill the drums went on and on for a new emperor who did not know they were there.

'Two thousand two hundred and thirty-what?' Natili whispered, when the cord was off and the lyre had come out again.

'Eight,' she said. 'You'll have to learn it.'

'I'll learn it,' he said.

* * *

The empire of [[pol:817|Lamlivji]] lasted three years. [[per:18331|Nadimbulu]] fell at Kamvamvil in two thousand two hundred and forty, by the grandmother's count, and his son put his name to the peace at Mbil the year after and was put to death before that year was out. The empress they would call the Last outlived them both, and saw Luth come apart under her all the same; in two thousand two hundred and forty-nine the kings of [[pol:821|Khanji]] made their seat in Lamlivji.

The grandmother did not see all of that. Natithmi did. She went down to the harbour wall at the first new moon of every year, first with the old woman on her arm and then without her, and said the number to the water; and later her daughters came with her and learned it, though nobody had asked them to. Nobody ever told them to stop. Nobody, just as the old woman had promised, ever came down from a fort about it.

**Author's note.** The three ways of naming a year are the record's: Luth and Adhgumgi counted by reigns (PAL·IN·01338, PAL·IN·01321), and Kashunji counted from the founding of its city (PAL·IN·04675); "went to his fate" is the diviner's phrase for the old king's death in PAL·OM·00169. The record says nothing of a wedding, and does not say why Nadimbulu claimed the throne. It keeps the [[evt:18419|Secession of Lamlivtse]], the [[evt:18420|Sixth Luthe Civil War]] that answered it, and the city of Kashunji, which no state held from 2223.
