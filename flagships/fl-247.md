title: Two Years After the Conquest: The Historical Nitllo Mish

Few founders are as thinly documented as [[per:21913|Nitllo Mish]]. The [[mov:74|Ninshisatl]] school he founded now counts some 25 million adherents, a large following for a philosophy that, by its own principles, belongs to one people and seeks no converts. Yet no text by him, no collection of his teachings and no contemporary mention of his name has been catalogued. This essay asks what can responsibly be said about such a figure, and argues that the honest biography is short, contextual and full of warnings.

## What the record holds

Nitllo Mish was born in AY 2531, a man of the Ya'cuam people who lived at Bakh, the seat of the City-State of Vakha; the record counts him a subject of the Kingdom of Yisamashtsi, which annexed that city. The record dates the [[evt:22120|Founding of the Ninshisatl School]] at Bakh to 2574, when he was about forty-three. He died of natural causes in 2584, at about fifty-three, after some ten years of teaching. There the personal record ends. Founders of other schools are often known through a collection of teachings compiled by their pupils. For Nitllo Mish nothing of the kind has been catalogued.

## The conquest of Bakh

The context is better documented than the man, and it is striking. Vakha had been independent since AY 2550. In 2571 and 2572 the Kingdom of Yisamashtsi fought two battles at Bakh, sacking the city after each, and by a treaty signed in Bakh itself in 2572 it annexed the city. I'cuil Fayatl, who had ruled Vakha since 2550, was executed at the conquest. The conquerors' state cult was the Cult of Mie'to'ieshio. Two years later, in the conquered city, Nitllo Mish founded his school.

Its teachings, as the record summarizes them, deserve to be read against that background. Time runs in a line toward a final judgement, and many gods share the world. Truth is found by each seeker's inner light and reason, not handed down by a priesthood. The good life is one of duty, order and right sacrifice, and the wise withdraw from wealth and appetite. Above all, the school belongs to one people and does not seek converts. A philosophy that keeps a people's identity without a state, and asks nothing of the conqueror's priests, would make sense for a citizen of a city that had just lost its independence.

I state the hypothesis in order to limit it. The record counts the sack of Bakh in 2572 among the causes of the founding, but no source says how the one led to the other. We do not know whether Nitllo Mish fought in 2571, whether he lost property or kin, or whether his teaching was already formed before Vakha fell. The coincidence of date and place is real. The motive is our inference.

## A name and its traps

The second warning concerns the name. Names beginning Nitllo and Nitllu are very common in the Ya'cuam and neighbouring traditions, borne by kings, generals and letter-writers across more than four thousand years of inscriptions. At least two later men shared our philosopher's full name. The Epitaph of Nitllo Mish (PAL·IN·05598) dates from AY 3231. The Temple inscription of Nitllo Mish at Tlilisyi (PAL·IN·05563) was set up in 3287 by a ruler who lived from 3250 to 3317. A third near-namesake, Nitllu Mish, is recorded on a gate inscription at Tivintukhe from AY 249. None of these is evidence for the founder. Any story that makes him a king, a builder of temples or a man honoured with an epitaph should be checked against them first. In a tradition so rich in namesakes, a legend can be assembled from other men's monuments without anyone intending to deceive.

## Conclusion

The historical Nitllo Mish is a Ya'cuam citizen of Bakh who began teaching two years after his city was conquered and taught for ten years before his death. The [[mov:74|Ninshisatl]] doctrine tells us what his followers held. Because the school sought no converts, those followers were presumably mostly his own people's descendants, and the school's long life tells us they found its teaching worth keeping. Everything else, his character, his motives and his relation to the conquest, must remain conjecture. The [[per:21913|Nitllo Mish]] of legend can be built from the record of others. The historical one has to be left almost blank, and the blankness is the most accurate part of his portrait.
