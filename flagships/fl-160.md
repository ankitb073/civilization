[[per:26010|Ramadheluekhen]], maker of engines in the royal workshops at [[set:11|Vieneynga]], to [[per:25676|Tathrelbanaven]], king of [[pol:1013|Diebegovebenga]]: greeting, and long life.

Majesty, five years have passed since the [[evt:26397|Treaty of Yiranunyirin]] gave you that city and its country, and the people celebrate the victory. I celebrate it too. I also remember what it cost. My brother carried a spear seven years ago in the [[evt:26378|Battle of Yiranunyirin]], which you led yourself. He came home. Many others did not. The enemy were few, yet from their walls and their ditches they cut down our men by the hundred, because their archers had grown up with the bow and ours had grown up at the plough. We won three times at Yiranunyirin, in three summers one after another, and three times we buried hundreds of our own, though we stood five to their one. Since then I have asked myself one question: how can a farmer be made the equal of an archer without first spending twenty years making him one?

I believe I have found the answer, and I submit it to your judgement.

Take a bow of horn and wood, shorter and stiffer than any man could draw with his arm. Fix it crosswise at the head of a wooden stock, as a yoke lies across the pole of a cart. Cut a groove along the stock to hold a short, heavy arrow, which I call a bolt. Behind the groove set a catch of bone, turning on a pin, which holds the string once it has been drawn. Beneath the catch put a lever which, when pressed, frees the string. Put a stirrup at the front. The soldier sets his foot in the stirrup and draws the string back with both hands and the strength of his back, which is far greater than the strength of the arm. The catch holds the string. The bow now waits, drawn, for as long as he likes. He can raise it to his cheek and aim at leisure, and when he presses the lever the bolt flies.

Consider what follows. An archer has to hold the full weight of his bow while he aims, so only a strong and practised man can aim well. With my engine the bow holds itself, and a farmer can learn to use it in a month. The bolt is heavy and travels flat. At a hundred paces I have seen it go through a shield of doubled oxhide and the board behind it. It does not shoot as quickly as a bow, I admit that. But a man behind a wall can shoot without showing more than his eyes, and a man in the line can kill a mounted noble who would have laughed at him before.

There is a philosophical objection, and I will not hide it from you. Some will say that I have taken the virtue out of war: the long training, the strength and the courage that used to separate the warrior from the herdsman. I answer that a state rests on its citizens, not on a few champions. If every householder can defend his own wall, the kingdom depends less on mercenaries and on the pride of great men. That is not a loss of virtue. It spreads virtue among more people. The priests of the [[mov:2|Zhienro]] teach that the good life is one of duty and right order. What could be more orderly than a city every one of whose sons can stand on the wall?

I ask for three things. First, an order to the armoury for horn, sinew and seasoned ash, enough to make fifty of the engines. Second, a field outside the walls where I may train fifty men of the city levy, chosen by lot. Third, a judge of your choosing who will watch them shoot against fifty archers at the next feast of [[dei:14|Buevme]]. If my men do not equal them, I will melt my bronze pins and say no more about it. The cost, as nearly as I can reckon, is less than four hundred [[cur:551|Niesh]], less than a single season's pay for a company of hired bowmen.

I have made an offering to [[dei:18|Eba]] of the forge, whose fire tempered the catch, and I commend the work and its maker to your favour.

Written at Vieneynga, in the year 259 of Diebegovebenga.
