title: A Teacher in a Falling Kingdom: The Historical Ue'manim

The [[mov:83|I'ashakh]] faith reached some 86 million adherents at its height, a modest figure beside the great universal faiths but a large one for a movement that began in a single city of a small kingdom. Its founder, [[per:23440|Ue'manim]], has suffered the usual fate of founders. He has been given the biography his followers needed, and in his case he has also been confused with others. This essay separates what the record establishes from what has been added, and suggests that the most important fact about the historical Ue'manim is one the legend rarely mentions: he taught in a kingdom that was about to disappear.

## The record

Ue'manim was born in AY 2659, a man of the Nishmam people. The record places the [[evt:23650|Founding of the I'ashakh Faith]] at Pivithie in 2694, when he was about thirty-five. Pivithie was then the seat of the Kingdom of Pibetie, whose king, Ipil Lieze II, the record calls "the Unlucky", and whose state cult was the La'im religion. The epithet was earned. In 2699 the Ana Empire took Pibetie in the Third Ana–Pibetie War, after a battle fought at Pivithie itself, and the kingdom was annexed. Ue'manim died in 2703, of natural causes, at about forty-four. He taught for nine years, and the last four of them were spent as a subject of a conquering empire.

It is tempting to connect this with his teaching. The I'ashakh says little of the world's beginning or end. It asks for duty, order and right sacrifice, and it honours teachers while denying that any of them owns the truth. A doctrine that turns attention from cosmic history to present conduct would suit people whose own history had just been taken from them. The temptation should be noted and resisted. No source tells us what Ue'manim thought of the conquest, or whether his teaching changed after it.

## The sayings

The Sayings of Ue'manim (PAL·SC·00046) survive on parchment and date from AY 2749, forty-six years after his death. Their heading claims that the words were "gathered by those who heard him", but their compiler, the priest Yi Mu'a, was born in 2712, nine years after Ue'manim died. The contents follow the familiar pattern of the genre. "Do not ask what was before the world; ask what is before you", "Seek a teacher, but in the end you must walk the road yourself" and "The lamp does not argue with the dark; it shines" all appear in sayings collections centuries older. So do the remaining lines. The collection is a faithful summary of what the community taught, assembled from inherited formulae. It is not a record of a voice.

## Three confusions

The first confusion is of names. A sect of the Veshethmekh, founded in AY 3303 by Lamfin Mu'a, also bore the name I'ashakh. Any account of Ue'manim that draws on material about that later movement goes astray, and the two must be kept apart.

The second is of place. Pivithie produced another founder within the same generation. Uzholpaya, who died in 2684, founded the Is'ava school there in 2668. It is easy to imagine the younger man as the older man's pupil, and the city as a school of founders. No source connects them, and I know of no evidence that they ever met.

The third is of scale. At the founding the faith held about three districts, and six centuries later only five or six. The first ruler to adopt it, in the record, was the queen of Myizinitie in AY 3606, more than nine centuries after the [[evt:23650|Founding of the I'ashakh Faith]]. The edicts that establish or proscribe it come later still, the most recent in printed gazettes of the last century. The faith's growth belongs almost entirely to the last fifteen hundred years. It was not carried by its founder.

The historical [[per:23440|Ue'manim]], then, is a Nishmam teacher of Pivithie who taught for nine years, saw his kingdom fall, and died under foreign rule. His community remembered him in formulae it already knew and kept the [[mov:83|I'ashakh]] alive for centuries before any ruler took notice. That is a smaller story than the legend. It is also a more credible one.
