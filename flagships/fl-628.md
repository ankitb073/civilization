Two stones carry the name of [[per:20699|Tlamfen Yueva I]] in his own voice. One is a relief cut into living rock near Myizinithie; the other is a limestone stele that stood near Listlitl. Both are weathered but legible, and both say the same thing in nearly the same words: he was "great king, king of Ana", he defeated the enemy, he killed a stated number of soldiers, and he set the monument up for his god, asking that the enemy's name be destroyed. Neither stone commemorates a battle he fought himself. His whole life sits in the gap between what he cut into stone and what the record allows.

## A usurper after usurpers

He was born in AY 2422, a man of the Lizo people, and his name means "Nine Fire". Of his parents the record says nothing at all, which in a royal life is a statement in itself: he did not come to the throne of [[pol:756|Ana]] by blood. He married a woman four years his elder, and in 2439 she bore the son who would one day reign as [[per:20730|Tlamfen Yueva II]]. A second boy, born in 2445, did not live out the year. His wife died in 2479. These are the only household facts we have, and they are the facts of most households in the empire: a marriage, a living son, a dead one.

The empire he took was old, large and unsettled. Its law gave the crown to the eldest son, but that law had been broken twice by the time he was four. A usurper seized the throne in 2421, the year before his birth, and was himself displaced five years later by [[per:20362|Yalni Shim'a]], who ruled for thirty-four years and spent them conquering neighbours. When Yalni Shim'a died in 2460, of natural causes and at seventy, the record marks a crisis, and in the same year Tlamfen Yueva, then thirty-eight, took the throne.

A usurper's court has every reason to call the [[evt:20866|Usurpation of Tlamfen Yueva I]] a rescue of the state from disorder. The record is plainer. It does not say he killed anyone. It says one usurper died, another took his place, and the empire's internal order sank to its lowest ebb of the century in the decade that followed. He was the third usurper in forty years, and he spent the next forty-three years ruling as though he knew it.

## War as a title to rule

The record lists his seizure of power among the causes of both great wars of his reign. The first was the [[evt:20882|Second Holy War of Ana against Miezenitie]], begun in 2464, which renewed a holy war Yalni Shim'a had fought in the 2420s. [[pol:891|Miezenitie]] was a small kingdom ruled since 2435 by [[per:20428|Ea Lieze]], and the imbalance of forces was grotesque. At the [[evt:20889|Battle of Nyinsitlasa (AY 2465)]] Tlamfen Yueva led 54,835 men against 5,845. He won, at a price the court never inscribed: Ana lost 9,097 men and the enemy 1,182. Nearly eight Ana soldiers died for each of the enemy, most of them, we may suppose, conscripts from the villages. A second battle on the same ground the next year, under another commander, went the same way. The Miezenitie account of the war is lost except for a fragment quoted two centuries later in the Ana account of the Third Ana–Pibetie War. That fragment says that in 2466, "by treachery, our men could not hold the field". It passes over 2465 in silence.

The decisive blow fell at the [[evt:20903|Battle of Faamni (AY 2468)]], where the king, with 72,998 men, broke 5,725 under [[per:20725|Inemovi]]. The enemy capital was sacked that year. By the [[evt:20905|Treaty of Myizinithie (AY 2468)]], sworn in the plundered city of [[set:15|Myizinithie]], Miezenitie gave up eight districts, owed tribute for fourteen years, accepted a fifteen-year truce and agreed to take up the faith of Ana. The fragment says its king, "wearied of bloodshed", granted peace. Defeated chroniclers write like that, and it may also have been true.

The second war was the [[evt:20980|Fourth Ana–Nimit War]] (2475–2480). [[pol:747|Nimit]] had been badly beaten by another neighbour in 2473, and the record names that defeat as a cause alongside Tlamfen Yueva's own usurpation. The Nimit account of the war, a palace chronicle of 2485, opens with an accusation: he "broke the peace and sent raiders across the border". At the [[evt:20989|Battle of Listlitl (AY 2476)]] he defeated [[per:20831|Miyuf]]. The Nimit scribe claims his side lost 836 men and Ana 13,401, and blames neglected omens; the record gives 2,880 and 5,121. Other Ana commanders fought the next two battles. In 2480, at fifty-eight, the king took the field once more at the [[evt:21029|Battle of Likhtli (AY 2480)]], with 88,742 men against 10,349 under [[per:20766|Lushahimim]]. Ana lost 10,153 men that day, its heaviest single loss of the reign. The [[evt:21030|Treaty of Likhtli (AY 2480)]], sworn at [[set:214|Likhtli]], handed over forty-one districts and four towns, Listlitl and Likhtli among them, with a heavy indemnity and a fifteen-year truce. The Nimit chronicle calls the losses "some villages of no account ... which had always been poor". Nimit never recovered: a province rose against it the following year, and in 2491 the kingdom collapsed.

## The reign by other hands

After Likhtli he never commanded again, but the wars went on in his name. Ea Lieze died in 2477 and his son [[per:20733|Pashsash]] succeeded him. In 2479 Miezenitie, following the will of the majority, abandoned the faith imposed eleven years before, and the record treats that reversal as a cause of the [[evt:21074|Ana–Miezenitie War]] (2486–2489). Ana's generals won twice and besieged the capital; in 2489 the city was sacked a second time, and now its archives burned. Much of what we cannot know about Miezenitie went up in that fire. The [[evt:21096|Treaty of Myizinithie (AY 2489)]] made the kingdom a vassal paying tribute for thirty-one years, and it fell apart in 2505. The rock relief belongs to this war. It says Tlamfen Yueva "killed 5,400 soldiers" beneath the walls in 2488. The record counts 736 enemy dead and names another man as commander; the king was sixty-six.

The last war was the [[evt:21182|Ana–Ihamiw War]] (2495–2499), against [[pol:911|Ihamiw]], the kingdom that had risen from Nimit's ruins. It scarcely deserves the name. In four battles the record sets Ana armies of 49,000 to 92,000 men against Ihamiw forces of a few dozen. Ihamiw's king, [[per:20984|Ihige]], was killed in the last of them near [[set:134|Listlitl]], and the [[evt:21230|Treaty of Listlitl (AY 2499)]] took twenty-two districts and bound the kingdom to thirty-eight years of tribute as a vassal. The limestone stele raised there claims a hundred enemy dead; the record counts seven. More troubling is the other column: Ana lost nearly 21,000 men in a war in which the enemy lost thirty-three. The record does not say what killed them, and neither does the stele.

## Death and what followed

He died in 2503, about eighty-one years old, of natural causes. The [[evt:21278|Accession of Tlamfen Yueva II]] that year was the first passage of the crown from father to son in Ana for ninety-five years, and it may be the achievement he would most have wanted remembered. Over his reign the empire grew by more than a quarter, the population of its towns nearly doubled, and the treasury swelled on tribute and indemnity from three defeated kingdoms.

The modern reader knows how little of it lasted. His son, already sixty-four, held off an invasion in his first years but was overthrown by another usurper in 2517 and died that same year, and the dynasty ended with him. In 2521 the former Nimit lands rose in revolt from one of the very towns ceded at Likhtli, and in 2530 the Ana Empire broke into six successor states. The old usurper had made war the proof of his right to rule. When the wars stopped paying, his house had no other proof.

The two stones still stand, the most durable things he made and the most misleading. The men who died at Nyinsitlasa and on the road to Ihamiw are a truer record of his reign.
