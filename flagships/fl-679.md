Everything [[per:43826|Ipil Nyime]] is known to have written dates from a single year. In AY 4593 he wrote to his king and filed a patent, and a newspaper reported on him. He was a man of the Lizo, born in AY 4559, and his name means *Seven Flower*. It was not a rare name: a usurper who bore it had seized the Ölie Empire, the state from which [[pol:1656|Ane]] descended, in AY 3993. The record is silent on Ipil's parents, any wife or children, and his schooling. He died in AY 4596, of natural causes, at thirty-seven.

The record's one entry for his life is a large one. The [[evt:44391|Invention of movable-type printing in Ane]] is the earliest in the record: no state anywhere held the art before Ane did in AY 4593. The next was the Kingdom of Tsey, ten years later.

## A kingdom of clerks

He was born fifteen years into the thirty-eight-year reign of [[per:43432|Shoe Luisha II]]. The survey of the 4560s counts 22,400,878 people in a feudal kingdom of 233 districts, with 264,124 men under arms. The record puts the capital, [[set:7|Ane]], at about 534,000 people in AY 4500 and nearly 600,000 by 4600. The coin was the silver [[cur:944|Myiu]] of 6 grams.

Ane was a winning state. In the [[evt:44323|Second Ane–Khyie War]] (4583–4585), when Ipil was in his mid-twenties, its army at Zhaalakhazhash outnumbered the enemy more than eight to one, 137,136 men against 15,882. The Treaty of Kheye gave Ane six districts, an indemnity, trading rights and a twenty-year truce. The throne was less steady. Shoe Luisha II died in 4582, and three kings followed in seven years. The third, Ishuishima II, the king Ipil would petition, was six years his junior. In 4590 the kingdom became an absolute monarchy. Its faith, [[mov:25|Mie'to'ieshio]], taught that truth is kept by an ordained priesthood and that the wise withdraw from wealth and appetite.

## One day in Muie

The letter, complete on paper in the state archive of Ane, is dated "25 Muie 4069 of the Myitiyishiu, in the fifth year of Your Majesty's reign", that is, AY 4593. It is a petition, and should be read as advocacy. It calls its author a "founder in metals and lately a maker of books" and sums up the method in a line: "Thus the same hundred pieces of metal may print a thousand different pages." The art had been "tested through three winters and many failures."

He makes his case to a king with new provinces to govern. "With pens this is the work of a hundred clerks for a year." Printed edicts would be "every copy the same to the last stroke, so that no clerk may alter a word by malice or by sleep." He expected the priests to object and answered them on their own ground: "the press multiplies whatever is set before it, and nothing else. Let the priests license what is printed". He asked for twenty years in which no one else in Ane could cast type without his licence, and for four hundred Myiu: "I do not ask for wealth; I ask for lead, tin, and time." That sum was 2.4 kilograms of silver, roughly a millionth of the treasury at the end of the 4590s.

The late edition of The Ane Observer carries the same date: "Ipil Nyime has demonstrated movable-type printing before a crowd of investors. Sceptics were silenced." So on the same day he was courting investors as well as the crown. Its grain price, 1.55 Myiu the measure, matches the record for that decade. It is the oldest newsprint in the archive, and the record does not say whose press printed it. His patent, PAL·PT·00001, is the archive's first. Its two clauses are "a single founder may cast a thousand alike in a day" and "The claims are limited to the arrangement described." It mentions neither the twenty years nor the money, and the record does not say whether he received either.

## First, or borrowed?

The letter is modest about priority: "I do not claim to have drawn it out of nothing." Merchants, it says, had brought "sheets printed in far countries by some like method", yet "I have not seen any man in Ane practise it before me." The record takes him at his word and then contradicts itself. It files his invention as a borrowing but names no earlier press anywhere. It counts the invention of Nazhige of Dagafu in 4604 as independent, yet the first clause of Nazhige's patent repeats Ipil's word for word, and so does Kathkun's of Kunnga in 4614. The documents suggest that the borrowing ran outward from Ane, beyond the reach of any privilege its king could grant.

## What the press multiplied

The press did exactly what the letter promised. In 4596, the year Ipil died, Wishsaluisha's account of an old war became the first chronicle the archive catalogues as a printed book (PAL·CH·07797). Of Lishimti in 4573 it says: "Of the enemy 4,510 were slain; of ours, 121." The record gives 2,332 and 577. It claims "15 districts in all" for a treaty that ceded seven, much as Pitlshe's handwritten account of the Khyie war had turned six districts into twelve. Every copy was the same, errors included. That year the Ane Herald announced the [[evt:44465|Second Ane–Zhukhi War]]: "Recruiting offices opened at dawn."

Change came slowly. In 4601 a word list for Ane's scribes was still written on a clay tablet. The earliest surviving printed edict of Ane, from 4615, orders paper notes to "pass in all payments at their face value". Those notes replaced the Myiu in which Ipil had asked for his four hundred. No piece of his type survives, and Nambili's report on his excavation of Ane in AY 4920 (PAL·EX·00049) lists no press among its finds. Nabuli wrote this year that "the printing press of AY 4593 left little immediate mark either, and only later readers saw what it had begun" (PAL·SP·00045). Ipil saw none of it. He asked his king for twenty years and lived for three.
