Of [[per:36833|Iminimin]] the record keeps a birth, a death and one act. He was born in AY 3869, a man of the Nishmam people and a subject of the Kingdom of [[pol:1398|Imshi]]. In 3916, at forty-seven, he founded the [[mov:137|Shamu]] at the old port of [[set:131|Shiüzhi]]. He died in 3930, of natural causes, at sixty-one. His name means ‘storm storm’. Of parents, wife, children, trade or face the record says nothing. Three documents carry his name, and none came out of the ground. An archaeologist has no house of his to dig: only his arguments, and words later put into his mouth.

## A kingdom that kept winning

Imshi was a feudal kingdom with an elective throne, ruled from the city of [[set:116|Imshi]]; its state faith was the [[mov:69|Kievi]]. Every war it fought in his lifetime ended in its favour, among them the [[evt:36894|First Imshi–Shathi War]] (3885–3894), the [[evt:37134|Tenth Yimla Trade War]] (3900–3906) and the [[evt:37296|Second Imshi–Shathi War]] (3912–3917). In the second Shathi war Imshi outnumbered the enemy in every battle by between thirty and sixty-four to one, and the whole war cost it 272 men. The Tenth Yimla Trade War, begun under [[per:36521|Shiilu I]], was dearer: it won seven districts but cost Imshi 25,363 men against Linath's 3,885, 24,365 of them in one defeat at Lininadhe in 3904. Between the surveys of the 3870s and the 3910s the kingdom grew from 192 districts to 221.

## The disputation

The one narrative of the schism is Iminimin's own. The Record of the Disputation at Shiüshi (PAL·CH·08447), an original parchment in the university library at Imshi, was written "in the 16th year of Shiilu", that is, of Shiilu II: AY 3917. The elders of the [[mov:58|Veshethmekh]] had written to the court against him, and a royal steward, "being of the faith of Kievi and no party to the quarrel", heard the case. Iminimin affirms three doctrines of the old faith: time running to a judgement, one divine power, an ordained priesthood. "In none of these things have I departed by a hair." He differs on two. Riches are a sign of the One's favour, and the faith belongs to one people.

His proof of the first was the war: "At Ninshinuesnun his captains were victorious; at Nuvun the king himself rode in the van". The record bears him out: Uzhuliluesha commanded at Ninshinuesnun in 3916, Shiilu II at Nuvun in 3917, and what remained of Shathi was annexed that year. His defence of the second was a figure of speech: "a vineyard must be walled before it is enlarged." He records the steward's smile, the elders' silence, and younger clergy crossing the chamber to his side. No account by the elders survives to set against his.

The record's map of faiths complicates this. On it, Shiüzhi's own district was never Veshethmekh: it followed the Kievi from the 3340s and, from 3899, the Cult of Mie'to'ieshio. By 3919 the map gives the Shamu seven districts. Three lay around Shiüzhi in Iith, including the district the record calls the Nishmam homeland, and had followed the Kievi or the cult. Four lay far off in Yimla and Ayshi, all formerly Veshethmekh, and three of them won from Shathi in 3894 and from Linath in 3906. A doctrine that read conquest as blessing took root partly in conquered land. Who its converts there were, the record does not say.

## Last words, borrowed words

Against the Veshethmekh (PAL·PH·00037), dated 3930, the year of his death, is complete and brief: three charges and a closing vow, "We will not return until they repent." It comes from a private collection of uncertain origin. Two of its charges, on "who may teach" and on "what the good life requires", fall on ground he had declared shared in 3917. Prosperity goes unmentioned. That same year, in the [[evt:37476|Imshi–Kunnga War]], Shiilu II led 93,347 men against 12,999 at Anavun and was beaten, losing 15,738. The record cannot say whether the silence and the defeat are connected.

The treatise holds that "the old books themselves are against" a faith for all peoples. The earliest collection of the founder's sayings, the Sayings of Tonofmalen (PAL·SC·00030), is a clay tablet of about 2290, dug up at Zhinontunu in 4899. It says the opposite: "This teaching is not for one people but for every nation; carry it to the ends of the roads."

The Sayings of Iminimin (PAL·SC·00081), a papyrus from Shiüzhi, was compiled in 3968 by the priest Aimaf, who was nine when Iminimin died. Its first four sayings repeat, word for word, the first four of Tonofmalen's. Where Tonofmalen's sends the teaching down the roads, Aimaf's reads "This is the way of our fathers; let others keep the ways of theirs", a line already in the Sayings of Bedonin (PAL·SC·00009) of AY 887. "The gods love a full granary and an open hand" stands in the Sayings of Nakavzi (PAL·SC·00001) of AY 106. The closing lamp is first found in the Sayings of Eshievashie (PAL·SC·00002), the founding text of the Mie'to'ieshio. Nadhash has shown that Tonofmalen's lines came from a common stock (PAL·SP·00058). Nothing in this one can be shown to be his alone.

## What the wall kept

The wall held, and enclosed little. The Shamu never spread beyond its first seven districts, and no state ever made it its faith. Shiüzhi had passed to the Kievi by 4039. After 4199 a single district in Ayshi kept it, for 240 years, until 4439. The record dates the movement's end to 4449, 533 years after [[evt:37371|The Shamu Schism]] and almost four centuries after the kingdom of Imshi fell. Of the ten offshoots the record derives from the Veshethmekh, the Shamu alone taught that the faith belonged to one people, and it alone has ended.

His argument was that the winners were beloved, and his sect outlived the winning. What I most want to know the record does not keep: the names of the poor fed at its doors, as he promised the elders they would be.
