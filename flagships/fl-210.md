When I translated the Tuptsinga account of the [[evt:2439|Second Chinontonu–Tuptsinga War]] (PAL·CH·00342, Veruldu, excavated at Dininge in AY 4913), I remarked on its completeness. I did not then test its numbers. I do so here, against the record and the rival Chinontonu account (PAL·CH·00341, Balman, excavated at Zhinontunu in AY 4899). The tablet I praised is the better guide to what happened, but its figures are scaled as ruthlessly as any.

## Background and dates

The war of AY 598–610 was a second round. In the [[evt:2340|First Chinontonu–Tuptsinga War]] (AY 585–587), [[pol:88|Chinontonu]] had taken Linnshal and other districts, sacked Dininge (the [[evt:2360|First Sack of Dininge]]) and imposed a tribute that was still being paid when the second war began. Ununkutnakin wrote the Tuptsinga account in AY 633, under the queen who had made the peace, and he had been a youth during the fighting. Lulrinpabukin wrote the Chinontonu account in AY 667, "in the 1st year of Pinin", a year before his kingdom ended. He was born nearly thirty years after the war. His tablet is water-stained, but its gaps fall inside sentences, never across a year.

## Field by field

*Who began it.* In the record, Chinontonu attacked, to take territory. Ununkutnakin agrees: "Shyepdominin of Tsinontonu, forgetting the oaths his fathers swore, set his hand against Tuptsinga." Lulrinpabukin reverses it: "there came word that Kutpupnon of Tupchinga had gathered an army against Chinontonu." (He uses the name of the older kingdom from which [[pol:99|Tuptsinga]] descended.)

*The battles.* The record has eight, four won by each side, all in [[reg:9|Tuvizhinge]]. Ununkutnakin has all eight, with the right victors. Lulrinpabukin has five: the four Chinontonu victories and one defeat, at Dininge in AY 605. He omits the [[evt:2451|Battle of Dininge (AY 599)]], the [[evt:2491|Battle of Dininge (AY 604)]] and the [[evt:2516|Battle of Linnshal (AY 606)]]. The middle omission can be detected from his own calendar, which runs from "the 36th year of Shyepdominin" in AY 603 to "the 1st year of Mometnut" in AY 605. Between those dates the record has King Shyepdominin I killed at Dininge. Ununkutnakin keeps the scene: "Shyepdominin himself was killed, and his head was brought to the king."

*Commanders.* The record names two royal dead, Shyepdominin I in AY 604 and Kutpupnon of Tuptsinga at the [[evt:2532|Battle of Pinpidhzhi (AY 609)]]. Ununkutnakin records both, including his own king's: "There fell Kutpupnon, whose name shall be remembered." Lulrinpabukin records only the enemy's.

*The dead.* This is where my praise must be qualified. The record gives Chinontonu 2,140 dead and Tuptsinga 2,100. Ununkutnakin claims 6,089 and 839. In each of his eight entries the Chinontonu figure is about 2.85 times the record's and the Tuptsinga figure about 40 per cent of it. Lulrinpabukin claims 215 and 5,145, with his own dead at about 22 per cent of the record's and the enemy's at about 3.2 times, again in every entry. One curiosity shows how mechanical this was. For the [[evt:2485|Battle of Zhinontunu (AY 603)]], Ununkutnakin writes "131 of ours were lost, but 125 of the enemy", conceding more Tuptsinga dead than Chinontonu ones. It looks like candour but is not. The record gives 327 and 44, and the multipliers did the rest.

*Outcome and land.* The record calls the war a stalemate. The [[evt:2540|Treaty of Pinpidhzhi (AY 610)]] restored the status quo and imposed a ten-year truce, and no district changed hands. Both chroniclers agree with the record that no land moved, and both claim victory with the same formula, each aimed at the other. In Ununkutnakin, "Tsinontonu, having gained nothing, withdrew to its own land". In Lulrinpabukin, "Tupchinga, having gained nothing, withdrew to its own land."

## Two courts and their dead

The difference between the tablets is less about honesty than about how each court treated a dead king. Ununkutnakin's court could mourn Kutpupnon, because his successor had made a peace that kept every district. Lulrinpabukin's court, near its end, wanted a war that had begun as a punishment and ended in the enemy's retreat. A king's head carried off at Dininge had no place in that story. So the battle went, and with it, very nearly, the year.

My earlier judgement therefore stands only in part. For the sequence of events, the Tuptsinga account is the better witness. For the figures, it is no better than its rival.
