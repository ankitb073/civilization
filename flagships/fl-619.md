Usurpers are usually brief. They take a throne in a crisis, hold it while the crisis lasts, and give way to the next strong man or to the old dynasty's revenge. [[per:25989|A'initlue'e I]] did none of this. He seized the throne of [[pol:922|Ana]] in AY 2928, held it for fifty-two years, died in his bed at ninety-one, and left a house that ruled until the empire itself came to an end. His name means "sword raven". This chapter asks how a man with no recorded ancestry held the throne of Ana for half a century, and what his long success cost.

## A man from nowhere

The record knows nothing of his parents. He was born in AY 2889, a Lizo, and he appears in public life only at thirty-nine, in the year of the [[evt:26322|Usurpation of A'initlue'e I]]. The record marks that year as a crisis. Queen [[per:25474|Pien'aliluesh'a]], who had reigned since 2906 and won a trade war in her time, died of natural causes in 2928, and A'initlue'e took the throne rather than inheriting it. Ana's law favoured the eldest son. A man of no lineage could not inherit; he could only seize.

What made the seizure possible? The court of the new house later had every reason to forget, and the record says nothing of who stood behind him. But the realm he took was troubled. The record's measure of its cohesion fell sharply in the decade of his seizure. Ana was also already at war: the [[evt:26308|Second Ana–Namosash War]] had begun in 2927 against the kingdom of [[pol:1118|Namosash]]. An empire at war with a dead queen needed a commander, and A'initlue'e was one.

## The general who became king

He proved it at once. At the [[evt:26344|Battle of Yyixi (AY 2929)]], a year after his usurpation, he led 123,319 men against 19,752 under King [[per:25954|Ea Tlie II]] and won. His diorite stele near the field, the Victory stele of A'initlue'e I: Battle of Yyixi (PAL·IN·01945), claims 22,800 enemy dead, three times the record's 7,518. That boast mattered more than most. A usurper's legitimacy rested on victory, and the stele was an argument for his right to rule.

The [[evt:26383|Treaty of Namosash (AY 2931)]] survives on bronze (PAL·TR·01748). "Peace is granted by A'initlue'e, emperor of Ane," it reads after the opening invocation. Namosash gave up twenty-four districts and three towns, with "the river Itlte to be the frontier", and owed tribute for eleven years. A boundary stone found in Yizamashti carries his name and a curse on the enemy. Within three years of seizing the throne he had made it hard to dispute.

## The long middle of the reign

The rest of his wars he directed from the capital. Sixteen years after the first peace with Namosash he attacked again. In the [[evt:26522|Third Ana–Namosash War]] (2947–2950) his generals won every battle, sacked [[set:30|Namosash]] itself and burned its archives. The [[evt:26567|Treaty of Namosash (AY 2950)]] took eleven more districts and two towns and made the kingdom a vassal. A second stele, for Yyixi in 2949, claims the victory in the king's name, though a general commanded there. The cost was out of proportion: 62,854 dead for Ana against 7,581 for Namosash. Namosash never recovered, and in 2969 the kingdom collapsed. Ea Tlie II, who had signed both treaties, outlived his state by thirty-four years.

The [[evt:26621|Ana–Yimola War]] (2956–2958) followed the same pattern against [[pol:1114|Yimola]]. The [[evt:26657|Treaty of Tiaiosh (AY 2958)]], sworn with [[per:25820|Tvivunen II]] after the sack of [[set:240|Tiaiosh]], took five districts and fourteen years of tribute. It bought the king another boundary stone in Yizamashti and cost Ana 36,295 dead.

## The war that was long and dear

His last war was the hardest. The [[evt:26734|Second Ana–Tamesitsiyi War]] began in 2964, when he was seventy-five, and lasted eleven years against the kingdom of [[pol:1115|Tamesitsiyi]] and its queen, Miekhönil II. Its worst day was Likha in 2967. There an Ana army of 198,170 lost 75,343 men to a defending force of 32,888. The queen's stele claims 410,400 Ana dead, a figure larger than the army. Four decades later the epic called The Song of Yi'otl (PAL·PO·00009) remembered the day more honestly than either court: "Seventy thousand of Ane, so the lists say, lay down at Likha and did not rise." The poet adds: "a city is not great by the count of its spears, but by the measure of its counsel."

Then came the plague. The Long Sickness of 2970–2975 killed, by the record's count, nearly a million and a half people in thirty-four cities, about one in six of those it reached. It struck Ana and its enemy alike, and the war went on through it. At Ivu in 2974 a general beat the new king of Tamesitsiyi. A'initlue'e, now eighty-six, raised a stele claiming 38,200 enemy dead (PAL·IN·01980), where the record has 4,959. The [[evt:26862|Treaty of Faamni (AY 2975)]], sworn with the young [[per:26485|Oe Ada]] at [[set:174|Faamni]], took twenty-six districts and two towns, imposed tribute for thirty-two years, and declared that Tamesitsiyi "acknowledges the overlordship of Ane and shall send its levies when summoned" (PAL·TR·01775). Over the whole war Ana lost 158,374 soldiers and Tamesitsiyi 46,112.

The surviving text of the Song never names the king. It names his generals, the enemy queen and the fallen. Its question, "What did the fallen buy?", is answered in the poet's own terms: "the furrow and the law". It is the answer a court poet would give.

## The old king and his heirs

He lived to see his realm grow from about 261 districts and fourteen million people on the eve of his usurpation to some 328 districts and eighteen million at his death. He also lived to bury his elder children. A son and a daughter, both born in 2913, died in 2978 and 2965. The heir who survived him, [[per:26573|A'initlue'e II]], was sixty-four when the [[evt:26929|Accession of A'initlue'e II]] took place in 2980, after his father's natural death. He reigned two years, and his son reigned one. Only with the next king, the founder's great-grandson, who reigned for thirty-eight years, did the house settle again.

The sword raven's house ruled [[pol:922|Ana]] until 3086, when the empire broke into successor states under the last king of his line. Few usurpers founded anything so durable. The record shows us how it was done: by victory claimed in stone and paid for in men, by treaties that turned enemies into tributaries, and by an extraordinary length of life. [[per:25989|A'initlue'e I]] remains what his name promised, a raven over a battlefield. The poet who would not name him may have understood that best.
