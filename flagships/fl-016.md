Nakathash, queen of [[pol:686|Nilu]] by the favour of the One Power, to the elders and the people of [[set:5|Nilu]], greeting.

I write to you from the camp in [[reg:24|Pièvi]], three days after the field at [[set:219|Lamlikulu]]. I write so that you hear the truth from me before rumour reaches you. Rumour runs faster than any courier and arrives swollen and crooked, and a people that governs itself by reason should not be governed by rumour.

Hear it plainly, then. At Lamlikulu the king of [[pol:629|Puvu]], [[per:16411|Napiningi]], came against us with more than twenty thousand men. [[per:16564|Naluvu]], who held the command that day, had scarcely seven thousand. Our ranks held from the morning until the light failed, and when they drew back they drew back in order, with their standards raised and their wounded carried on their shields. Of ours, one thousand three hundred fell; I have read every name. Of theirs, the field was left to them to count, but our scouts and the smoke of their pyres tell me not fewer than a thousand. Let Puvu call that a victory. One more victory of that kind and Napiningi will be king over a country of widows.

You will ask, as reasonable citizens ought, why we are still in this country in the ninth year of the [[evt:16569|Holy War of Nilu against Puvu]]. I will answer as I would wish to be answered, and set down our losses alongside our successes.

In the second year I led you myself at [[set:191|Shimbayi]], with seven thousand against more than fifteen, and we left near three thousand of the enemy on that plain for fewer than a thousand of ours. I will not hide Dadifi from you, nor the second field at Shimbayi, where I gave ground. Remember, though, that we yielded the ground there and not the army, and they were two to our one. [[per:16514|Navande]], whom I faced twice at Shimbayi, has since gone before the judgement, and his king has had to take the field in person. In the fifth year [[per:16516|Nalavili]] paid back Dadifi at Shimbayi. In the seventh he broke them at [[set:212|Dambizhini]]: three and a half thousand of theirs fell there against fifteen hundred of ours, though they were twenty thousand and we were ten. Last year at [[set:119|Kagga]], [[per:16549|Nashinjè]] was pressed back, and even there he made them pay more than a thousand of their men for the field.

So on every field of this war that we have won we have killed far more than we have lost, and where we lost the field we made them pay for it. Puvu does not fight with right. It fights with numbers. Its people kneel to a crowd of carved and painted gods, and its king seems to think that a crowd of gods can be justified by a crowd of men, so he drives his subjects onto our spears the way a farmer throws chaff to the wind. That is the mark of a power that does not believe in its own cause.

Some in the assembly say that the One Power, which leads time in a single line from the making of the world to its judgement, cannot want so long a war. I reply that the One does not measure justice by the calendar. Each of you has the inner light by which to judge, and I ask you to use it rather than lend your ears to the timid. Moderation is a virtue in peace. In war, moderation means neither giving up a just cause too early nor pressing it with cruelty. I have forbidden the burning of their fields, and I have hanged two of our own men for plundering a shrine. The One sees that.

I ask three things of you. Raise the spring levy without delay. Pay the war tax in [[cur:359|Fiyi]] that the council has put off since winter, because bread for an army is not cheap when grain stands near a fiyi and a half the measure. And keep the lamps lit in the houses of prayer of the [[mov:24|Uthungala]], so that the soldiers who return to you will find a city that remembered them.

Prosperity is the sign of the One's favour, and our prosperity has not deserted us. Farewell.

From the camp in Pièvi, in the year 1992 of the Uthungala.
