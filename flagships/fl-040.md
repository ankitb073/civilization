title: Memoir of a Lancer of Yidhe in the War for Tuvizhinge
---
In the name of the One, who leads all things from their making to the judgement, and whose servants the old gods are: I, an old lancer of [[pol:1562|Yidhe]], who held land from my lord and owed him my body in war, set down what I saw of the war in [[reg:9|Tuvizhinge]]. I set it down so that my sons will know what their father owed and what he paid, and so that the house of prayer may keep it.

The clerks of the treasury call it the [[evt:38627|Second Yidhe–Lienunushal War]]. We who rode in it called it the war of the fourteen summers, because I was nineteen when the banners went out from [[set:0|Tallin]] and past thirty when they came home. My lord rode in the following of the noble [[per:38104|Tutafbafin]], and I rode behind my lord, as it is written that the servant shall follow where the master's lamp goes.

In the second summer we came to [[set:193|Uthtafpu]]. Our host covered the plain like locusts, more than two hundred thousand men, and [[per:38105|Namilin]], who held the field for [[pol:1430|Linunushal]], had not half of that. We broke him. I saw the priests going among the wounded at evening, giving water to ours and to theirs alike, for the teaching says that suffering has no nation. Many of ours lay among the dead. I will not set down how many, because the number is with the One.

I must also write of the bitter years, since a memoir that keeps only the sweet is a lying book. At [[set:6|Zhinontunu]] our lord the emperor [[per:37966|Dipimtin]] gave battle in the third summer, and the field went against us. In the fourth summer at [[set:177|Pikhtunge]] it went against us again. The queen of that country, [[per:37847|Buyithnimagunath]], who ruled [[pol:1432|Riniy]] as well and brought its men to the war, was a hard enemy. I do not say this to her shame. It is no shame to an eagle to be called fierce. Of those two years I remember mostly the rain, the burying, and the silence round the fires.

Six times in that war we came before Zhinontunu, and three times the One gave it to us. The emperor himself broke them there in the sixth summer. The noble Tutafbafin broke them in the ninth, and again in the eleventh, when twenty-eight thousand of the enemy lay in the reeds and our losses were light. On that last day the queen herself led them, and I saw her standard go down the river road.

The greatest day was at [[set:292|Rini]], in the eighth summer. The chroniclers call it the [[evt:38698|Eighth Battle of Rin]], because our fathers had fought on that ground seven times before us. [[per:38164|Shuyinlun]] led the host of Linunushal there, and it was a vast host. When the sun went down, thirty-five thousand of them were dead, and fewer than twenty thousand of ours. In that same year Riniy fell into pieces behind the queen, as a house falls when its roof beam rots. Two summers later we beat [[per:38184|Nguwithnithkun]] before [[set:1|Nini]].

In the fourteenth summer the peace was sworn. Linunushal gave up Zhinontunu and Uthtafpu and the land of Tuvizhinge, and paid silver besides, and swore a truce of fifteen years. My lord gave me a field by the river for my service. I have ploughed it ever since.

Now I am old. Linunushal has broken apart, as Riniy broke before it, and its queen is dead. I prayed for her soul as the [[mov:58|Vishithmikh]] teaches, because the teaching says that the enemy is also bound for the judgement. The noble Tutafbafin still lives, may the One keep him. In the new war at Vuvu he showed again that his hand had not grown weak.

My sons, the One gave Yidhe that land, but the price was paid in men, and I think of them more than of the land. When you pass the stones at Zhinontunu, say a prayer for those under them, theirs and ours. Mercy is the last duty of a soldier, and the most difficult.

Written at Tallin, in the year 4908 from the founding of Tallin.
