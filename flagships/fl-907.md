In AY 1517 a king of twenty sat in a herdsman's hut in the hills and wrote a letter on his knee. The clay was poor, and the signs are uneven. A note on the edge explains why: it was written "by the king's own hand, because the scribe ran." [[per:10857|Lokholon]], called the Last, had been king of [[pol:310|Pome]] since he was four. He had slipped out of his capital, [[set:17|Himi]], at night, "by the little gate with twelve men and a donkey." Now he wrote to the priests who had stayed behind (PAL·LT·01781):

> The great house is broken. The pot that our fathers made is broken into six pieces, and every piece calls itself a pot. In the house of my fathers in Peme sits the lord of Devegobevenga.

His governors, he says, "kept the roads, but for themselves. They sent the tribute, but to themselves." Then he adds a line that every ruler in this chapter might have written: "It did not begin with me. Let it be written that it did not begin with me." He names the battles of his grandfathers' time at Diviminnge and Nuwkennga, and the two defeats at Ontiedzey, where his own mother the queen [[per:10794|Norshedhefath]] "went out with a great host, and she came back with a small one." The record agrees with him. It lists those four defeats among the causes of the [[evt:10790|Collapse of the Pome State]]. Pome had won both of the wars in which they were fought. The previous chapter ended with that bill unpaid, and in 1517 it came due.

The fugitive lived another fifty years and never ruled again. His letter sets out the main lesson of the period from AY 1500 to 1750. Victory was the most expensive thing a state could buy, and a remarkable number of states bought it at a ruinous price. The whole population under states barely moved: 64.6 million in 1500, 63.7 million in 1620, 64.0 million in 1740. The map moved a great deal. Thirty-five states in 1500 became fifty by 1610, the most crowded political map the world had yet known, stood at forty-seven in 1620, and then fell back to thirty-six. In 1620 the largest state by territory, [[pol:414|Tagath]], held only 8 per cent of the land.

## Wars that ruined both sides

The first decades of the century show the pattern twice. In 1501 the kingdom of [[pol:254|Nambe]] attacked [[pol:377|Kabave]] for the land of [[reg:21|Daadüdhih]]. The [[evt:10660|Nambe–Kabave War]] lasted nine years and ended where it began, with a treaty that restored the frontier. Kabave, the defender, lost 14,013 men to Nambe's 8,036. Half a century later the record still counts the defeats at Alunzhi in 1505 and 1506 and at Dambali in 1508 among the causes of Kabave's fall. The empire reached its greatest extent, 6.0 million people, around 1550, under the queen [[per:10837|Navavun]]. In 1560, under [[per:11400|Natange]] the Last, came the [[evt:11268|Collapse of the Kabave State]]. One of its six heirs, seated at [[set:12|Kabaf]], took the name [[pol:506|Puvu]], the name of the empire from whose ruins Kabave itself had risen in 1307. It lasted until 1842. Another heir, the kingdom of [[pol:507|Nilu]], lasted seven years.

The [[evt:10785|Second Ana–Man'e War]] (1516–1525) was worse. [[pol:349|Ana]] attacked the [[pol:312|Man'e]] empire in [[reg:17|Nis]] to force it to pay tribute. It won four of the seven battles and lost the other three, all at Listlitl, and the peace again changed nothing. Ana lost 18,080 men, Man'e 5,541. The victory stele that the Man'e king Inacu set up for Listlitl in 1523 (PAL·IN·00747) claims 33,800 enemy dead. That is almost twice what Ana lost in the whole war; the record gives 6,534 for the battle. Yet the war broke both sides. The [[evt:10943|Collapse of the Man'e State]] came first, in 1534. Its court left a farewell in clay, *The Last Decree of the Great House of Mane* (PAL·DE·00958), its seal struck through with a single line: "Barley was bought for silver, measure by measure, at a price the fathers never heard of." Five kingdoms, among them [[pol:485|Mane]] and [[pol:486|Nimit]], took its place. Ana followed six years later. Its queen [[per:10891|Ma'sha Ash]] the Last had been put on the throne at two and had reigned for thirty-five years. In 1540 the [[evt:11014|Collapse of the Ana State]] divided the empire into six. One heir, the kingdom of [[pol:492|Tomözel]], lasted until 1892. Another, the kingdom of [[pol:491|Mii]], revived the name of one of the great empires of the ninth century.

Lesser fractures ran through the same years. Two claimants took Inikhi out of Navnga, in 1506 and in 1509, and the [[evt:10744|Secession of Kayagu]] split Luthe in 1510. Nambe had its own breakaway in Nzhinduvi in 1522, Kivuna in Unushti in 1541, and Yif in Lamlivji in 1545. The record follows several of these splits to the end of the state: Navnga broke apart in 1526, Nambe in 1542 and Kivuna in 1579.

## Tantethi's long noon

One empire seemed to be immune. [[pol:291|Tantethi]], whose capital [[set:152|Tanshedi]] had fed itself so cheaply in the previous age, entered the century under [[per:10720|Ish'o Lo]] with 7.0 million people and grew for eighty years. Its politics, though, were violent. The queen [[per:11070|Yalni Ta'e]] reigned from 1529 to 1549. Then three usurpers took the throne in five years: Mi'epayal in 1549, who fell in battle the next year, Ki'i'igi in 1551, and [[per:11387|Minimin I]] in 1554.

The usurpers won their wars, and the stone inflates the wins as usual. In the [[evt:11080|Tantethi–Kvile War]] (1546–1551) the [[pol:449|Kvile]] empire gave up twenty-three districts in [[reg:1|Yizamashti]] and Baan, including the city of Ashash, and paid about 38 talents of silver a year for thirty-five years. Ki'i'igi's stele (PAL·IN·00773) claims 9,600 dead at Ashash, where the record gives 1,989. In the [[evt:11240|Fifth Yizamashti Trade War]] (1557–1563), the [[pol:410|Pibetie]] empire attacked to win trade. After losing an evenly matched first battle, Tantethi met it with armies of 24,000 to 32,000 men against about 10,000. Tantethi won four of the five battles. Minimin's stele (PAL·IN·00780) claims 7,600 enemy dead at Shithiy in 1563. The record gives 3,001, and Tantethi lost 3,603 there. Over the whole war Tantethi lost 15,551 men against Pibetie's 15,203. The prize was a clause letting Tantethi's merchants pass through Pibetie free of tolls. Even that survives only as a fragment, quoted 169 years later in a Pibetie chronicle (PAL·TR·00744).

By 1580 Tantethi held 9.7 million people and 408 districts, the largest state of the age. Thirteen years later it was gone. In 1593, under the usurper's son, Minimin II the Last, came the [[evt:11672|Collapse of the Tantethi State]]. The record gives its causes as the three usurpations, a revolt of 1563, and the one battle of the trade war that Pibetie won, at Shidyun in 1558. Its heirs included the kingdom of [[pol:525|Tanchethi]], the Va'mi empire and the [[pol:526|Los]] empire, which would last until 2120. The loser of 1563 survived the winner. Pibetie, under [[per:11295|Lienpe'sa]] the Old (1548–1593) and then [[per:11816|Milamamshe]], lasted until 1802. It peaked in 1740 at 8.3 million people, ruled from [[set:91|Pivithie]]. In 1565 the war chariot appeared independently in Luthe, credited to Napyivli, eighty years after its independent appearance in Tantethi.

## The hungry roads

The age was slightly cool, about 0.08 °C below the long-run mean, and it was much hungrier than the last. In 1541 [[evt:11004|The Red Plague]] broke out at [[set:0|Taulin]], the Yeda capital, and spread to twelve more cities, among them Rile, Himi and Nilu. It killed about 863,261 people, one in five of those it reached. Plague returned with the Weeping Cough of 1633 (524,927 dead) and the River Death of 1644, which began at Ajü and killed 378,877, more than a quarter of those it reached.

Drought did more lasting damage. The [[evt:11394|Onset of the Great Drought in Baan (AY 1574)]] began eighty-eight dry years over 216 districts, and the record ties the famine of 1599 in Dyimbi (229,293 dead) to it. The worst single blow was the [[evt:11772|Onset of the Great Drought in Yizamashti (AY 1608)]], which lay over 417 districts. Famine struck Iith in 1608, where about 567,137 people died, one in seven. It struck Yimla in 1609, killing 111,926. The [[evt:11777|Famine of AY 1608 in Mbambanlule]] killed fewer, 11,653, but they were 23 per cent of the people there, the highest share of any famine of the age. The price of grain shows the scale. At Tanshedi a measure had cost about 11.5 grams of silver in the 1590s. It cost 39 in the 1600s and stayed above 25 for three decades.

From Yimla the hungry walked. The record counts 309,122 Ya'cuam who moved into Yizamashti between 1609 and 1625. Their own memory of it survives in *The Walking Song of the Yakkamu* (PAL·PO·00073, AY 1626), excavated at Lininadhe:

> In Yimola the barley stood up green,
> in Yimola the barley lay down brown. [...]
> of every eight that ate at the hearth, one ate no more.

Its arithmetic is exact. One in eight is the record's thirteen per cent. "Three hundred thousand we were, and more," the singers say, and that is right too. They also say something the record cannot: "We said: we are not spears, we are mouths."

The middle of the century brought a second wave. The famine of 1652 in Yidhe killed 534,523 people. Grain at Taulin cost 24.7 grams in the 1650s, and at Rile and Nodhinge it passed 60, the highest prices of the age. In Khanji, a grandmother's memories, written down by her grandchild in 1660 (PAL·CH·08474), join the River Death to the arrival of hungry strangers from Ng'jinaguh: "Their speech was not our speech. Their bread was not our bread. But their hunger was our hunger." Across some fifty markets the median measure of grain rose from about 12 grams in the sixteenth century to between 13 and 14 for much of the seventeenth. The silver standards themselves were never debased. It was hunger, not the coin, that drove the price up.

## Paying for victory: the wars of Yeda

The new [[pol:439|Yeda]] at Taulin, heir of the empire that broke in 1442, shows most clearly what victory and defeat cost. Its first great test came under Nelan, who succeeded the queen [[per:11102|Olotafbafen]] (1531–1572). In the [[evt:11452|Devegobevenga–Yeda War]] (1574–1583), Pome's heir [[pol:475|Devegobevenga]] invaded [[reg:16|Yidhe]] and was held to a stalemate. Its kings carved their victories very large. Kolboman I's stele for Virathkhanu (PAL·IN·00797) claims 25,400 Yeda dead, more than Yeda lost in the whole war (13,913). The record gives 4,164 for that battle. His successor [[per:11674|Kolboman II]] claimed 11,300 at Nilu (PAL·IN·00804); the record gives 1,451.

In the [[evt:12303|First Yeda–Nodhenga War]] (1642–1651), Yeda attacked [[pol:441|Nodhenga]] for land in [[reg:9|Tuvizhinge]] and this time won. Nodhenga ceded sixteen districts and a town, opened its trade, and paid some 35 talents of silver a year for forty years. The Nodhenga court chronicle (PAL·CH·02236) is a model of the defeated scribe's art. For the opening battle at Nunuunge in 1644 it reports 14,208 Yeda dead and 105 of its own. The record gives 5,922 and 305, so Nodhenga won the battle and the scribe still multiplied its size. It explains the later defeats by treachery, allies who failed to come and a rising river. It ends with the treaty: "Some villages of no account were given up, which had always been poor."

The greatest war of the age was the [[evt:12697|Yeda–Devegobevenga War]] (1675–1682). Lokhramadhen the Brave, king of Yeda since 1670, went to war to make Devegobevenga pay tribute. Against him stood [[per:12503|Vanavshefen]], who had ruled Devegobevenga since 1651 and commanded in person. Seven battles were fought around Rile, Shivange and Virathkhanu, and Yeda won three of them. Lokhramadhen raised a stele for one of these, Shivange in 1677 (PAL·IN·00897), which claims 5,300 enemy dead. The record gives 948 and names his general Tonon, not the king, as the commander there. At Virathkhanu in 1679, Yeda lost 7,644 of 17,580 men. At Rile in 1682 it lost again, and Lokhramadhen was killed, aged thirty-one. Yeda's dead numbered 28,629 against Devegobevenga's 8,348. The Treaty of Rile (PAL·TR·00854) turned the war's purpose upside down. Yeda, which had gone to war to collect tribute, would now pay it: 98 talents of silver every year for thirty-six years. Devegobevenga's merchants also gained the right to cross Yeda free of tolls.

The debt fell to a ten-year-old girl. [[per:12804|Jelieveyath]] the Great came to the throne in 1683 and reigned for fifty-six years. She did not restore Yeda's size. In 1740 it held 6.5 million people in only 155 districts, a compact and densely peopled state. She did keep it whole. Devegobevenga, under [[per:12873|Totovan]] (1689–1733), was at its largest in 1740 and was gone by 1748.

The kingdom of [[pol:370|Yennga]] lived through the same contradiction. Under [[per:12386|Tamon]] the Victorious (1645–1699), whose life has its own study in this archive, it won the [[evt:12471|First Yennga–Mikhuthitsu War]] and nine districts of [[reg:10|Mikhukhizhi]] from [[pol:494|Mikhuthitsu]]. It lost 19,159 men to the enemy's 5,841. By 1720 Yennga held 8.1 million people and 323 districts, its greatest size. A year later came the [[evt:13135|Collapse of the Yennga State]]. The record names the defeats of Tamon's war at Kheye and Nivanga, and the two Nebanga rebellions, among its causes. A new [[pol:580|Yennga]] empire at Yinnge took up the name. Beside it stood the kingdom of [[pol:581|Nivanga]], seated in the old rebel capital.

## New faiths, a new language and the new signs

Faith moved mostly by conversion at court. In the year Pome fell, [[per:11013|Esiesh Ash]] founded the [[mov:44|Esue'otl]] cult at Ashash. [[pol:465|Tonnga]] adopted [[mov:5|Lelmi]] in 1524. The [[evt:11001|Conversion of Luthe to Uthungala]] followed in 1535, and in 1536 Navnga adopted [[mov:30|Lovanu]]. The [[evt:11157|Conversion of Litlatlchi to La'im]] came in 1543, and in 1549 [[pol:489|Kivuna]] adopted [[mov:13|Onsatlmom]]. New founders appeared as well. The [[evt:11511|Founding of the Badhumpimu Cult]] at Divnüzith in 1577 was followed by the Yèninambi cult at Nilu in 1685. By 1620 [[mov:0|La'im]] held 17 per cent of the land and Jinru 13, with Kieve and Ya'cuam 8 each. Trade carried on through all of this. Fifty-five merchant houses were founded, among them the house of Kuvfi at Dagafu (1546–1679), which traded tin, iron, timber, horses and grain.

Speech kept changing. The record dates sixty-three sound changes to these years, among them palatalization in Tasemem around 1510 and umlaut in Telnu around 1541. It also marks four new languages: Nukngu by 1599, and Mioim, Nekhko and Khubunji by 1699. The last grew out of Khovave, and it is the language in which this book is written.

The most consequential change came from a storehouse. In 1725 Nangashu, scribe of the storehouse to King Nanjenvi of Khanji, wrote to his king (PAL·LT·01707). Sailors from the far coast, he reported, scratched marks on their jars: "They did not write words. They wrote the sounds of words, one sign for one sound." He counted the sounds of his own speech and found them "fewer than the fingers of three men." He made a sign for each and gave a tablet to a porter's boy who could barely read the old signs. "In the morning, my lord, a boy read it to me." He foresaw the objection of the priests of Khupande and answered it in advance: "Let the priests keep them for the gods. … Let the new signs be for the storehouse and the market and the king's messengers." Twenty-three years later, in 1748, the Khukunji of the same kingdom made the Mbingi script, few letters and one sound each, cut from the old Khave signs. By way of the Ladhu and Luzhe scripts, the letters of this book descend from it.

Our sources are still mostly royal. Of the 883 documents that survive from these years, 369 are inscriptions, 218 chronicles and 108 treaties. Only 26 are letters and 24 are school exercises. Voices like the fugitive king's, the walking people's and the storehouse scribe's are rare, and survival favours stone over clay that was never fired. The wars of this chapter are therefore better documented than its famines, though the famines killed far more people.

The age ended as it had begun, with drought. The [[evt:13311|Onset of the Great Drought in Daadüdhih (AY 1740)]] spread across 695 districts, the widest dry spell yet recorded. It brought famine to Baan in 1745, with 177,582 dead. In 1740 Tagath, under [[per:13014|Namumbiv]] the Old, stood at its height: 7.3 million people in 344 districts. The next chapter begins with a schoolboy learning the new letters at Adhgumgi in 1750, and with Tagath's fall eleven years later.
