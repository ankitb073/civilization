title: Not Worth the Kiln
---
The clay came from the canal bank outside the water gate, and since Luyulule nobody went out by the water gate, so Navavun worked from what she had left: four vats of it, settled and skimmed until it was fine as fat. She trod it barefoot in the court of the house of tablets with her skirt knotted at the knee. When it was soft she knelt and worked it with the heels of her hands, folding it over on itself to drive the air out, and slapped it into pillows the size of a palm for the scribes.

They wanted a great many. They were counting.

For nine days two scribes had sat at the east gate of [[set:84|Kazhe]] with the muster on their knees. When a man came in off the road they asked his name, his father's name and his ward, found him on the muster, and pressed his name again into a fresh tablet of the men come home. In the house of tablets the scribes laid the two lists side by side, and whoever was on the first and not on the second they wrote onto a third. Five thousand two hundred and sixty-four men had gone out to Luyulule behind the general Najime. On the tenth day the scribes stopped waiting and finished the third list, and it held one thousand six hundred and fifteen.

Navavun did not read. But on the morning the levy was mustered she had stood behind the scribe at the gate until he showed her, with the end of his reed, where her son was: three signs, one like a bird's foot, one like a door with a bar across it, one like two reeds leaning together. Nagizhi. Lion. His father had named him that when he was a red, furious thing no bigger than a loaf, and had not lived to see him grow into a long, mild boy who apologised to doorposts when he walked into them.

Najime, scribe of the palace, brought the third list out into the court in a reed tray and set it down in the sun to harden. He was a man of thirty-two with clay under his fingernails, like hers.

"Show me," she said.

He did not ask what. He crouched beside the tray and began at the top of the first tablet, moving his finger down the rows, and she watched his finger and not the signs, because she did not want to be the one who found it. He went down the first tablet and the second. On the third, near the bottom, his finger stopped, and there they were: the bird's foot, the barred door, the leaning reeds.

She sat back on her heels. The court smelled of wet clay and of old dung ash under the kiln, which no one had lit in days. After a while she said, "The general came home."

"He did." Najime wiped his finger on his hip. "He has my name. People keep telling me they're sorry, as if I'd lent it to him."

"Peace," she said. That was what the name meant.

"Peace," he agreed, and went back inside.

There were not so many names in the world, she thought. In this country the kings and the scribes and the dead all wore the same few, and passed them back and forth like bowls.

On the shelf above the door of the cool room, where the light did not reach, stood the tablets that had been fired: the words of kings, which were meant to last. A tally was for a season. When the season was over you soaked it and made the clay into something else, and that was right, because clay was dug with effort and carried in baskets and settled for days, and nobody wasted it on what was finished.

Three years ago the king had walked up and down this court dictating a letter to Nagubu of [[pol:246|Kezivi]], while Najime pressed it into clay that Navavun held ready. That king was dead now, in his bed this year at thirty-six, and his son was seven. [[pol:500|Khishkhizi]] itself was six. She remembered the king stopping by the vats to say the last line twice, to hear how it sounded: "If you will not answer, let the sword answer." Two tablets had been written. One went to Kezivi. One went into the kiln and came out the colour of a cooking pot and was set on the shelf above the door.

* * *

The men of Kezivi came in on the twelfth day. Afterwards everyone in Kazhe knew which gate they had come in by, and it was always a gate on the far side of the city from where the speaker lived.

They were fewer than she had imagined. She had imagined a flood, and they were men, tired, dusty to the eyebrows, with bronze at their belts and sores on their feet, and they knew which doors to kick. They spoke as she spoke, the same words with the vowels laid down a little differently, and when one of them stubbed his toe on the kiln step he called on [[dei:107|Nizhinande]] exactly as her husband used to.

She was behind the vats when four of them came into the court. They looked at the vats and laughed, because everybody knows that when a city is about to fall people sink their silver in the clay, and they went into it to the shoulder, one after another, groping, swearing at the cold. Then they tipped the vats over to be sure. The clay slumped out across the court in grey tongues and lay there taking straw and grit and the prints of their feet, and the sun began at once to skin it.

They took the bronze lamp. They took the scribes' bronze knives, the little ones for trimming the edges of tablets. They took the wet cloths she kept the clay under, because cloth is cloth. Then a thin man with a pouch at his belt came in after them and went into the cool room with a torch, and Navavun, who could not afterwards have said why, got up and went to the door to watch.

He was a scribe. She could see it in the way he held the tablets, by their edges, tilting them to the light. He went along the shelves putting the tallies back where he found them. On the shelf above the door he found the fired ones, and took one down, and read it out to the soldiers in the flat voice of a man who reads for a living, and she knew it by its shape before he had said ten words.

"With me all goes well," he read, and the soldiers laughed.

He read on, to the end. "If you will not answer, let the sword answer."

Nobody laughed at that. The scribe turned the tablet over, as if there might be more on the back, and then set it on its shelf again, square to the edge.

"We have the other one," he said. "At home."

One of the soldiers had taken the torch from him and was holding it under the reed baskets on the lowest shelf, looking at him, waiting.

"It's clay," the scribe said. "Fire bakes clay. You'd be keeping it for them." He took the torch back, not roughly. "Besides. It's [[dei:104|Shilimu]]'s house. She's ours too."

They went out past Navavun as if she were a doorpost.

* * *

The swearing was to be in the great hall, and for a swearing there must be a tablet, and for a tablet there must be clay.

The clay in the court had dried into a crust full of footprints. The canal bank was a camp of the men of Kezivi. Navavun said this to Najime, and Najime looked at her for a long moment, and then went into the cool room and came back with the reed tray.

"These," he said.

She looked at the third list, dry now, pale as bread.

"The count is made," Najime said. "The king's mother has been told it. The priests have been told it. Nobody is going to want to hear it twice."

"And when somebody does?"

"Then I'll tell them. One thousand six hundred and fifteen." He touched his forehead. "That will keep."

She carried the tray to the soaking trough. There was nothing to argue about. This was what tallies were for, and everyone knew it, and the thin man from Kezivi had known it too, standing in the cool room with the torch in his hand. Fire would have kept them. He had kept the house from burning, and so the house would keep nothing.

Before she lowered the third tablet she found the signs again, without anyone's finger to show her. Dry clay breaks clean if you know where to press. She set her thumbs on either side of the bird's foot, the barred door and the leaning reeds, and bent, and the corner came away with a small sound: a piece no bigger than the top joint of her thumb, with a sliver of some other woman's son along its broken edge. She put it in her sash.

Then she put the rest in the water.

Unfired clay goes soft from the outside in. By evening the signs were blurred, as if seen through tears, though she was not crying; by morning they were gone. She drained the trough and trod what was in it, barefoot, with her skirt knotted at the knee, and the men of Luyulule went under her heels and folded and came up and folded again, and the air went out of them, and they were good clay. It was the best she had made in a year. She was ashamed of how good it was.

* * *

She carried it to the hall under a cloth borrowed from the kitchens and waited at the scribes' bench at the back, where no one looked.

The king sat on his father's chair with a cushion behind him so that his feet nearly reached its edge. His mother, Nagubu, stood at his shoulder with the king's seal on its cord around her neck, because she held it for him now. Across the floor stood Nakadhash, king of Kezivi, a broad man of thirty-three whose own father was only a year in the ground. The two kings who had quarrelled by letter were both dead. It was their sons who stood in the hall.

The priests of the [[mov:16|Ninzhe]] stood on both sides of the floor, and they were the same priests, near enough, calling on the same gods, on Shilimu, on [[dei:105|Khudhaze]] of the sky, on [[dei:106|Kumbe]] of the moon, to come and witness.

Then a herald read the terms. The city of Luyügizi with its fields. Then the districts, one by one, by name, and Navavun, who could count, counted them on the knuckles of her hands and then again, and came to forty-six. A tribute, every year, for thirty-one years. Ten years in which no man of the one land would take up arms against the other.

"Kezivi asks for peace," said Nakadhash.

Nobody moved. It was the form. The one who has won asks and the one who has lost grants, and that way both can go home and tell it. Beside her on the bench Navavun saw Najime set the point of his reed to a scrap of clay and lift it again, as if he had not yet decided what he was writing down.

The king's mother bent and spoke into her son's ear. The boy looked up at her, and then out at the hall.

"[[pol:500|Khishkhizi]] grants it," said [[per:11308|Nakhize]], in a clear high voice that carried to the back, and then, less clearly, "Is that all?"

It was not all. The scribes took the clay from under her cloth and smoothed it and wrote, while the hall waited, and the writing took a long time. When it was done the king's mother lifted the seal from around her neck and pressed it into the foot of the tablet, and lifted it, and pressed it again.

Then the boy climbed down from his father's chair, though nobody had said he could, and came across to the bench where Navavun stood holding what was left, a lump the size of a fist. He looked at it. She held it out, and he put one finger into it, slowly, the way children do, to see how far it would go.

"It's cold," said [[per:11308|Nakhize]], king of Khishkhizi.

"It's from the canal," she told him, and his mother called him back.

The tablet went to the kiln that evening with a guard to sit beside it. Treaties are fired.

* * *

At home, by the moon in the doorway, she took the corner out of her sash and pressed it into the lump, beside the small round hollow the king had left, until the clay closed over its broken edge and held it. Then she set her own thumb into the soft clay underneath, once, and lifted it, the way the king's mother had lifted the seal.

She had no kiln and no right to one. But sun-dried clay keeps for a lifetime, if it is kept out of the wet. She set it on the sill where the morning would find it, and she meant to keep it out of the wet.
