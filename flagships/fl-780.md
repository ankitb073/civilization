**11 Nurnin 2211 of the Vishithmikh.** Fair, wind from the hills, the first true warmth of the year. Brought the ewes down to the lower fold at noon. Counted one hundred and twelve, with nineteen lambs living and two dead at birth, which is a good lambing by any measure and a miracle by the measure of the last fifteen years.

I set down here, while the evening holds, a reckoning I have owed myself since the sun came back. My father kept this book before me, and I have his entries for the first of the ash years, written in a hand that grows smaller every winter, as if the cold had got into the ink.

In that first year he writes that the sky "turned the colour of dishwater in the month of the shearing, and stayed so." He writes that the snow which fell that winter was grey, and that the ewes would not lick it. He writes that the almanac-seller at the fair said a mountain had burst, and that he, my father, did not believe a mountain could darken the whole sky. He was wrong, as it turns out; the hand-copied sheets that came to the village afterward called it the [[evt:42770|Eruption of Mount Bavmumungra]], and named the cold that followed the [[cli:228|Volcanic Winter of Mount Bavmumungra]]. But I do not blame him. I did not believe it either, at first.

My own tally, since I took the book: in the third year we had sixty ewes. In the sixth year, forty-one. In the ninth year we killed the ram to get through the hungry month before the grass, and borrowed a ram from the widow on the ridge on shares, which debt I paid off only last autumn. Summers that were not summers. Hay that rotted standing. Lambs born into sleet in what should have been the season of blossom.

Now a matter of argument. Our curate held, and preached it from the pulpit through all those years, that the ash was a judgement, that the one Power had drawn a veil over our sins. The teachers of the [[mov:58|Vishithmikh]] do say that time runs to a final judgement. But they also say that the good life is the life of compassion and of release from suffering, and I put it to him at the harvest supper that a judgement which falls on ewes and lambs, who have no sins, is an odd sort of justice. He answered that the ewes suffered for us. I answered that then I would rather they had not, and that a mountain is a likelier cause than my neighbour's adultery. The table laughed. The curate did not.

Wool fetches a fair price again. Grain at the market town is about two [[cur:956|Tuthshi]] the measure, which my wife says is the first year she has not had to sit down when she heard it.

News from [[set:147|Binge]] comes slowly up the valley, but it comes. The young emperor, [[per:42565|Nithkuvbin]], is said to be a boy still; it was in his name that the armies of [[pol:1710|Binge]] carried out the [[evt:43061|Sack of Tungaruikhrimu]] five years ago and made the [[evt:43062|Treaty of Tungaruikhrimu]] after. My cousin's son marched in that war and came home with a limp and a brass buckle he will not tell us how he got. Out here we noticed chiefly that the levy took our young men in the worst lambing season of all.

**Same day, evening.** The sky at sunset was clean and red, the old red, the red my father remembered and I had half forgotten. My daughter, who is eleven and was born in the ash, asked whether this is what the sky is supposed to look like. I told her yes. I hope I told her true.
