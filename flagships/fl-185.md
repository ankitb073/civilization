*A PANEGYRIC upon His Majesty NANGANDUMBE, King of Dagafu, composed upon the news of the victory at Dyizhih, and written fair at Dagafu the 24 Khumbe 4506 of the Uthungala.*

It is the custom of panegyrists to begin with the ancestors of their prince, and to show how the virtues of the grandfather flowered in the grandson. I shall not follow it. My prince needs no ancestors. Others inherit greatness as they inherit a coat, and wear it ill; [[per:43607|Nangandumbe]] cut his own from the cloth, and it fits him.

I know what the pedants of the academies say, in corners, behind their hands: that His Majesty came to the throne of [[pol:1670|Dagafu]] not by birth but by his own hand, four-and-twenty years ago, in a time of confusion. Let us reason with them, since reason is the lamp that our faith places in every breast. When a ship is driving on the rocks and the helmsman has lost his wits, does any sailor ask whether the man who seizes the tiller was born to it? He asks only whether the ship is saved. Dagafu was that ship. Look at it now.

Consider the record, and let the envious answer it if they can.

In the seventh year of his reign, rebels rose against the realm; he put them down, and joined their lands again to the crown. In the eighth, he carried a holy war beyond the frontier, and came home with provinces. In the fifteenth, an enemy invaded us and was so broken that he begged to be our vassal. These would be glory enough for any other prince. For ours they were the exercises of his youth.

Then came the [[evt:44298|secession of Nilu]], when that ancient city, forgetting what it owed to the crown, set up a throne of its own and called itself an empire. What followed was the [[evt:44299|civil war]], the bitterest of the four that have afflicted this kingdom. I will not pretend that it was cheap; the dead of Dagafu at [[evt:44313|Khayagu]] and at Khambe are a debt that every citizen owes. But at [[evt:44328|Khambe]] the rebellion was broken, and at Khambe the claimant came to His Majesty's tent and set his name beside his king's; and by [[evt:44329|that settlement]] Nilu and all its lands returned to the crown. His Majesty did not hang the claimant. He pardoned him. Let the philosophers who praise moderation in their lecture rooms come and see it practised in a king.

And while the house was still in disorder, the thieves came to the door. The Khishkhis, thinking our kingdom exhausted by its civil war, began [[evt:44316|their war upon us]], and came down upon our frontier with an army of more than a hundred thousand men. They met ours at [[evt:44332|Dambüzhi]], and their general did not go home. They came again this year with a second army, and at [[evt:44334|Dyizhih]] they met ours a second time, and again their general lies among their dead. Two armies, two generals; I leave the arithmetic to the Khishkhis.

Our faith teaches that prosperity is a sign of the favour of the One. Then look about you. The granaries are full; grain is cheap in the markets of [[set:9|Dagafu]]. The [[cur:923|ngathe]] is heavy and honest silver; the merchants of three kingdoms ask to be paid in it. The roads are safe. The courts are open. Is this the work of a usurper? Then let the One send us more usurpers.

And yet I would not have it said that I flatter. The [[mov:24|Uthungala]] teaches also that time runs in a line toward a judgement, and that every prince shall stand there and give his account. I do not know what account my prince will give. I know only that, if the One weighs justice and moderation and the welfare of the people, as our teachers say, then the scale on which Nangandumbe stands will not be light.

The war is not yet ended; the Khishkhis are stubborn, and we must be patient. But let them learn what we already know: that Dagafu, under this king, does not break.

Long live the King.
