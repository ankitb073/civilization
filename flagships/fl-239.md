title: Unmade Along Its Seams: Conquest and the Fall of Tallin

The documentary consensus lists five causes for the [[evt:4077|Collapse of the Tallin State]] in AY 825. All five fall between 763 and 770: four lost battles in the war against Navnga and [[evt:3665|The Marsh Pestilence]]. Taken at face value, the list implies that [[pol:61|Tallin]] died of wounds received more than half a century before. I shall argue that the wounds were real but that they began an erosion rather than ending a life, and that the empire finally came apart along the seams of its own conquests.

## The war that was not lost

Tallin attacked Navnga in 761, aiming at conquest, and the war ended in 770 in stalemate. Tallin won three of seven battles and lost fewer men overall, 10,861 against Navnga's 16,362. Its four defeats share a pattern: three times it attacked a much larger army. At the [[evt:3691|Battle of Rile (AY 763)]] it brought 9,643 men against 20,119 and lost 2,538 against 1,351. At the [[evt:3709|Battle of Shivange (AY 765)]] it brought 6,023 against 14,377 (losses 2,175 against 701), and at the [[evt:3760|Battle of Vuvu (AY 770)]] 10,221 against 22,408 (losses 2,332 against 1,748). Only the [[evt:3737|Battle of Rile (AY 769)]] was fought on roughly equal terms, and it too was lost, 1,117 against 461. These are the losses of an ambitious attacker, not of a state fighting for its life. Queen Shijilialath, who opened the war, commanded at the first Rile in person and died the next year.

The court remembered none of it. The Annals of Tallin, AY 700–799 record the victory at Shivange in Evenin's first year, "the army of the king prevailed", then that "a sickness came", then the peace. The four defeats do not appear. On the annalist's own selection, eight of the twenty-seven battles Tallin fought in those years are mentioned at all.

## The pestilence at home

The pestilence matters more than the battles, and for a reason the usual accounts pass over: the record traces it to Taulin, Tallin's own capital, in 765. The disease that reached thirty-two towns and killed about 1.48 million people across the stricken states began at the empire's centre. On the record's estimates Tallin's population fell from about 4.8 million in AY 750 to about 4.4 million by 770.

Then the empire recovered, and more than recovered. By 820 its population stood at about 6.8 million and its territory had grown by some two fifths. The treasury, which had fallen through the war years, had climbed back to within a few parts in a hundred of its level of 750. Nor did the throne fail. Evenin, Pimkultuth, Rayngen and Makhshishenmumath the Last succeeded one another without a single usurpation. Neither the economy nor the dynasty explains 825.

## Five successors, four conquests

The successor states do. Apart from the core, which kept Taulin and the name [[pol:171|Tallin]] until 1141, each formed in land Tallin had acquired by treaty or conquest. [[pol:172|Duegowinga]] arose at Nudhuruigirimu, taken from Buneynga by the Treaty of Bimnge in 545. [[pol:174|Noltennga]] arose at Nuwkennga, gained by the Treaty of Taalinnuy in 726. [[pol:173|Tlü'ti]] took Tuzhi, the newest acquisition, won by the Treaty of Taulin in 780. And the [[pol:175|Yinnga]] Empire rose at Yinnge, conquered in 689 from a kingdom of the same name, which thus returned after one hundred and thirty-six years.

The record's measure of internal order supplies the link. It fell in every decade but one between 740 and 820. The war and pestilence of the 760s began that fall: they discredited the court and emptied the treasury for a generation. The expansion that followed added new provinces faster than the centre could bind them. The 760s explain why the decline began. The conquests explain where the empire tore.

Climate, finally, offers little. The 740s and 770s were cold, but no famine in the record touches Tallin in its last century. Tallin was not starved, bankrupted or usurped. It was outgrown by its own acquisitions. The return of Yinnga under its old name suggests that the conquered had never stopped regarding themselves as conquered.
