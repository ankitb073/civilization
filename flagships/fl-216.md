title: A Kingdom Already on Its Knees: The Second Yi'otl–I'iti War and the Arithmetic of Humiliation

The [[evt:2942|Second Yi'otl–I'iti War]] (AY 664–669) is usually read as a straightforward Yi'otl conquest, and in outcome it was one. The timing matters more than that reading allows. Three years earlier, the [[evt:2926|Treaty of Bau (AY 661)]] had ended another war, against the Tantöti Empire, and left [[pol:87|I'iti]] a vassal of that empire, paying tribute and short of six districts. [[pol:30|Yi'otl]], which had already beaten I'iti once in the [[evt:2166|First Yi'otl–I'iti War]] a century before, attacked a kingdom already on its knees. I argue that this context explains the most extravagant figure in either chronicle, and that the figure should be read as a measure of humiliation, not of battle.

## The tablets

Both accounts were translated by Nazhigi, so differences of idiom between them belong to the scribes, not to the translator. The I'iti account (PAL·CH·00438, Saba) was excavated at Bau, the city that gave its name to the earlier treaty, in AY 4918. Losh'ashim'am wrote it "in the 5th year of Sha Tlu", AY 671, two years after the peace and within a decade of two lost wars. The Yi'otl account (PAL·CH·00437, Ie'im, excavated at Pivithie in AY 4913) is Ma'she Ibe's, dated AY 680, "in the 1st year of Esiesh Ada", the king later called the Conqueror. Both scribes were adults during the war.

## Field by field

*Who began it.* In the record, Yi'otl attacked, to seize territory. Losh'ashim'am agrees: "Ishwishitlwi'a of Yi'utl, swollen with pride, coveted the lands of I'iti." Ma'she Ibe reverses it: "Shim'am'igi of I'ithi broke the peace and sent raiders across the border of Yi'otl. So the king went out to punish him." That a vassal with a fresh tribute to pay sent raiders against a stronger neighbour is not impossible, but the record does not support it.

*The battles.* The record has three, all Yi'otl victories: the [[evt:2959|Battle of Yimla (AY 666)]], the [[evt:2971|Battle of Yimla (AY 668)]], both in [[reg:14|Yimla]], and the [[evt:2975|Battle of Bathshi (AY 669)]]. Both scribes have all three, correctly ordered, with the right victor. Losh'ashim'am supplies the excuses: "by treachery" at the first, "through the cowardice of hired men" at the second.

*Commanders.* The record names one: King Shim'am'igwi II of I'iti, killed at Yimla in AY 666. Both tablets keep him. Ma'she Ibe has "Shim'am'igi himself was killed, and his head was brought to the king." Losh'ashim'am has "There fell Shim'am'igwi, whose name shall be remembered." Here, as so often, a royal death within living memory is a fixed point that no scribe moves.

*The dead.* The record gives Yi'otl 4,552 dead and I'iti 873. Ma'she Ibe reports 3,414 I'iti dead (1,650, 1,228 and 536), almost four times the record in each battle. He enlarges the I'iti musters by about two and a half (3,078, 2,824 and 1,272, against 1,236, 1,134 and 511), and admits 2,430 dead of his own, about half the truth. Losh'ashim'am gives his own side 518 dead (251, 186 and 81, about three-fifths of the record) and Yi'otl 13,134 (2,952, 5,447 and 4,735), nearly three times the record's 1,023, 1,888 and 1,641. That total is close to the size of an entire Yi'otl army, which the record puts at fifteen to sixteen thousand in each battle.

*Outcome and land.* Yi'otl won. The [[evt:2976|Treaty of Bathshi (AY 669)]] ceded four districts and imposed an indemnity of 3,121 kg of silver and a ten-year truce. Ma'she Ibe has I'iti sending "envoys with gifts and pleas", and he counts nine districts "returned to Yi'otl". Losh'ashim'am has a king who, "wearied of bloodshed", agreed to terms, with no land lost.

## Reading the large number

Losh'ashim'am's 13,134 is not a careless figure. Each of his three entries multiplies the record by the same factor of nearly three. He wrote for a court that had, within a decade, become a foreign empire's vassal, lost a king in battle and ceded land twice. Such a court could not claim victories, because everyone knew there were none. What it could claim was cost: an enemy that won every field and nearly bled to death doing it. Ma'she Ibe's distortions are milder and more conventional. A new king called the Conqueror needed a war that the enemy had begun, cheap victories and a generous cession.

The general lesson is that the size of a distortion measures the need behind it. A court in I'iti's position needed the largest number it could plausibly write.
