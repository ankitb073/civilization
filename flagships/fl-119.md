*Extracts from the private diary of a physician attached to a public infirmary in [[set:71|Lamlivje]], printed with the author's consent for the instruction of the profession and the public. The author's name is withheld at his request.*

**11 Vande.** Three admitted overnight to the east ward with the same picture: high fever, a dry cough, and by the second day a red flush spreading from the throat down the chest, like a scald. The porters already call it the red ague. Two of the three are railway men off the night goods train. I have asked the matron to keep their bedding apart and to boil the linen, though the senior physician thinks this an old wives' precaution.

**12 Vande.** The wire from the Ministry this morning confirms what the newspapers have been printing for a week: the sickness first showed itself at [[set:288|Shudhunki]], and is following the railway. Private telegrams speak of cases at [[set:46|Kashunji]] and [[set:49|Adhgumgi]]. The evening paper adds rumours from [[pol:1951|Uva]] and [[pol:1638|Khishkhizi]], which I cannot confirm. Eleven admitted today. Four of yesterday's are dead. I wrote the figures in the ledger and then sat for some time without writing anything.

**13 Vande.** Rounds from six until two. Of the twenty-six cases now under my care, the flush has turned dusky in nine, and those nine, I fear, are lost; when the colour darkens the breathing fails within the day. The rest may pull through. If I reckon honestly, we are losing about one in three. I have not said this aloud in the ward.

A man of the seventh bed, a veteran of the [[evt:48260|first battle at Shudhunvidhi]], told me he had walked off a field with three hundred thousand dead upon it and never thought to die in a bed of a cough. He asked whether it was true that the [[evt:48267|Treaty of Shudhunvidhi]] had let the sickness in with the new trade. I told him the treaty was eight years old and the ague eight days, and that he should not believe what is shouted on street corners. He laughed, and then he coughed, and I had to leave him.

**15 Vande.** The Ministry has ordered the goods yards disinfected with carbolic and the third-class carriages held at the junction for inspection. The infirmary steward says the price of carbolic has doubled in a week; a bottle that cost two [[cur:1150|line]] on the first of the month now costs four. The druggists have been warned, but the warning is a piece of paper and the druggists are men. Thirty-eight admitted. We have put beds in the chapel.

**16 Vande.** The king, [[per:46912|Nalyilimvu]], has given a sum from the privy purse for blankets and coal for the infirmaries of the capital, and the papers print his message to the nation: that every citizen of [[pol:2009|Khanji]] should do his duty, keep his house in order, and not flee. I read it aloud in the ward. Some of the men saluted from their beds. Whatever is said in the coffee-houses, it is a good thing for a sick man to hear that his king has not forgotten him.

**17 Vande 2522 of the Vimekhulu.** I write this at three in the morning, in the porter's lodge, because my own room is given over to a nurse who has the flush. She is twenty years old. I have done for her what I know how to do, which is little: cool water, quiet, a window open to the night air.

The chaplain of the [[mov:70|Vimekhulu]] came through at midnight and said what his teachers say, that all things turn upon the wheel and what ends will begin again; that the One holds the sick and the well alike; and that our part is duty and right order, not fear. I am not a pious man, but I found it easier to go on with my rounds after he had spoken.

What is this [[evt:48330|Red Ague]]? Where does it go next, and how many will it take before it has done? I do not know. The ledger does not know. Tonight I counted fifty-one in the wards and wrote the number down, and that is all the science I have.
