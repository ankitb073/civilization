**CHAPTER THE FIRST: WHY WE WENT INTO THE STREETS**

I have been asked to set down what I remember of the [[evt:46797|Navnge Revolution]] before my memory improves it any further. I warn the reader that I was a printer's journeyman of twenty-two, that I saw a great deal from the top of a cart and very little from the council chamber, and that my opinions have not mellowed.

People say the revolution came from the philosophers. It came from the war bulletins.

In those years the telegraph office stood across the square from our shop, and every afternoon a clerk came out and pasted the despatches on the board. I remember the afternoon of the [[evt:46710|Battle of Navnge]], fought within a day's march of [[set:10|Navnge]] itself, when the despatch said "our forces retired in good order" and the whole street understood it to mean the opposite. I remember the next summer, the [[evt:46723|Battle of Piyikhzhidhe]], when the despatch did not trouble to say anything about good order. And I remember the day the [[evt:46742|Treaty of Navnge]] was posted on that same board: the frontier provinces given up, an indemnity laid on us that the newspapers worked out to so much a head for every family in the kingdom, and twenty years of truce to pay it in. The treaty was signed in our own capital, in the old king's own hall, with the enemy's officers drinking our wine.

Nobody I knew had been asked about the war or the peace. We were asked only to pay for both.

**CHAPTER THE SECOND: THE THREE DAYS**

I give only what I saw of the three days; the historians may quarrel over the rest.

On the first day the journeymen of four print-shops walked out at noon and set our type cases in the street as a barricade, which was foolish, because the cases broke, and for years afterwards children of that quarter found lead letters in the gutters. On the second day the soldiers of the city garrison were sent to clear the square and did not clear it; a sergeant whose name I never learned put his rifle down on the cobbles and sat on it. On the third day the old king received a deputation of the assembly, and that evening the proclamation was read from the palace steps: the king would rule by the constitution and with the consent of an elected chamber, and the crown would make no war, levy no tax and sign no treaty without that consent.

I stood near enough to see the old king's face. I have never decided whether it showed fear or relief.

**CHAPTER THE THIRD: WHAT CAME OF IT**

The old king kept his word, more or less, until he died. His son, [[per:45991|Dimintun]], has kept it rather better, which I did not expect, and I record it here in fairness.

But the chamber has not made Navnge a peaceful country. Five years ago the King himself led our army at the [[evt:46893|Battle of Yuftuy]], and the chamber voted the [[evt:46894|Treaty of Yuftuy]] by a large majority and the bells rang. A year after, our army won the [[evt:46908|First Battle of Tlilisyi]], and in the [[evt:46909|Eleventh Sack of Tlilisyi]] that followed, the archives of that city were burned. I read the despatch on the same board where I had read the treaty that shamed us. The people, once asked, will sometimes say yes to war. We did not make the revolution so that [[pol:1960|Navnge]] would never fight. We made it so that Navnge would fight only when it had been asked. Whether that difference is great or small I leave to the reader.

The young do not come to our anniversary dinners. Many of them went over to the movement that was founded the year after the three days; the [[evt:46911|Founding of the Inithnòthtunism Movement]] is to them what the barricades are to me. I do not understand their pamphlets; they do not understand mine.

The priests of the [[mov:58|Vishithmikh]] say that time runs in a line toward a judgment. If so, I should like the judgment to note that on the second day a sergeant sat on his rifle.

*Navnge, 19 Shinmum 2598 of the Vishithmikh.*
