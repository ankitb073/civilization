title: Night and Victory: Nakange of Puvu, Last of the Nabangèle

[[per:20014|Nakange]] of [[pol:826|Puvu]] bore a name meaning "night", and his court called him "the Victorious". He reigned for fifty years, from AY 2425 to 2475, and saw five wars through to a treaty, winning every one. He commanded in eight battles and lost only one. His victory steles, six of which survive, made sure no one would forget it. And yet his life ended in a way no stele could have predicted. He was the last of a house that had ruled Puvu for two centuries, and at his death the throne passed to a usurper. This chapter follows the victories and then asks what they could not secure.

## The queen's son

He was born in AY 2402, a Khufiènji of the house of Nabangèle, which had founded the Puvu state in 2271. His mother, [[per:19850|Navavun]], reigned for forty-four years and is remembered as "the Old". She was a commander in her own right. She led her armies at Dyizhih in 2404 and at Jyijinjivji in 2415, and raised steles for those victories (PAL·IN·01470, PAL·IN·01476). Nakange grew up in a court where the sovereign went to war in person and was expected to come home with a stone to set up.

The [[evt:20500|Accession of Nakange]] followed her death in 2425, when he was twenty-three. He inherited a realm of some 190 districts and nearly five million people, governed from Nilu. By the record's measure it was in reasonably good order. He also inherited a war. In 2423 his mother had begun the [[evt:20481|Holy War of Puvu against Luyulule]], against a kingdom only ten years old.

## The holy wars

The new king went at once to the field. At the [[evt:20508|Battle of Luyügih (AY 2425)]] he led 44,289 men against 2,408 under [[per:20357|Najime]] and won. His generals finished the war. Luyululi was sacked twice in two years, and both times its archives burned. The [[evt:20527|Conquest of Luyulule]] in 2427 annexed fourteen districts. It was signed by the conquered king, [[per:20208|Nadunge]] the Ill-Starred, and the record states that he was executed that year. Of [[pol:896|Luyulule]]'s own account of the war only one line survives, quoted centuries later (PAL·CH·03778). It is the defeated scribe's usual excuse: "because the omens had been neglected, our men could not hold the field." Nakange raised steles for both of his generals' battles (PAL·IN·01492, 01495), claiming 3,100 and 7,200 enemy dead. The record gives 680 and 1,028.

The [[evt:20572|Holy War of Puvu against Khanji]] (2434–2438) was more fully his own. He commanded three of its four battles. At the [[evt:20584|Battle of Shuthunile (AY 2435)]] he defeated the army of [[per:20148|Nambaf]], queen of [[pol:821|Khanji]]. At the [[evt:20617|Battle of Adhgumgi (AY 2438)]] her general [[per:20459|Naguf]] was killed. The bronze text of the [[evt:20618|Treaty of Adhgumgi (AY 2438)]] survives (PAL·TR·01392). Khanji gave up "10 districts in all" and the town of Shuthunnile. A second article set out the purpose of a holy war: "Throughout the land of Khanji the worship of Uthungala shall be permitted and its shrines protected; its ruler shall make offering at its altar." Just before the scribe's name, the tablet gives an instruction that is easy to pass over: "Let it be read aloud in the markets of both lands." The victors meant ordinary people to hear what had been decided for them. Khanji itself collapsed eleven years later.

## A young king killed

The Puvu–Tabüthif War (2448–2451) produced the reign's most human moment. At the [[evt:20725|Battle of Dadifi (AY 2449)]] Nakange, now forty-seven, met the young king of [[pol:829|Tabüthif]], [[per:20368|Natsunge V]], called "the Brave". The two sides lost almost equal numbers, but Natsunge, twenty-seven years old, died on the field. Nakange's rock relief there claims 9,900 enemy dead (PAL·IN·01519); the record has 3,120. The [[evt:20764|Treaty of Dabithifu (AY 2451)]] was sealed in the name of Natsunge's son, [[per:20576|Nashandith]], a boy of eleven. It ceded a single district, which Nakange marked with a boundary stone. The boy grew up. In 2469, as a man of twenty-nine, Nashandith attacked Puvu, and the record names Nakange's war of 2448 among the causes.

## The years of defence

By then the wars had turned defensive. In the [[evt:20829|Third Khishkhizi–Puvu War]] (2458–2464) King [[per:20474|Nabule II]] of [[pol:792|Khishkhizi]] invaded. At the [[evt:20844|Battle of Luyügih (AY 2459)]] Nakange suffered the one defeat of his career: with 64,613 men he lost to 19,109, and 15,263 of his soldiers died against 1,006 of the enemy's. He recovered and beat Nabule twice at Mungu, in 2460 and 2461, but the [[evt:20885|Treaty of Luyügih (AY 2464)]] brought nothing except a truce. Puvu had lost 40,659 men to defend what it already held. Nashandith's war that followed, the Tabüthif–Puvu War, was fought by generals. It ended with the [[evt:20970|Treaty of Dadunde (AY 2474)]], another bare truce. The old king still raised steles for its battles, claiming 31,600 dead at Khavazhu and 16,500 at Dadunde (PAL·IN·01527, 01529). The record gives the invaders' losses as 6,255 and 2,761.

## The house without an heir

The record lets us glimpse his family. He had three daughters, two born in 2419 and one in 2420. One of the elder pair died at nine. No son is recorded. His wife died in 2456. Puvu's law gave the throne by seniority within the royal house, and the record names no kinsman left to claim it.

The realm was also failing beneath the victories. Between the end of the 2440s and the end of the 2460s the treasury fell by nearly a quarter, and in the decade of his death the record's measure of order fell by about two-fifths. Nakange died of natural causes in AY 2475, aged seventy-three. In the same year the [[evt:20976|Usurpation of Nakathash I]], a crisis by the record's reckoning, ended the Nabangèle house after two hundred and four years. Under [[per:20816|Nakathash I]] the realm's order collapsed further. Puvu survived as a state until 2612, but its later kings faced revolt and secession.

"The Victorious" was true of the battlefield. Five of the six steles that carry Nakange's name commemorate battles that other men fought, and the sixth multiplies the enemy dead threefold. The treaties of his last decades bought only quiet. A king who commanded well, who had treaties read out in the markets, and who was conscientious in his way, could not give his house what it needed most: an heir. The night of his name came at the end, when Nakange died and a usurper took the throne of Puvu.
