To my brother, greeting, from the garrison at [[set:97|Kankush]], in the year 3106 from the founding of the city.

I am well, and so is my spear, which is more than I can say for my purse. You asked in your last letter why I send home less than I used to. I will tell you, and you may laugh, for it is the kind of joke only a soldier understands.

We are paid as we have always been paid: eight [[cur:664|Mbiv]] at each new moon, counted into the hand by the paymaster at the table under the gate. Eight is eight, says the paymaster, and in his arithmetic he is right. But since the [[evt:29724|Debasement of the Mbiv]], two years ago now, eight is not what eight was.

I did not believe it at first. The coin looks the same; the stamp is the same; it rings almost the same on the counter. But the money-changer in the lower market keeps an honest scale, as honest as a money-changer can, and he showed me. He put four of the old coins in one pan and five of the new in the other, and the old coins still sank the pan. Five of ours, brother, do not weigh what four of our fathers' did. Every soldier in the garrison has now stood at that scale, and every one has walked away quiet.

The grain-sellers learned it faster than we did. A measure of grain costs one and a third of the new coin, and the baker's loaves have grown thinner in proportion, as if the coin and the bread had agreed to fast together. The priests of [[mov:62|Nidunde]] tell us that prosperity is a sign of divine favour. If that is so, the gods have looked on this garrison and made a careful reckoning.

The philosophers who argue in the colonnade say a coin is a promise the city makes to the man who holds it. If so, the promise has become smaller, and nobody told the men who were promised. I do not say the king lied. [[per:29108|Natange]] was a child of three when the crown was set on him, and I do not suppose he weighs the coin himself. But someone weighed it, and took away a part, and that part went somewhere.

Where? In the barracks there are three opinions. The first is that the silver went to Luthe, to pay what was promised at the [[evt:29488|Treaty of Khankhush]], year upon year. The second is that it goes into the palace. The third, which is mine, is that it goes both ways, and that the soldier is the one place it does not go. I was a boy when the [[evt:29487|Third Sack of Khankhush]] blackened the lower town, and our uncle lay already among the dead of the [[evt:29477|Battle of Jyijinjivji]]. We paid for that war in sons first. Now we pay for it in silver, a little out of every coin, like a tax no one voted.

So I send you three of the new coins with this letter. Weigh them before you thank me. And when the oil-seller pays you for our olives, do not take his new coin at the old rate, as he will try to make you do; for he takes rent from his own tenants by weight, I promise you.

Greet our mother. Tell her I eat well enough, and that I have not quarrelled with the paymaster, though half the garrison would like to. Be careful of the light coin, brother. It is the only thing in [[pol:1223|Tadudüvi]] that has grown lighter while everything else grows heavier.

Farewell.
