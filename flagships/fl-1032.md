title: His Mother's Vowel
---
The host of [[pol:1562|Yidhe]] came down the valley in so many pieces that it went on arriving for four days, and for all four of them Imdhin counted it.

He sat on a pack-saddle at the side of the road with a bundle of tally sticks across his knees and a knife for the notches, and the companies went by him in their own dust: levies with their spears on their shoulders and their sandals hung round their necks, archers, men-at-arms sweating in quilted coats, mounted knights who did not look down. The captain of each hundred called out his number as he passed. Imdhin cut it. At night he carried the sticks to the tent where Khuthnakhlomin sat with a lamp, and the old man read the notches with his thumb, the way the blind read faces, and wrote the sums on paper, and coughed.

On the fourth night he put the reed down.

— Two hundred and four thousand, three hundred and sixteen, he said. Say it back.

Imdhin said it back. He had the voice; that was why he had been taken off the copying benches in [[set:0|Tallin]] at fifteen and put on a mule. The old man's own voice had begun to go somewhere on the march. It came back in the mornings, for an hour, and then it went again.

— And over there?

Down the valley [[set:32|Viradhuthpi]] showed its walls and a few lamps and nothing else. The men at the fires said three thousand. Some said four. Some said the boy king had nobody left at all and would come out alone in his dead mother's armour, and they laughed, and then for a while nobody laughed.

— They say the king of [[pol:1714|Baani]] is the emperor's son, Imdhin said.

— He is.

— Then why...

He did not know how the question ended. Why two hundred thousand men, for a son. Why any.

Khuthnakhlomin drew a fold of his cloak over his knees.

— Twenty-nine years ago I wrote a peace for the emperor's father, at Nivirathyirshi. There was an article at the end of it. I can still say it. "To bind the two houses, Pimpavbukhin, child of Pimpavbukhin shall be given in marriage to Zhidhuvbulath, child of Maatafbafin." I was twenty-five. I thought it was the best sentence I had ever written.

— You married them.

— I never saw her. I wrote her name. That is all a scribe does, he said, and blew out the lamp, though Imdhin had not finished cutting.

* * *

They came out in the morning, which no one had thought they would do.

The gate opened, and the men of Viradhuthpi walked out onto the stony ground below their own walls and made a line there, archers mostly, a few horse at the ends, and stood still. From the baggage rise where the scribes were kept, Imdhin could see the whole of them at once. He could not see the whole of his own side at all. It went back behind him along the river further than the dust would let him look, and most of it was still boiling its breakfast.

[[per:40549|Nathdalomin]] sent the knights. Imdhin heard the archers before he understood what he was hearing, a dry rattle like hail on a tiled roof, and then the knights were among them and the sound changed into something he had no word for. It did not last. The line broke from the middle outward, and what was left of it went back through the gate, and the gate shut. The rear companies had not finished eating.

The knights came back up the slope at a walk, leading horses with empty saddles. Four of them carried a man in a cloak. That was the Baani commander, [[per:40576|Ununbilshiwin]], who had stood in the middle of the line and had not gone back through the gate. Someone said he had been twenty-nine. Imdhin saw the old man do the sum, though he did not mean to: born in the year of the peace at Nivirathyirshi. Imdhin had not cut a notch since dawn. His hands wanted something to do, and he cut one, for nothing, and then did not know how to take it out.

* * *

The general wanted the count by dark.

— Ours from the rolls, said Khuthnakhlomin. The captains of hundreds answer for the absent. Theirs we count with our feet.

Ours came in by evening: three hundred and eighty-eight. Theirs took the afternoon. The old man walked the field leaning on Imdhin's shoulder, and Imdhin carried the sticks. The ground smelled of horses and of the black pitch that seeped up there between the stones, so that a man lying face down looked, from a little way off, as if he had been lying there a long time. Most of them were archers. Imdhin could tell by their hands: the hard groove across the first joint of the fingers where the string sat, in the same place where his own fingers had a groove from the reed.

Women came out of the gate in the middle of the afternoon. Nobody stopped them. They went from one body to the next and turned the faces up and laid them down again, carefully, as if it mattered which way a face lay.

One of them came to Imdhin. She was not old; there was a burn on the back of one hand, the kind a woman gets from a bread oven. She said something in the city's own tongue, which had nothing of his in it, not one word. He shook his head. She looked at the knife and the stick and the rows of notches, and he saw her understand what he was doing, and he saw what it cost her. She held up her hands, all ten fingers, and opened and closed them: how many. He did not have the words. He held up the sticks.

She found her son near the end of the line, where the horse had been. Imdhin did not see his face, because she covered it with her own body. After a while she began to say the words for the dying, the words of [[mov:58|Vishithmikh]] that every child is given: that a life is one line, laid down once from the first day to the judgement and never laid down again; that at the end of the line there is release. She said them in her language, which he did not know. But the words have a shape, and he knew the shape, and he found that he was saying them too, in his own, half a beat behind her, the way children say them after a priest.

When she had finished she sat back on her heels. She pointed at her son. Then she pointed at the stick.

He cut the notch. She watched him do it. Then she went back through the gate with the other women, and the gate shut behind them.

That night the old man added the sticks twice and said it to the lamp.

— One thousand three hundred and forty-five.

* * *

The peace was sworn at Viradhuthpi on the twentieth day of Vithni, and while the great men were made ready, the scribes waited in a palace room full of shelves.

A clerk brought them water and, when the old man asked, a roll from the shelf. He spoke the court's speech, which was Imdhin's own tongue with the vowels bent. Khuthnakhlomin untied the roll. It was the peace of Nivirathyirshi. "Each party keeps a copy sealed with the seal of the other," and here was the copy Baani had kept, with the dead emperor's seal hanging from it on a cord. The old man ran a finger down the last article, along his own young hand.

— Your queen took the field against us at Yirantudhu, he said.

— Against her husband's general, said the clerk. Then she came home, and died last year in her bed. We were three thousand eight hundred and twenty, out there, on the day. Nobody ran this time. There was nowhere to run but home.

Then the old man wrote. Imdhin ground the ink and held the parchment flat, and Khuthnakhlomin read each line aloud as it went down, as he always did, in the last of his morning voice. "Peace is granted by Pimpavbukhin, emperor of Yidhe, to Lotafbafin of Baani, on these conditions."

The clerk made a small sound.

— That isn't his name.

— It is how it is written in Tallin.

— His mother's people say it... and he said it, and it was nearly the same, only the first vowel lay further back in the throat, darker, like a stone dropped into a deeper well. Lòtafbafin.

The old man did not change it. He wrote the land and the towns; he wrote the money; he wrote the truce. He said, without looking up:

— When I was young I wrote down a lawsuit for a judge. A man of Baani wanted his father's fields back after the first peace. The steward said, "No private claim survives the treaty." They gave the man a hundred and thirty-seven [[cur:897|Rimi]], and the fields stayed with the crown. I wrote it. It is true. It is nearly always true.

He wrote the last line and touched it with the dry end of the reed, and then he touched Imdhin's chest.

— "Let it be read aloud in the markets of both lands." That is you. I haven't the voice.

Imdhin was not let into the hall for the swearing. He sat on the steps outside with the ink going dry in its dish. He saw the king of Baani go in. The king was seventeen, a year older than Imdhin, and he walked fast with his head down, like a boy late for a lesson.

* * *

He read it three times that afternoon: at the grain gate, at the well, and on the steps of the house of the Vishithmikh. A crier of the city stood one step below him and gave each sentence back to the crowd in the city's tongue, so that everything was said twice, once in the voice of the people who had won and once in the voice of the people who were listening.

The crowd was mostly women. At Niyidhazhi a man shouted something and was pulled back. At the money, 1,023,175 Yuthtu, said once in his tongue and once in theirs, there was no sound at all. Imdhin had seen the coins that morning on a changer's table: on one side the old queen Shidhilath, the boy's grandmother, who had sworn the first peace; on the other the city goddess, seated. He thought of them going up the road in carts to the boy's father, more than a thousand thousand of them, the grandmother's face over and over. At the fifteen years of truce an old man laughed out loud, and the crier, who was also old, told Imdhin why, in the court's speech: fifteen years was what the last one had said.

At the grain gate, and again at the well, Imdhin read the king's name as the parchment had it, and each time the crier, giving it back, put the dark vowel in its place without seeming to think about it, the way you straighten a thing on a shelf in your own house.

On the steps of the house of the Vishithmikh he saw her. She stood near the back with the burn on her hand, and she was not looking at him but at the steps, as if there were something written on them. The stick with her son's notch on it was in the bag at his hip with all the others, and he could no longer have told it from them.

He began again from the top. When he came to the name he let the first vowel drop, back and down, into the deeper well, the way the clerk had said it, the way the dead queen's people said it. Lòtafbafin.

The crier opened his mouth to give it back, found there was nothing in it to mend, closed it, and went on.

The parchment went to Tallin with the emperor's spelling. What Imdhin said on the steps was not written down by anyone. But for as long as it took to say, in the market of Viradhuthpi, the boy's name had his mother's vowel in it.

**Author's note.** The record gives the [[evt:41010|Battle of Viradhuthpi]] as 204,316 men against 3,820, and the [[evt:41011|Treaty of Viradhuthpi]] survives on its parchment (PAL·TR·02568). Khuthnakhlomin wrote it, and the marriage peace of 4257 (PAL·TR·02547), and the lawsuit of 4258 (PAL·LS·00676); his "captains of hundreds" come from his muster roll of 4273 (PAL·MU·01220), the coin from PAL·NU·00400. Yidhe's documents write Lotafbafin; his own stele writes Lòtafbafin (PAL·IN·03100). The rest of the vowel is mine. The record adds what Imdhin never learned: the old scribe died the next year, and from 4308 the boy ruled [[pol:1562|Yidhe]] itself.
