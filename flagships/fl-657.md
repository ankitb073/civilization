title: Four Times at Nuidaduth: Xwimsap, the Rebel Who Inherited Iti

In AY 2344 a prince of [[pol:717|Iti]] set up a rival throne at [[set:31|Bakh]] and lost every battle he fought for it. Fifteen years later he sat on the throne of Iti anyway, because his elder brother died leaving no child. [[per:19314|Xwimsap]] (AY 2322–2379) is a rare case. Of the roughly 650 breakaway states in the record, I find only two others whose rulers later governed the realm they had broken from. He left one short letter and a two-sentence epitaph, so this chapter reads him from the places where he stood, above all the field near Nuidaduth, where he fought four times.

## The conqueror's second son

Xwimsap was the second of five sons of [[per:18920|Ximamacu]], called the Victorious, and his wife Nüseyüp. In 2323, the year after his birth, his father led 17,410 men against Bakh and was beaten by 2,373 defenders, losing 5,488 men to their 593. By 2325 his armies had taken the city. Bakh had been the seat of kingdoms called Vakha from 2120 to 2312, and then of Yisamashti, and it did not forget. A palace scribe there, writing in 2340, still dated his account "in the 29th year of Yisamashti", fifteen years after that kingdom ended. He credited the victory of 2323 to "the faithful of the Miyi'te'yishio", the city's own cult.

Of his boyhood the record says nothing. Around him, famine in Ayshi killed 101,749 people in 2333–2337, and Iti began a nine-year war on Yide and lost it. His father died in 2339, when he was seventeen, and his brother [[per:19313|Fiscuamamxi]], three years older, became king. In 2340 Fiscuamamxi won a battle at [[set:247|Nuidaduth]], then signed a treaty there that made Iti pay Yide 1,649 talents of silver. The record counts that treaty, two lost battles of the Yide war and his father's defeat at Bakh among the causes of what Xwimsap did next.

## Vakha, 2344–2348

In 2344, aged twenty-two, Xwimsap claimed the throne of Iti. In the [[evt:19501|Secession of Vakha]] he took 72 of its 268 districts, made Bakh his capital and gave his state the old name of Bakh's kingdoms. The land was not his people's: the record counts only seven of those districts as Tinim. Thirty-five belonged to the people it calls Ya'cuam, and in just over half the prevailing faith was Bakh's cult, not the state pantheon. The record does not say why a Tinim prince and a resentful province chose each other; the dates suggest that his grievance found theirs. That year his wife, Tlamfin Lwixa, bore him a son and a daughter. The boy bore his great-grandfather's name, Lwixafayatl.

Pivish, Yide and Tomözel all attacked the new state, but Xwimsap's own part in the [[evt:19502|Iti Civil War]] was three attacks on one place. At the [[evt:19515|Battle of Nuidaduth (AY 2345)]] he led 9,685 men against 15,244 under [[per:19394|Yi Ax]] and lost 2,829 to Yi Ax's 693. In 2347 he faced his brother himself, 7,666 against 20,678. Outnumbered nearly three to one, he lost 804 men to Fiscuamamxi's 568, the closest fight of the war. In 2348 he came again with 8,535 against Yi Ax's 16,335 and lost 2,283 to 379. In 2345 his son died, aged one; in 2346 his wife died at twenty-seven.

The palace account of the war (PAL·CH·03594) gives Xwimsap about two and a half times his real numbers and counts his dead at 9,420, 2,677 and 7,602, each almost exactly three and a third times the record's, while Iti's dead are cut to a little over half. Such regularity is the scribe's arithmetic, not any report. He wrote "131 districts were joined to the realm" where there were 72. Fiscuamamxi's steles claim 11,600 dead for the first battle and 8,500 for the third, and neither names his brother. "May Laxla destroy the name of the enemy," the first prays. No stele survives for 2347, the one battle Fiscuamamxi fought himself. The chronicle's verdict, "swollen with pride, coveted the lands of Iti", is less a judgement than a form. The scribe of Bakh had used the same words of Xwimsap's father.

In 2348 Vakha surrendered. Both brothers signed the [[evt:19540|Suppression of the Vakha Revolt]] at Nuidaduth. It restored the 72 districts and included an amnesty, though the record names no one in it. Xwimsap lived. His mother died the next year.

## The inheritance

For eleven years the record says nothing of him, except that his daughter, Vaimap, died in 2357, aged thirteen. Iti came apart: by the end of the 2350s its measure of order stood at zero. In 2355 [[pol:801|Yennga]] began the [[evt:19609|Holy War of Yennga against Iti]], and the record lists the Secession of Vakha among its causes. Iti lost at Nudhuruigirimu in 2358 and 2359, and Yennga sacked [[set:26|Bami]] and burned its archives; the excavators of 4898 found the ash. In 2359 Fiscuamamxi died at forty. Under the law of male succession the throne passed to the brother who had fought him for it. The record does not order these events within the year.

Xwimsap, thirty-seven, signed the [[evt:19651|Treaty of Bami (AY 2359)]] with [[per:19122|Kobon II]] in his ruined capital. Iti gave up nine districts, among them the town of Ivun, and paid 1,652 talents of silver, three more than his brother had paid Yide. The tablet declares: "The war which Shevemthape stirred up is ended." Shevemthape is the Yennga spelling of his name. Yide's treaty of 2340 had blamed Fiscuamamxi in the same words, but here the record half agrees with the formula. Kobon then raised a boundary stone in the ceded land. I translated it myself (PAL·IN·06675), and its curse is brief: "May Bovme curse the enemy."

## Twenty years a king

His one surviving letter (PAL·LT·01187), from 2365, went to [[per:19558|Ba'aliof]] of [[pol:740|Pivish]]. The tablet is water-stained and partly illegible, but plain: "Your men have seized my merchants and taken their goods. Return what was taken, or I will come and take it." It was found at Bami, so it may be a copy, or never sent. In the event Pivish started the war. Ba'aliof lost at Iemi in person, and by the [[evt:19758|Treaty of Iemi (AY 2368)]] he paid Iti an indemnity.

The palace account of the [[evt:19720|Iti–Shedi War]] (PAL·CH·03625) says "the king went out to punish him", but Xwimsap did not command in it. One of his generals was Yi Ax, who had twice beaten him at Nuidaduth. The [[evt:19770|Treaty of Viririnu (AY 2369)]] gave Iti four districts with the town of Hiviyu, and tribute for thirty-one years. The palace scribe made it "7 districts in all" and "land which they had stolen", though the record shows no Iti rule there since 2300; the Shedi account calls it "Some villages of no account". That scribe, Süzholximin, also wrote the civil-war chronicle that called his king proud, dated "in the 3rd year of Xwimsap". Whether Xwimsap read it the record cannot say, but it was copied and kept.

Under him things mended a little. Between the surveys of the 2350s and 2370s the treasury grew by half and the army by more than a quarter; the record's measure of order rose from zero to 0.14, then slipped to 0.05.

## The fourth battle

In 2375 Iti attacked [[pol:871|Yimola]], a kingdom five years old. In the [[evt:19906|Battle of Nuidaduth (AY 2378)]], thirty-three years after his first defeat there, Xwimsap, fifty-six, led 14,691 men against 813 under [[per:19759|Ramenin]] and won, losing 23 to 191. It was his only recorded victory, over a force one-eighteenth the size of his, and it settled nothing. That year Tomözel conquered Yimola, leaving the [[evt:19850|Iti–Yimola War]] to end without a settlement.

He died a natural death in 2379, aged fifty-seven, with no living child. His epitaph does not mention Vakha: "Xwimsap, king of Iti, son of Ximamacu, dwells in this tomb. May Nili protect his soul." Two years later a pupil at Bami copied the king list, writing "Xwimsap, reigned 20 years", and misspelled his predecessor. "Careless! The names of the kings are sacred," the teacher wrote. The four years at Bakh were not on the list. His youngest brother, [[per:19317|Ai Yüp]], reigned three years. In 2382 Iti broke into six states, and the record again lists the Secession of Vakha among the causes. One of the six was a Kingdom of Yisamashti, seated at Bakh. Like Xwimsap, the province that followed him in 2344 waited, and in the end it had its throne.
