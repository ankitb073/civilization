To the magistrates of the treasury of [[pol:911|Ihamiw]], and through them to the king, [[per:21124|I Ash]], whom the One Power preserve: a merchant of [[set:52|Ihamiw]], who has paid his tolls at the city gates for thirty years and never been fined, sends greeting and asks to be heard.

I do not come, magistrates, as one who despises the city's need. I come as one who shares it. Yet a man who sees the foundation of a house cracking does the owner no service by praising the roof.

Consider first what has been done. Two years ago the mint struck a new [[cur:491|If]]. It bears the same face and the same legend; to the eye of a child it is the old coin. But place four of the old upon one pan of the balance and five of the new upon the other, and the old will carry the beam down. The [[evt:21407|debasement of the If]] has taken from every coin more than a fifth part of its silver, and the treasury has asked us to believe that nothing has changed.

Now consider what has followed. Ten years ago a measure of grain in the market of this city was had for a single If. Today the same measure, from the same fields, costs an If and something more than a tenth. The farmer has not grown greedier; the grain has not grown scarcer. It is the coin that has grown smaller, and the farmer, who is no fool, has noticed before the magistrates admitted it.

The foreign traders noticed sooner still. They no longer count our coin; they weigh it. A trader from abroad who once took my word and my If together now takes neither without his scales, and he looks at me across them as one looks at a man suspected of theft. Thus the shame of the mint is worn on the face of every honest dealer in the city.

I know the answer that will be given me, for I have heard it in the colonnade. The treasury is empty, it is said, and not through idleness. Since the unhappy field of [[evt:21264|Ashmath]], in the very first year of the king's reign, the city has paid for war after war. We lost at [[evt:21376|Kivunge]]; we lost twice at Iazhash; and by the [[evt:21395|Treaty of Iazhash]] we gave up lands whose rents once filled these same coffers. Then came [[evt:21464|the holy war against Niz]], begun and ended within a single year, which brought home little but its bills. I grant all of it. A city that has bled must find blood somewhere.

But, magistrates, a coin is not merely metal. It is a promise the city makes in its own name to every stranger and every child: this is what you are owed, and this is what you will receive. When the city lightens its coin in secret, it does not merely pay its soldiers with less silver; it teaches every citizen that a promise may be shaved. And what is shaved in the mint will soon be shaved in the market, in the contract, in the marriage portion, in the oath.

Since the city turned to [[mov:72|Ishiu]], the teachers in the porticoes have instructed us that the wise withdraw from wealth and from appetite. I do not quarrel with the teachers. Let the wise withdraw. But the city cannot withdraw from honest measure, for honest measure is not appetite; it is justice made visible. And since there is to be a final judgement, as the teachers say, I should not like the mint of Ihamiw to stand before it with light coins in its hand.

I ask, therefore, three things. First, that no further coin be struck lighter than the present one. Second, that the new coin be marked plainly as new, so that men may know what they hold. Third, that the old If be received at the treasury by its weight and not by its name, so that those who saved honestly are not robbed for having saved.

Grant these, and the merchants of Ihamiw will bear the city's burdens as they always have. Refuse them, and the balance in every shop will become a court in which the city is tried daily, and daily found wanting.

Written in the year 2514 from the founding of Ihamiw.
