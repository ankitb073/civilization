The verses were printed on the front page of an evening newspaper of [[set:136|Mbilu]] in AY 4990, beneath the national flag and the notice of the two minutes' silence. They were unsigned.

> The names came down the wire.
> They came down the wire at night to the post-office in every town,
> and the clerk with the green eyeshade wrote them out in a fair hand,
> and in the morning they were nailed to the board beside the door.

The poet then gives the Ministry's total for the Kingdom's dead in its recent war against Ölye: "Thirteen thousands, and nine hundreds, and five more." (PAL·PO·00022, "The Names Came Down the Wire"). The record's figure for Mbilu's losses is 13,905. The poet's number is exact. Again and again, earlier chapters of this book have had to set a boasting stele or a court chronicle against the record and measure the gap. In this last chapter the gap has nearly closed. The states of this last age mostly counted their dead accurately, published the count, and still sent more men to be counted.

The period from AY 4750 to 5000 was the most transformed in the whole record. In 4750, 266.3 million people lived under states. In 4870 the figure was 598.6 million, and in 4990 it was 1,730.3 million. In the four and a half thousand years before, the world had added fewer than a fifth as many. The mean temperature, which had been cold for two centuries, ended the period well above the long-run mean, because people had begun to burn coal and oil. The wars of these years were the largest ever fought, and money failed on a scale no earlier age had seen. This chapter tells that story and then, as the last chapter of the book, steps back to look at the whole.

## Republics, contraction and the new machines

The age began with a run of revolutions. The [[evt:45920|Shivange Revolution of AY 4750]] followed a lost war against Tallin and the indemnity that ended it. The [[evt:45977|Yiutl Revolution of AY 4757]], led by [[per:45268|Mashe Mish]], made a republic of a state seated at [[set:114|Thimisitsiy]], and that republic grew to 107.2 million people. In Khishkhis, the state from which this book is written, the [[evt:46037|Khishkhis Revolution of AY 4764]] deposed the empress [[per:45163|Navaavun II]] in favour of a republic led by Najimi. The record names the causes plainly: the two defeats at Nzhivi in the Ane Colonial War, where Khishkhis lost 73,642 men in 4752 and 26,616 in 4757, and a provincial revolt in 4755. In the 4750s the Khishkhis treasury fell to under 39,000 kilograms of silver, about a fiftieth of what it had held in 4700. Our own revolution began with an empty treasury. The [[evt:46121|Mana Revolution of AY 4770]] followed. The record counts thirty-nine revolutions between 4750 and 4999, almost nine in every ten of all those it knows.

Then trade broke. In 4753 [[evt:45919|the Great Contraction of AY 4753]] cut long-distance trade on one continent by about 55 per cent. Its recorded causes are a drought in Daadüdhih that had begun in 4715, the collapse of Dadthifè and a run of conquests that had swallowed the smaller states between the great powers. The Kingdom of Tsey, the federation that had made the first revolution in the record, broke into six states in the [[evt:46015|Collapse of the Tsey State]] of 4761.

The machines arrived regardless. The combustion engine was first adopted in Yiutl in 4770 and credited to Bailada. Railways were first built at Fyinan in 4793 by Tlamfin Muie, the electric telegraph came in Luthe in 4798, and rifled firearms in Kankush in 4802. Each depended on the steam engine of Chapter 19 or on the experimental science behind it. New creeds kept pace with the machines, and many were of a new kind. In 4772 [[per:45483|Nazhige]] founded [[mov:166|Khunzheism]] at Avash, the first movement the record classes as an ideology rather than a faith. Zhièvinduvuism followed in 4805, and in 4821 [[per:45847|Yi Imam]] founded [[mov:171|Ixiuism]] at Yiethithi; by 4988 the record counts eleven such ideologies. The old faiths went on splitting as before, and the [[evt:46451|Lave Schism]] began at Mbilu in 4808.

## When money broke

The coin of Khishkhis lost more than a quarter of its silver in 4779, falling from 7.6 grams to about 5.5. Our neighbours fared far worse. In [[pol:1769|Wemu]], a usurpation in 4761 was followed in 4787 by the collapse of the Midi, which fell from about 7.6 grams of silver to 0.06. A secession followed, and the [[evt:46425|Collapse of the Wemu State]] in 4806. Wemu's second war against Ane, from 4790 to 4802, ended in Ane's favour. In its first battle, however, at Lyismiyi, Ane lost 321,294 men, and its capital was sacked for the seventh time in its history. The Muie of Ane, already down to about 1.5 grams, collapsed to less than a hundredth of a gram in 4800. The recorded causes of the [[evt:46675|Collapse of the Ane State]] in 4834 include that hyperinflation, the sack and the slaughter at Lyismiyi. As so often in this book, the winner did not long survive the loser.

Tallin fell the same way. After the reign of [[per:45118|Pavtaalin]], called the Conqueror, ended in 4765, his successors spent beyond the empire's means and borrowed beyond its credit. Its treasury held more than four million kilograms of silver in the 4770s. By the 4820s it was nearly ten million in debt. In 4799 [[per:45641|Balmaanin]], known as the Usurper, seized the throne. In 4800 the state defaulted to a merchant house and cut the silver in the Nivbe from 4.5 grams to 4.05. In 4817 it replaced the coin with paper, which had fallen to 2.7 grams by 4829. Meanwhile the army grew from 1.1 million men to 1.4 million. In 4830 [[pol:1785|Tallin]] held 86.3 million people and 612 districts, the largest it ever was. The next year it was gone. The usurpation and the debasement are the only causes the record gives for the [[evt:46656|Collapse of the Tallin State]] in 4831. No enemy army appears among them.

Out of these ruins came the powers of the present. Tallin's heirs included [[pol:1963|Biniynge]], the city that had risen against Taulin and been crushed in Chapters 18 and 19, now an empire of 102.1 million under rulers such as [[per:45872|Pimtikhaalith]] and [[per:46189|Shikhshin II]]. Another was a new [[pol:1959|Yidhe]] Empire at [[set:0|Taulin]] itself, which grew to 216.4 million people under [[per:46334|Shifin]], called the Great, who reigned sixty-five years. Ane's heirs included the Kingdom of [[pol:1965|Mii]], seated at the ancient city of [[set:7|Ane]], and the [[pol:1970|Muvush]] Empire. Wemu's heirs included Yiethithi. After a collapse of its own in 4929 it was reborn as the [[pol:1997|Yiethithi]] of [[per:46710|I Li]], called the Old, with 91.8 million people crowded into sixty-six districts.

## The warming and the hungry margins

The record dates the [[evt:46452|Onset of the Industrial Warming]] to 4818, the point from which the burning of coal and oil began to warm the climate. Between the cold 4750s and the 4990s the world's mean temperature rose by about 1.8 degrees. The record traces twelve famines to the warming, beginning in the year of its onset. Some were very large. The famine in Yiutl in 4848 took 4,261,278 people, and the famine in Yizamashti in 4934 took 4,956,536. Others were small in number but total in effect. The [[evt:47454|Famine of AY 4902 in Nuekhmokh]] killed or drove out 41 in every hundred people in the district. A settler who had sailed there that year with four hundred people from Imshi, to plant a colony on a coast "with no master on it", reports a conversation with the ship's engineer. The engineer said the summers had grown hotter "for eighty years and more, ever since the mills first darkened the sky," and that the professors had a name for it. "I said it was a strange thing to be proud of. He said he was not proud of it, only that it was measured." On shore the settlers found the country's people starving. At home, he notes, grain cost under an *if* the measure; "Here there is no price, because there is no grain." (PAL·DI·00006, "Journal of a Settler at Sasi in the Hungry Year").

The [[evt:47756|Famine of AY 4937 in Mbambanlule]] killed or drove off about 100,000 people, 46 in every hundred. The survivors' petition to the king, of unknown provenance, explains that since their old kingdom dissolved twelve years earlier their district had had no governor, no magistrate and no relief office: "We have been, in plain terms, nobody's people." They ask not for charity but for administration, and they end: "We ask our gods for rain and Your Majesty for order. We do not think it unreasonable that one of the two should answer." (PAL·LT·01701).

This is the central paradox of the age. As these famines struck, food across most of the world became cheaper than at any time in the record. The average measure of grain in the record's markets cost 18.3 grams of silver in the 4750s, 9.6 in the 4850s and 4.9 in the 4990s. Synthetic fertilizer was first adopted in Khishkhis in 4895, credited to Nangash, and electrification at Mbilu in 4918. Railways carried grain to the cities, and the cities grew enormously: [[set:85|Nambi]] passed two million people by 4900. The petition says what that paradox felt like from the dry edge of the world: "The famine is over in the reports of the merchants. It is not over in Mbambanluli."

## The wars of the counted millions

In 4826 Khishkhis became a one-party state, and Mbilu followed in 4930. The wars that began in the 4930s were fought by industrial states with conscript armies, and the numbers are unlike anything earlier in this book. The [[evt:47798|Holy War of Mbilu against Muvush]] of 4932 to 4941 ended in stalemate and a return to the old frontier, at a cost of nearly a million men. Mbilu's hymn for the peace nevertheless proclaimed that "at Nimid the foe is broken" (PAL·PO·00021). In the [[evt:48158|Fourth Khanji Trade War]] of 4964 to 4968, Khishkhis sent 3.2 million men against 31,520 at the first battle before [[pol:2010|Shudhunvidhi]], and over the war it lost 5,673 men to the enemy's 17,786. A veteran's memoir published in 4971 says: "I will not set down here the numbers of our dead. They are printed in the official returns, and every family that sent a son knows its own share of them." (PAL·CH·08439, "Four Summers before Shudhunvidhi"). The record gives the figure he would not.

Then came the [[evt:48251|Yidhe–Khishkhis War]] of 4970 to 4977, which Yidhe began to seize [[reg:21|Daadüdhih]]. It is the deadliest war in the record: about 1.07 million lost on the Yidhe side and 588,000 on ours. At Ndulamlol in 4971, 1.47 million men attacked 3.33 million, and Yidhe lost 473,980 to our 36,430. Yidhe's own epic gets this right: "Near half a million of ours went down into the clay … and of theirs not forty thousands, and the clay did not ask whose sons." (PAL·PO·00019, "The Song of Tadudhiv, from the Fourth Canto"). The peace of Ndulamlol in 4977 made Yidhe pay Khishkhis about 1,790 tonnes of silver. The [[evt:48290|Second Mbilu–Ölye War]] followed, with battles at Lunge and Unushti, fields where Binge and Ölie had fought in Chapter 18, six hundred years earlier. So did the [[evt:48297|Yiethithi–Imshi War]]. I Li's letter from headquarters after his defeat before Imshi in 4981 lists the enemy's losses accurately for three earlier battles. It claims "upward of twenty thousand" enemy dead in the latest, where the record gives about sixteen thousand, and it withholds his own loss of 180,131 (PAL·LT·01674). In the [[evt:48410|Khishkhis–Yisamashtsi War]] of 4989 to 4991 our state took Bakh and lost 16,973 men in two years. The Office of Public Song in Nambi had the workers' choirs sing that "It was not for gold we marched" (PAL·PO·00020).

War and trade also carried disease. [[evt:48330|The Red Ague]] broke out at Shudhunki in 4980 and spread to fifteen more cities, killing 13,442,510 people. A physician in Lamlivje noted that it "is following the railway", reckoned that he was "losing about one in three", and found that the price of carbolic had doubled in a week (PAL·DI·00003). The record puts the ague's mortality at 32 per cent. The [[evt:48328|Famine of AY 4981 in Nivi]] followed. Money failed again as well: the currencies of Muvush in 4955 and Shivange in 4966 and 4971 collapsed, the last of these to a hundred-thousandth of a gram of silver. Our own agni was issued at 5.47 grams in 4864 and is worth about 0.31 today. The [[evt:48451|Yiutl–Mii War]] ended in 4997, with Mii the victor. Powered flight came at Yaaril in 4941, electronic computing at Baani in 4947 and radio at Fuvu in 4962. By 4990 [[pol:1638|Khishkhis]] held 363 million people in 625 districts, with nearly seven million under arms and a treasury almost empty.

## Five thousand years at Taulin

From the summit of this history, one fact deserves to be set beside all the numbers. The city of Taulin was already old when the record begins. Since AY 0 it has been the capital of sixteen successive states, for all but about two hundred and fifty of the five thousand years, and they have carried only two names, in shifting spellings. The first chapter of this book opened with a Kingdom of Tallin there. Yida followed, then Tallin again, then Yeda, Tallen, Yidha and Yidhe, Tallin in 4387 and Yidhe once more in 4831. Empires fell at Taulin more than a dozen times, and each time a new state took the old seat and one of the old names. [[set:7|Ane]], founded three centuries before the record, has been sacked seven times and is now a city of millions. The Vimekhulu faith of Khishkhis teaches, as a hospital chaplain put it in the plague year of 4980, that "all things turn upon the wheel and what ends will begin again." Taulin's names seem to prove it right.

The numbers do not turn. About 12 million people lived under states in AY 100, about 66 million in AY 2000 and 226 million in AY 4000. They number 1,730 million now. The record knows 2,021 states, 5,013 wars and 2,960 treaties. By its count all the wars fought before AY 4750 cost about 38 million men. The wars of this last century alone have cost more than 11 million. The teachers of the Veshethmekh, whose orators praised the green lady in Chapter 18, held that time runs in a single line from creation to judgement. The record's figures for this last age run that way: people, armies, the cheapness of bread and the heat of the summers have all risen together, and none of them has turned back.

The record itself has changed in the same way. Of the eighteen thousand or so documents in the archive, those of the first millennia are royal and monumental, cut in stone or pressed into clay. They survived because clay and stone last, while parchment and early paper mostly did not. Those of the last centuries are newspapers, telegrams, ledgers and yearbooks, and they record the names of ordinary people. The kings of the early chapters told us how many enemies they killed and hid their own dead. The clerk with the green eyeshade wrote the dead out one by one. My own discipline belongs to this age as well: all 315 excavations in the record date from 4880 to 4955. What survives at either end is what was meant to last or what was printed in great numbers. The silences in between, the burned archives and the districts that were "nobody's people", are part of the record too.

The record ends at AY 5000, and this book, written in its last year, ends with it. Nothing in five thousand years of evidence tells us which teaching of time is right, the wheel or the line, and a historian has no business pretending to know. Our task has been smaller and, we hope, more useful: to read the clerks in order. Nifpun at Taulin in 4365 wrote that "water was sold in the market by the jar." The petitioners of Mbambanluli in 4941 wrote that they asked their gods for rain and their king for order. Nearly six hundred years apart, they recorded what mattered and trusted someone to read it. We have read them.
