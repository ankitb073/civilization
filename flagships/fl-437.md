*Verses that I, [[per:23666|Nambave]], son of the king, made for my wife on the day of our marriage, and now set down fairly, one year after, in the year 3316 from the founding of Nilu.*

**I.**

Let the city that was twice burned put on its garlands,  
let the gate that was twice broken stand open for a bride.  
You, [[set:5|Nilu]], who saw the [[evt:23853|Sack of Nilu]] in one summer  
and in the next summer saw the [[evt:23865|Sack of Nilu]] again,  
wash the soot from your lintels; today the soot is not the news.  
Today a daughter of [[pol:979|Khambe]] comes in by the river road,  
and the house of [[pol:976|Puvu]] goes out to meet her with lamps.  

**II.**

I will not pretend, as poets do, that this is only love.  
The teachers of the [[mov:24|Uthungala]] bid us deal justly, even in songs.  
My father, [[per:22946|Nambaze]], the king, set his seal to the [[evt:23866|Treaty of Nilu]]  
with a hand that did not shake, though his heart shook;  
since then the silver of Puvu goes out each year to Kavu,  
weighed in [[cur:501|Shizhi]] on a stranger's scale, and our banners dip to theirs.  
A house so humbled needs friends; a prince so placed needs allies.  
Let that be said, and done with. A just man names his reasons.  

**III.**

And having named them, let me name the rest.  
I saw [[per:23755|Nanjijim]] first in the hall of her father,  
not adorned, not expecting a prince, reading by a window;  
she looked up, and asked me what I thought the soul was,  
and laughed when I answered badly, and told me why.  
The teachers say each seeker finds the truth by an inner light.  
I had not known a light could be so close and so impatient.  

**IV.**

Remember with me, wife, the years before you came:  
the [[evt:23844|Battle of Khambive]], where our spears were broken;  
the [[evt:23852|Battle of Nilu]], fought within sight of these walls;  
the [[evt:23864|Battle of Khayagu]], the third defeat, the last.  
I was a young man then, and I learned the arithmetic of losing.  
I have no wish to teach it to our sons.  
Let them learn, instead, the arithmetic of patience:  
that a vassal's yoke is weighed in years, and years are spent,  
and the one power that orders all things orders also endings.  

**V.**

Time, say the teachers, runs in a line and does not return.  
Then let this line run straight: from the burned gate to the garlanded gate,  
from the stranger's scale to our own, from my house to yours, from yours to ours.  
I do not ask the old gods for this; they are servants, as we are.  
I ask the One that judges at the end of the line  
to find us, at the end, to have dealt justly with each other.  

**VI.**

Now, Nanjijim, give me your hand before the witnesses.  
Khambe, you have lent us your daughter; Puvu will not squander her.  
Moderation in all things, the teachers say. In all things but this.  
Let the lamps burn till morning. Let the city that was twice burned  
burn once more, tonight, with lamps and with nothing else.  

*Here end the verses. Written by my own hand, which is less steady than my wife's.*
