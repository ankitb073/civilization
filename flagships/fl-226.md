title: The Harvest Returns to the Record: The Age of Parchment and Plough
---
The Age of Parchment and Plough is, after the Age of Bronze, the longest of our conventional ages, running from AY 3154 to 4593, and the hardest to characterise in a single lecture. Its name points to two things: the spread of written administration and the agrarian base that paid for it. I want to argue that the second deserves more weight than it usually receives. War remained, as in every earlier age, the commonest cause of collapse. But in this age the harvest re-entered the record of collapse, after more than a thousand years in which it had been all but absent, and that tells us something about what these states had become.

## War on a new scale

First, the familiar. My list of eighteen great collapses runs from the [[evt:29331|Collapse of the Yarel State]] in AY 3198 to that of Yisamashtsi in 4118, and most of them, like nearly all the age's lesser collapses, follow on war. The armies that fought those wars were larger than any earlier age had seen: battles in which one side fielded more than a hundred thousand men recur in the record of the 3400s and 3600s. The [[evt:32324|Collapse of the Tomzel State]] in AY 3466 followed a campaign in 3448 in which the [[pol:1212|Tomzel]] empire lost more than twenty-one thousand men in a single battle. The [[evt:34512|Collapse of the Khishkhis State]] in AY 3666 followed defeat by the [[pol:1279|Dagafu]] empire in 3644–3646, at the hands of armies of up to a hundred and thirty thousand men.

That collapse deserves a word in this journal. The [[pol:1243|Khishkhis]] kingdom broke into a new [[pol:1454|Khishkhis]] empire and several neighbours, and our present state descends from that empire. Dagafu, the victor, fared no better for long. The [[evt:34645|Collapse of the Dagafu State]] came in AY 3681, and its recorded causes lie inside its own borders: a usurpation in 3620, a revolt and a civil war later in that decade. Victory abroad did not buy stability at home.

## The harvest

Now the new element. Famine does not appear among the recorded causes of any collapse in the Ages of Iron or of Coin and Creed, and in the Age of Bronze only twice. In this age it returns. The [[pol:1137|Yarel]] empire, before its collapse in 3198 into city-states such as [[pol:1264|Yida]] and [[pol:1265|Rila]], suffered a famine in 3176 that the record counts among the causes. So did the [[pol:1139|Lienonoshal]] empire, struck in 3290 and broken in the [[evt:30623|Collapse of the Lienonoshal State]] of 3320. So did Tomzel, struck in 3460 by a famine that the record ties to an anomaly of the climate, six years before its fall. And the Yidhe collapse of 3883 followed, in a single year, a famine, a migration and a civil war.

I do not want to overstate this. By my count famine figures among the causes of only five of the age's two hundred or so collapses, against war in nearly all of them. But it is new in kind. A state that can be undone by a bad harvest is a state whose army and court depend on the surplus of its fields year by year: a plough state in the full sense. The bronze kingdoms, smaller and closer to their land, appear to have been less exposed. It is the size of these empires, and the armies they raised, that made the harvest a political event.

## Heirs and chains

The age also shows, more clearly than any other, how long the chains of succession could run. The [[evt:29590|Collapse of the Luthe State]] in 3219 gave birth both to a [[pol:1277|Puvu]] empire and to Dagafu. The Tomzel of 3466 left a second [[pol:1371|Tomzel]], whose own fall in the [[evt:35280|Collapse of the Tomzel State]] of 3739 produced the [[pol:1497|Ölie]] empire, which in turn collapsed in 4114. The [[evt:32890|Collapse of the Iethithi State]] in 3517, after a pestilence and two usurpations, produced an [[pol:1397|Ayshi]] empire that broke in its turn in the [[evt:34897|Collapse of the Ayshi State]] of 3702, leaving among its heirs the [[pol:1480|Nuwkennga]] empire.

What defined the age, then, was not a new kind of war but a new kind of vulnerability. The record cannot tell us how hungry ordinary people were in the years when no state fell; it records famines chiefly when they mattered to thrones. That is its limit, and ours.
