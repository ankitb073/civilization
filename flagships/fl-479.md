**CHAPTER THE FIRST: WHY WE WENT INTO THE STREETS**

I am asked, now that fifteen years have passed and grey has come into my beard, to set down what I remember of the [[evt:46957|Yisamashtsi Revolution]]. The publishers want a book of heroes. I can give them only a book of tired men who did a necessary thing and were surprised to find it done.

Let me begin where every man of my generation begins: with Yyixi.

I was six years old when my father went to the [[evt:46582|Battle of Yyixi]]. He did not come back. Neither did nearly twenty-seven thousand others, by the War Ministry's own count, though in our street we always said the Ministry could not count. Three-quarters of a million of the enemy came against us, and our army was little more than a hundred thousand. Then came the [[evt:46583|Sack of Namosash]]. I remember the smoke over the river quarter, and my mother pressing my face into her coat so that I would not see the bridges burning. I saw them anyway.

The crown survived that year. It survived as a cracked bell survives: it still hung in the tower, but no one liked the sound of it any more.

Seventeen years later came the [[evt:46736|Secession of Tiaiosh]], and a whole province walked out of the kingdom as a lodger walks out of a house where the roof leaks. The King's ministers called it treason. We called it arithmetic.

**CHAPTER THE SECOND: THE NIGHT ITSELF**

By the time [[per:45981|Mashe Imin]] had sat ten years on the throne, the price of bread in [[set:30|Namosash]] was a weekly scandal in the papers, the railway shareholders sat in the King's council and the railway workers sat in the King's prisons, and the priests of [[mov:25|Myitsiyishio]], who are not quick to speak, had begun to preach on justice in dealings and on the wisdom of withdrawing from appetite. Everyone in the congregation knew whose appetite was meant.

I was a compositor then, setting type for one of the evening papers. That is how I came to know [[per:46071|Aida]]. He brought us his articles himself, late, smelling of tobacco, and stood over the stone while we set them, correcting commas. He was not a great speaker. He was something rarer: a man who could make a frightened crowd feel that it was a public.

The night itself I remember in pieces. The gas lamps going out one street after another as the lamplighters joined the march. The telegraph office taken without a shot, because the operators had been on our side for a month. The palace guard standing aside. Aida on the steps of the Assembly, reading the proclamation in a voice that did not carry beyond the third row, so that it was passed back through the square man to man, like water in a fire line. The kingdom of Yisamashtsi ended that night, and the Republic began.

Mashe Imin was not harmed. He left the capital and lived out his days quietly. He died two years ago, and I confess I felt something like grief. He was not a wicked man. He was a man in the wrong chair.

**CHAPTER THE LAST: WHAT CAME OF IT**

[[per:46071|Aida]] is dead too, these five years. He did not live to see the end of the holy war that was brought against us: the defeat at the [[evt:47089|Battle of Tunavükhini]], the turn at the [[evt:47093|Battle of Bakh]], the great victory at Bakh the year after, and at last the [[evt:47123|Treaty of Bakh]], which President [[per:46173|Isyish Tluie]] signed two years ago. Those who said a republic could not defend itself have had their answer.

I will not pretend everything is well. Bread in Namosash is cheaper, at a little more than one [[cur:1112|Ime]] the measure, but the railway shareholders have found their way into the Assembly as easily as they once found their way into the council. The priests still preach on appetite. They have not run short of material.

But I can do one thing now that my father could not. I can write this, and print it, and sign it, and sleep in my own bed tonight. That was what we went into the streets for. It was enough.

*Namosash, 20 Fyiow 4346 of the Myitsiyishio.*
