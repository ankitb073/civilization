title: Dying at the Height (AY 2750–3000)

In AY 2908 a scribe named Yi Müa, keeping the register of a temple house at Azha, set down what the year had done in three plain sentences:

> The wells ran dry and the river shrank to a stream. Grain stood at 0.872 Iscuax the measure. The canals stood dry and cracked.

The record (PAL·RE·00113, *Record of the dry years from AY 2907*) is one of very few documents of the period written neither to praise a king nor to blame one. The years on either side of it saw the most violent turn of the age. In 2906 the Khishkhis Empire broke into six states. In 2908 the Khambe Empire broke into three. In 2911 the Yeda Empire broke into six and the Peme Empire into five. Seventeen years earlier, in 2889, the Yiotl Empire had broken into six.

## The map of 2870

Look at the map of AY 2870, on which only three parts in a hundred of the land lay outside any state. The four largest states were [[pol:1004|Yeda]], with 15 per cent of the land, [[pol:924|Yiotl]] with 12, [[pol:967|Khishkhis]] with 11 and Peme with 7. Between 2889 and 2911 every one of them was gone, broken into twenty-three successors. None of them withered first. Yiotl reached its greatest size, 18.4 million people in 364 districts, in 2880, nine years before the end. Khishkhis peaked at 12.9 million in 2900. Yeda counted 29.4 million people and 489 districts in 2910, the year before it fell. These empires died at their height, and that is the puzzle of the chapter.

The states broke, but the people under them kept multiplying, from 106.4 million in 2750 to 123.0 million in 2870 and 145.8 million in 2990. Nor was it a matter of money. The record shows no debasement of any coin anywhere between 2750 and 3000. Yeda's silver Tonyo was struck at 5.4 grams from first to last, and its treasury still held some 2.4 million kilograms of silver in the decade it fell. Merchants founded 159 houses in the period, and rulers introduced 53 new coinages and weight standards. Whatever broke these empires, it was not a shortage of silver.

The century had opened with a collapse left over from the last. The old [[pol:887|Turamtu]] empire, carried over from the previous chapter, ended in 2761 under [[per:23715|Tyethtithith]], remembered as the Last. She outlived her state by twelve years. The record lists the causes of the [[evt:24349|Collapse of the Turamtu State]] as a plague of 2692, defeats in 2699, 2716 and 2719, and a secession in 2717. Of the six heirs, the kingdom of [[pol:1052|Turamtu]] held the old capital and lasted until 3036. Its queen [[per:24329|Tuenaluth]] reigned there for fifty-seven years, from 2778 to 2835. Elsewhere the first decades were a run of splits: the [[evt:24224|Secession of Diegine]] from Kaazhe in 2752, the [[evt:24236|Secession of Nistloti]] from Vami in 2753, and the [[evt:24480|Secession of Kambambül]] from Tangath in 2771. Kambambül returns as an empire in the next chapter.

## The house at Taulin

Few states of the age fought as often or won as steadily as Yeda. From its capital at [[set:0|Taulin]] it fought eighteen wars between 2755 and 2911 and lost none of them. Under [[per:24169|Tashefen]], called the Great, who reigned from 2765 to 2811, it beat Rieney, Jenontono, Zhievampeva and Diebegovebenga in turn. His successor [[per:24362|Toshen II]] annexed Virrnu and took land from the new kingdom of Turamtu. Then [[per:25080|Toshen III]], the Conqueror, who reigned from 2842 to 2878, swallowed Monu, beat Tomözel and Veni, and annexed Dueminnga. Under him Yeda grew from 321 districts to 450.

The court historians made these wars easier than they were. The Yeda account of the First Yarel–Yeda War (PAL·CH·04480), written at Taulin in 2835, says that at Nin in 2779 the enemy brought 19,595 men and lost 6,764, against 482 dead for Yeda. The record gives the old kingdom of Yarel only 8,112 men, of whom 2,113 fell. Yeda, commanded by Tashefen himself, lost 828. The chronicle more than doubled the enemy and cut its own dead by two-fifths. It is right that the Yarel usurper Vakolvan died in the field in 2778. Court chronicles of this age are seldom wholly invented. They mostly bend the arithmetic.

The best witness to what Yeda's victories cost comes from its enemies. In the [[evt:25793|First Peme–Yeda War]] of 2881–2886, [[pol:1006|Peme]] attacked and lost. But the Peme poet of *The Lay of the Few at Nelo* (PAL·PO·00010), writing in 2904, did his sums honestly. At Nelo, which the record calls Nilu, 45,801 men of Peme met 164,183 of Yeda. The poem says that "for every three sons of Peme who lay down, two of Yeda lay beside them." The record agrees: Peme lost 4,481 and Yeda 2,724. For the whole war the poet gives Peme "twenty thousand dead, and Yeda not half so many." The record gives 19,918 and 7,709. Against him stands the rock relief that Makotnakhen II of Yeda set up near Benga (PAL·IN·01904), which claims 28,400 enemy dead at Binge in 2885, nearly six times the true figure of 4,880. Peme gave up nothing at the peace of Binge except a promise of fifteen years' quiet. "Which city, then, was broken?" the poet asks.

The record's measure of order inside Yeda fell almost without a break, from its height around 2800 to nothing at all by 2910. In the same years the army grew from about 144,000 men to 294,000. After Toshen III died in 2878 three rulers followed in seventeen years. A second Peme war, from 2901 to 2904, was won as the first had been. A holy war against Yisamashtsi in 2910 simply dissolved. In 2911 came the [[evt:26088|Collapse of the Yeda State]], and the record names no cause for it at all. I think that silence is honest. Yeda was not defeated. It had grown too large and too often at war to hold together. Of its six heirs, a kingdom of [[pol:1135|Yeda]] kept Taulin. Two others would become the great powers of the next century: [[pol:1137|Yarel]] at [[set:245|Yaaril]] under [[per:25757|Tathononen]], and [[pol:1139|Lienonoshal]]. In the same year the [[evt:26095|Collapse of the Peme State]] left five states where Peme had been. Each enemy had worn the other out.

## Cold decades and dear bread

Elsewhere the empires faced pressures that Yeda's heartland largely escaped; at Taulin a measure of grain stayed between 7 and 13 grams of silver throughout. The average temperature of the period sits only 0.18 °C below the long-run mean, but that average hides a deep trough. Two great eruptions, of Mount Latlmis in 2840 and of Mount Tütsithi in 2861, made the 2840s and 2860s the coldest decades of the age, nearly 0.6 °C below normal. Droughts came in a long series: the [[evt:24561|Onset of the Great Drought in Daadüdhih (AY 2780)]] across 339 districts, another from Tsiey in 2808, then the [[evt:25355|Onset of the Great Drought in Khishkhih (AY 2846)]], which lay on 271 districts for a quarter of a century, and two droughts from Baan, in 2856 and 2880. Between 2856 and 2866 the record lists five famines, which together killed more than 107,000 people.

Across some 240 markets the average price of a measure of grain rose from 11.1 grams of silver in the 2750s to 13.9 in the 2780s, as the Daadüdhih drought took hold. Local prices tell more. At [[set:85|Nambi]], the capital of Khishkhis, which lay inside both the Tsiey and Khishkhih droughts, grain cost 9.6 grams in the 2750s and nearly 20 in the 2830s and 2840s. At [[set:114|Thimisitsiy]], the Yiotl capital, it cost 9.4 grams in the 2750s, 26.1 in the 2850s and 33.0 in the 2860s. Bread there had trebled, and it stayed dear for three decades before the empire broke.

Yiotl had made its own troubles as well. Under [[per:23952|Mashe Yueve I]] and the queen [[per:24635|Mashe Yueve II]], called the Victorious, who reigned from 2804 to 2845, it fought eleven wars in its last century and won most of them. By the 13th Ayshi Trade War of 2864–2870 it was sending 83,911 men against 3,806 of Veni at Shish, and it lost 811 men over the whole war to the enemy's 2,354. The record's measure of order in Yiotl fell from moderate in the 2780s to almost nothing by the 2880s. After the queen [[per:24880|Piebe]] and her successor Yewamovi, the throne passed under a regency in 2882. Seven years later came the [[evt:25844|Collapse of the Yiotl State]]. As with Yeda, the record lists no cause. Hunger and endless war lie in the background, but neither is written down as the cause.

Khishkhis followed the same course more slowly. Under [[per:24479|Nangash]] the Old and then [[per:24965|Nanane]] it won almost everything it attempted. The [[evt:25616|Khishkhis–Khambe War]] of 2864–2869 ended with the Treaty of Khambe, which survives on a bronze tablet (PAL·TR·01701). By it [[pol:979|Khambe]] gave up 84 districts and eight towns in the [[reg:24|Pyivi]] country and paid a yearly tribute for twelve years. But the record lists as a cause of Khishkhis's eventual collapse the battle that opened this war, at Dambüzhi in 2865, where Khishkhis lost 7,244 men to Khambe's 3,673. The empress [[per:25020|Nadazum]], called the Pious, came to the throne in 2876. In 2893, the year the Kashunji rose against her, the realm took up the faith of Uthungala, which most of its people already held. The Kashunji rebels lost 2,876 men. Putting them down cost Khishkhis 1,236. A holy war waged by Kaazhe from 2896 to 2901 was beaten off at a cost of 12,255 more. The [[evt:26014|Collapse of the Khishkhis State]] followed in 2906. A smaller [[pol:1125|Khishkhis]] kept Nambi and lasted until 3122, and the kingdom of [[pol:1126|Puvu]] was among the other heirs. Nadazum lived on until 2921. Khambe, bled by the same war, went too, in the [[evt:26036|Collapse of the Khambe State]] of 2908. One of its heirs was [[pol:1132|Tambakh]], which under [[per:25994|Natsunge I]], called the Great, became a great power within a generation.

## Altars changed by consent

If the age destroyed states, it was fertile in faiths. The record dates seven new ones to the period: [[mov:85|Ninxisatl]], founded by [[per:24103|Xa Ximam]] at Shish in 2757; [[mov:88|Enethnothton]], founded by [[per:24211|Taftovan]] in 2764; [[mov:89|Bangegumgi]], founded by [[per:24486|Nashinjè]] at Dyizhih in 2785; and [[mov:92|Lave]], founded at Avash in 2822 by another [[per:24921|Nashinjè]]. The two are easily confused; the first was a woman, the second a man. [[mov:94|Udushmamash]] followed in 2878 under [[per:25404|Tuhim]], then the [[evt:25953|Founding of the Setlietlpovi Faith]] at Ishwyithie in 2893, and Taletuetpoth at Biniynge in 2947. Four schools of philosophy date from the same years. The old cult of Mie'to'ieshio still held a quarter of the land in 2870, ahead of Uthungala and Jinru at 12 per cent each.

What makes this period unusual is not the founding of faiths but the way courts went over to them. The record lists twenty-four conversions, twenty-two of them made because most of the people had already changed, and the literary sources describe several from the inside. The converts all say the same thing. In 2823 the queen Huhhimin of [[pol:987|Mane]], beaten twice at Chezhietle, took up [[mov:57|I'ishiu]] before her council in a sermon that survives (PAL·SC·00137). "A queen who will not follow her people where they have already gone is not leading; she is merely late." In 2875 the king of [[pol:1053|Veni]], beaten at Shish by Yiotl and sacked the next year by Diebegovebenga, wrote to his old teacher that grain cost "nearly three Yvenu" below his palace and that "the poor have done a sum" (PAL·LT·01742). The *Hymn of the Inner Lamp at Iethithi* (PAL·PO·00071) tells the same story for 2906, and the homily of Maeshi Mish at Ihaemiw (PAL·SC·00138) for 2933: "The people led the king." Each of these conversions followed defeat. Mie'to'ieshio was the faith most often abandoned, seven times in the period. It taught moderation and withdrawal, and to people who had just lost a war that was cold comfort.

These new faiths did not stay gentle. The record's sharpest irony falls in 2964, when a later kingdom of Mane, ruling from the same city of Tsivintuha where Huhhimin had preached compassion, persecuted the Udushmamash. King Ashni's letter to the keeper of the ordained (PAL·LT·01745) encloses the last words of a condemned teacher: "I go straight on. Do not look for me on the wheel." The king defends the edict and then admits to doubts. He orders that the dead man's letter "is not to be published, nor burned", but kept under seal. That is one reason it survived. The record lists two more persecutions before 3000, the first stirrings of what would become a whole age of shuttered shrines.

## The quarrel over Yiotl's estate

The last third of the century was spent fighting over what Yiotl left behind. Its heirs, [[pol:1115|Tamesitsiyi]], [[pol:1116|Iethithi]], Mieshlethie, Imshi, Namosash and Iti, lay in and around the country of [[reg:5|Yiutl]] and Iith. Their neighbours, and in time each other, fought over them for the rest of the century.

[[pol:922|Ana]] did best. It had already beaten off Yisamashtsi in the [[evt:24529|Yisamashtsi–Ana War]] of 2777–2791, which [[per:23894|Shi'omamshe]] ended with trading rights and a fifteen-year truce. Here too the losing side's poet was more honest than its king. The victory stele of Ainsima II (PAL·IN·01813) claims 18,400 men of Ana killed at Pivithie in 2786; the record has 2,506. But the *Elegy for Those Who Fell for Yiotl* (PAL·PO·00011), written at Ool in 2805, gives Yisamashtsi's dead as "twenty-three thousand and more" and Ana's as "as many, and a few more". The true figures were 23,411 and 23,549. Under [[per:25989|A'initlue'e I]], who reigned for fifty-two years, Ana turned on the Yiotl heirs. The [[evt:26734|Second Ana–Tamesitsiyi War]] of 2964–2975 cost Ana 46,865 dead to Tamesitsiyi's 15,579. At Likha in 2967 alone Ana lost 42,867 men out of 95,554. *The Song of Yi'otl* (PAL·PO·00009), written at Ane a generation later, puts the dead of Likha at "Forty thousand of Ane" and blames appetite for the disaster. The peace of Faamni gave Ana twenty-six districts, tribute for thirty-two years and Tamesitsiyi's vassalage. Its text is lost, and a single clause survives only because a chronicle quoted it 831 years later (PAL·TR·01775). Survival in this archive is often that much a matter of chance.

In the middle of that war, in 2970, [[evt:26793|The Long Sickness]] broke out at Ivu. It spread along the roads to thirty-three more cities and killed about 1.43 million people, one in six of those it reached. The list of stricken places reads like the war's order of battle: Faamni, Thushashfi, Likha, Nyinsitlasa, Pivithie, Onithie, and Thimisitsiy itself. The record does not say that the armies carried it. It does show the sickness following the armies' routes, city by city. The record counts it among the causes of Kaddanul's fall in 3005. A new drought from Ayshi began in 2971 and lasted seventy years. Ana kept fighting anyway. The [[evt:27011|Ana–Mieshlethie War]] of 2989–2993 put 115,337 of its men in the field at Pivithie and made [[pol:1117|Mieshlethie]] a vassal. By 2990 Ana had 19.9 million people, more than any other state.

Meanwhile the [[evt:26578|First Imshi–Iethithi War]] of 2951–2960 pitted two of the heirs against each other. Iethithi won, and King Luesh'ayof's stele at Azha claims 43,400 enemy dead at the battle of Ash, eight times the 5,436 of the record. In 2990 [[pol:1119|Imshi]] reopened the quarrel as the [[evt:27036|War of the Iethithi Succession]], which was still undecided in 3000. In the [[evt:26984|Ninth Yimla Trade War]], from 2986 to 2996, the kingdom of [[pol:1052|Turamtu]] fought [[pol:1122|Eshueethie]] for the markets of [[reg:14|Yimla]]. It is hard not to connect this war with prices at home. Grain in the city of Turamtu, about 9 grams a measure in the 2770s, stood at 31.7 in the 2970s and 35.2 in the 2980s. The new drought lay across Yimla. Queen Nathuth wrote from the camp before Nuidaduth in 2991 (PAL·LT·01664) that the enemy had left "more than seven thousand dead". The record gives 5,449, and Turamtu lost 2,528, which she declined to "number in a letter that an enemy might read." Turamtu lost the war in the end: its dead totalled 46,333, against 22,592 for Eshueethie.

Arch and aqueduct building appeared at Kaazhe in 2930. Seven years later Ramadheluekhen, an engine-maker at Diebegovebenga, wrote to his king about a bow laid crosswise on a wooden stock, promising that "a farmer can learn to use it in a month" (PAL·LT·01709). He asked for horn, sinew and ash for fifty of them and offered to melt his bronze pins if they failed. The crossbow was first adopted there in 2937 and was invented again, independently, at Kapne in 2966. Language kept changing as well: Anim, Ishyi and Isfo were recognized as separate tongues by 2799, Mbavu and Lile by 2899, and the record dates seventy-eight sound changes to the period. Even our evidence is tilted toward rulers. Of the 751 routine documents that survive from these years, 442 are inscriptions, about half of them victory stelae, and only sixteen are ledgers. A royal stele was made to last and a merchant's ledger was not, so we see these kings far more clearly than the people who paid for them.

In AY 3000 the great empires of 2870 were gone. Ana was the largest state on the map, with 20.4 million people, but the record's measure of its internal order was low. Lienonoshal and Yarel were growing out of Yeda's ruins, Tambakh out of Khambe's, and [[pol:1078|Luthe]], which had arisen in 2810 and had lost half a million people to the famine of 2986, was expanding under [[per:26504|Nakambège II]]. The Iethithi succession was still being fought at Tanshedi. The next chapter takes up the story there.
