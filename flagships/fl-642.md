title: A Small Thing in the Likeness of the Great: Ma'she Yueve and the First Wheel

In AY 186, in the kingdom of [[pol:0|Shue'ethie]], a man called [[per:476|Ma'she Yueve]] made the [[evt:386|Invention of the wheel in Shue'ethie]]. No wheel appears anywhere in the record before it. Of the man almost nothing is kept. He was born in AY 154 and died a natural death in AY 188, at thirty-four. His name means "Ten Fire". The record gives him no father, no mother, no wife and no children, and a single role, held for a single year. Beyond that there survive one speech, in a copy made long after, and one line in a court annal. This chapter tries to read both fairly.

## A kingdom of barley

He was born in the sixth year of [[per:388|O'e Ada]], who had taken the throne in AY 149 at fourteen, "being a child", as the annals put it, and would keep it for forty years. Shue'ethie was a Bronze Age kingdom of the [[mov:0|La'im]] gods, ruled from [[set:3|Ishwyithie]], a city already seven centuries old. By the end of the 150s its survey counted 1,299,376 people in 38 districts and an army of 7,796 men. Only 39,020 of those people lived in towns, three in every hundred; the rest worked the land. By the end of the 180s the kingdom held 1,513,547 people in 44 districts. The record knows no war fought by Shue'ethie in all his thirty-four years. When he was four the city of Ashash was founded; when he was twenty-two the king's government fixed the silver weight of the ashfan.

Its harvest moved on human backs. Seventy-eight years before his birth, Queen Todifoyatl's ordinances had bound every village to bring "the tithe of its barley to the granary of the queen at the threshing, one sheaf of ten" (PAL·DE·00957). Whether the rule still stood in his day the record does not say.

## Words in the house of tablets

The one text in his voice is the Words of Ma'she Yueve in the House of Tablets (PAL·SP·00006), dated to "the 38th year of O'e Ada", AY 186. What survives is a medieval copy kept in the palace archive at Ishwyithie, with several leaves missing, here read in Nahuvdhi's translation. It uses the formulas of the age ("Hear me", "I have spoken"), and a copyist's hand may lie over the speaker's. Still, the voice is not a king's. "I am not a priest. I am not a scribe. My hands are rough." He calls himself a carpenter and a carpenter's son; the record can neither confirm nor deny it.

Before him, he says, temple stones moved so: "Forty men pulled it on a sledge," over logs carried from the back to the front. "Many men. Many days. Many backs broken." He remembers a log rolling down a riverbank when he was a boy, and the thought that "the log wants to roll." Then come a carpenter's steps: a round, a pole of oak, grease of fat in the hole, and a hard lesson, "A round cut from one log splits," so he built it from "three planks, pegged together with pegs of hard wood". It reads like workshop instruction, not hymn.

His claims ("What forty men pulled, now four oxen pull. What went in ten days, now goes in two") cannot be checked; they are a petitioner's numbers. Notice whom he names first among those it helps: "The barley of the fields goes to the granary in one load, not in twenty baskets." The king paid him "a field and a house and a weight of ashfan silver."

He was careful with the priests, too. In a faith whose truth was handed down in order by an ordained priesthood, a new thing needed defending, and he gave it a theology: "I did not make a new thing. I made a small thing in the likeness of the great thing." He also asked the war-goddess to "not take the wheel for the host before the farmer has it for his barley. But if the king wills it, let it be so."

## One line in the annals

He asked that it be written "that the wheel was made in Shue'ethie, in the 38th year of O'e Ada the king, by Ma'she Yueve the carpenter." The next sentence of the copy is broken: "Let [...] another city say: I made it." The gap probably held a "not". It took the word that mattered most to him.

Thirteen years later Figwes, a scribe of the house of tablets, closed the Annals of Shue'ethie, AY 100–199 (PAL·AN·00002). The year's entry reads, in full: "The wheel was devised." The device is kept; the carpenter is not. Figwes was twenty-two in AY 186 and may have heard the speech. Ma'she Yueve died two years later; O'e Ada followed in 189. We do not know whether the carpenter lived to work his field.

## The wheel after him

The other cities did say it. In AY 207 I'cuil Mish of Tivintusa made the wheel again, independently by the record's reckoning, and others made it twice more: by Sho'e To'a, a woman of Ana, in AY 287, and in Luyulule in AY 468. In AY 262 a servant in the Tivintusa camp at Yuftuyi wrote to his king that "The war-carts went down into the mud, the wheels went down into the mud" (PAL·LT·01714). The record confirms his figures: Tivintusa lost 1,366 men there to Navnga's 422. Shue'ethie never had the war chariot in the record before it fell in AY 434; the record's first war chariot appears at Tsinondonu in AY 1081.

His name outlasted him too. In AY 793 a man also called Ma'she Yueve kept the day-book of the House of Titho at Fienanna (PAL·LE·00121). The first wheel belongs to someone the record can barely see: a carpenter who watched a log roll, asked for his name on a tablet and got a line without it, and hoped the farmer would have his wheel before the soldier did.
