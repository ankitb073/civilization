title: Our King Could Not Suffer the Insult: The Tatudüfe Rising of AY 520–523 in Its Two Chronicles

In a war of independence, the suppressor's scribe magnifies the threat so as to magnify the suppression. The rebel's scribe, if the rebellion failed, has to explain why it was worth attempting. The [[evt:1857|First Tatudüfe War of Independence]] (AY 520–523) shows both habits with unusual clarity. I argue that the Tatudüfe account, though it records almost nothing, is the more revealing, because of what it chose to say instead.

## The tablets

The Nilu account (PAL·CH·00236, Injèlu, excavated at Nilu in AY 4901) was set down by Nakatha "in the 17th year of Nambave", AY 532. That was nine years after the war, and the last year of the Nilu state. The Tatudüfe account (PAL·CH·00235, Kukuge, excavated at Shyih in AY 4894) is Natinangu's, dated "in the 15th year of Tatudüfe", AY 534. That reckoning counts the years of a kingdom that had existed for only three of them. Nanjil excavated both, and Nalilimi translated both. Natinangu was a grown man during the rising. His silences are not the silences of ignorance.

## Field by field

*Who began it.* The war grew out of the [[evt:1856|Revolt of Tatudüfe]] in AY 520: [[pol:71|Tatudüfe]] took up arms for its independence. Nakatha agrees: "there came word that Nanethe of Dadothefè had gathered an army against Nelo." Natinangu reverses it, borrowing the stock formula of the wronged party: "Nambave of Nilu, forgetting the oaths his fathers swore, set his hand against Tatudüfe. Our king could not suffer the insult." For a rebel scribe this is less a lie than a point of view: the insult came first, and the revolt answered it.

*The battles.* The record has three engagements, all won by [[pol:5|Nilu]]: the [[evt:1871|Battle of Kabaf (AY 521)]], the [[evt:1875|Battle of Kabaf (AY 522)]] and the [[evt:1879|Battle of Shyih (AY 523)]], fought at the rebel capital in [[reg:21|Daadüdhih]]. Nakatha has all three, in the right places and years. Natinangu has none. His tablet passes directly from the insult of AY 520 to the end of the war in AY 523. (Translators should note that the record knows two rebel commanders called Nanüdi and two Nilu commanders called Nambave. Nakatha's text does not distinguish them.)

*The dead.* The record's figures are unremarkable: 1,365 Tatudüfe dead against 874 for Nilu, from rebel forces of two to three thousand facing Nilu armies of three to eight thousand. Against the record's Nilu losses of 532, 47 and 295, Nakatha admits 221, 20 and 123: 364 dead in all. For the enemy he gives 1,152, 1,068 and 2,545, three and a half times the record's 330, 306 and 729. His rebel musters (4,896, 2,841 and 4,466) are about half as large again as the record's 3,267, 1,896 and 2,980. Natinangu gives no dead at all.

*Outcome and land.* Nilu won, and the [[evt:1880|Suppression of the Tatudüfe Revolt]] annexed all nine of the rebel kingdom's districts. Nakatha says so, in his most candid sentence: "Dadothefè is no more, and its people serve Nelo." But he counts twenty-two districts won, nearly two and a half times the size of the whole kingdom at its height. Natinangu records no terms and no lost district. He says only that in AY 523 "the last of the strongholds fell", and asks who will now remember the kings of old.

*Commanders.* No commander died in battle. But the record adds what neither chronicle mentions: Nanüdi, the rebel king, who had commanded at Shyih, was executed at the suppression.

## The weight of a sentence

Measured against that execution, the tablet's one sentence of motive, "Our king could not suffer the insult", is the most poignant line in either text. The king who would not suffer an insult suffered execution, and the tablet does not say so. It was written in AY 534, two years after Nilu itself had collapsed. No Nilu court survived to contradict it. The king keeps his honour, the battles go untold, and the dead go uncounted, since counting them would concede a war lost.

Nakatha's distortions are the ordinary arithmetic of a victorious court, which needed the rebels to look dangerous and the cost of crushing them to look modest. Natinangu's are the arithmetic of grief, and colleagues who rank his tablet below Nakatha's for completeness are measuring the wrong thing.
