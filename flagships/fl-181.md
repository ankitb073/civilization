*The Star and the Moon: a Panegyric upon the August Empress Shithkhukhuth, wherein it is shown by seven arguments that her name was given by Providence. Delivered in the Great Hall of the College at [[set:0|Tallin]] on the 5th of Ushuth, 2210 of the [[mov:58|Vishithmikh]], and now set down in writing.*

The learned know that in the ancient speech of the Jego, from whom our Empress descends, her name signifies *star and moon*. It has pleased some idle wits to say that names are mere sounds. I propose to refute them, not with verses, which prove nothing, but with arguments, which the reader may test.

**First argument: that the star rises early.** A star is seen before the sun is fully set. So our [[per:42203|Shithkhukhuth]] came to the throne of [[pol:1785|Tallin]] in the year 2173, when her father [[per:42039|Balshiymaanin]] departed this life, and she was a child of two years, not yet able to walk the length of the throne room. Other princes are crowned; she was carried. Yet the throne did not fall, the provinces did not rise, and the council that governed in her name governed in fear of the day she would come of age. That fear was the first homage paid her.

**Second argument: that the moon has phases, and returns.** Here I must speak of the [[evt:42969|war against Tunnge]], begun in 2198, when the Empress, being then in her twenty-eighth year, resolved to recover the province that Tunnge had long withheld. At the [[evt:42991|first battle of Yinnge]] in 2199 her arms prevailed. At the [[evt:42999|first battle of Shiniy]], in the year following, they did not. I will not pretend otherwise; panegyric that lies is mere flattery, and flattery dishonours its object. The fog came down; the general fell; the losses were very great, and the city mourned. But the moon that wanes is not destroyed. It returns.

**Third argument: that the moon returns brighter.** In 2202, at the [[evt:43007|second battle of Yinnge]], the armies of the Empress broke the enemy on the very ground where they had first engaged him, and the dead of Tunnge outnumbered ours by more than two to one.

**Fourth argument: that the star leads.** Here is the proof that admits no answer. In 2204 the Empress went out herself. At the [[evt:43020|battle of Ture]], with her own standard in the field and her own voice giving the orders, she met the King of Tunnge and drove him from it. Let those who think a woman cannot command explain how this was done.

**Fifth argument: that the moon governs the tides.** In 2205 her armies returned to Shiniy, where five years before they had been undone, and at the [[evt:43049|second battle of Shiniy]] they undid the undoers; the field that had swallowed our sons swallowed theirs. In 2206, at the [[evt:43056|battle of Thinirnge]], the last host of Tunnge was broken, and at the [[evt:43057|eighth siege of Togannge]] the enemy was shut within his walls. Thus the tide of war, which had flowed against us at Shiniy, was made to ebb at her command.

**Sixth argument: that star and moon together light the whole night.** By the [[evt:43058|treaty of Togannge]], which the Empress signed with her own hand in 2206, Tunnge surrendered not one province but many, with their towns and their river, and swore twenty years of peace. Our maps were redrawn. The night was lit from end to end.

**Seventh argument: that the star has a successor.** The ancients held that no star sets without another rising. And see: beside the throne stands the Prince [[per:42887|Pavbukhshifin]], a boy of thirteen, already grave, already learned, already loved. In him the light of his mother is continued.

To these seven arguments I add a corollary for the merchants, who care less for stars than for coin: that in all the years of her reign the [[cur:897|Rimi]] has not lost a grain of its weight.

I conclude, therefore, that her name was given by Providence, and that the One, who leads all time toward Judgement, has placed in Tallin a light by which the Empire may steer. *Which was to be shown*, as the geometers write at the foot of a proof; or, as the common people say more briefly, long live the Empress.
