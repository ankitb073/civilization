title: Write That the Vines Did Not Ripen
---
Nunadun woke because the lane was quiet.

At that hour there should have been the canal, the small sound it made going over the sill of the sluice, and the neighbour's daughter beating out the bedding, and somebody's cough. Instead there was a quiet with weight in it, like a hand laid flat on the roof. He lay and listened with an old man's patience, which is mostly the patience of the joints. Then he got up and went out into the court on bare feet, and stood there with his mouth open like a child.

The court was white. The lip of the water jar was white, and the beam of the well-sweep, and the reed mat over the door. Along the top of the wall it lay in a ridge as neat as a line of salt. When he lifted a foot, the snow had kept the print of it, every toe, and the cold came up through his heel into the bone he had broken as a boy.

He had seen snow in [[set:79|Talenonoy]] a handful of times in his life, always in the dead of winter, always gone before the sun was a hand high. This was the month of harvest.

— Grandfather. Your feet.

Yobot stood in the doorway behind him with the blanket from her bed around her shoulders. She was ten. She had never seen snow at all, and she was looking at it the way she looked at strangers, with her chin down.

— Get your sandals, he said. Then come and look at the vines.

* * *

The vines ran in rows along the canal bank below the house, as they had in his father's time and his father's father's. Nunadun had pruned them for sixty harvests. He knew them the way he knew the faces of his dead.

They stood now under the snow like guests caught out of doors in their festival clothes. The leaves, which should have been yellow and dropping by now, were still green, and each held a small white pillow. Under the leaves hung the grapes. All summer he had walked these rows waiting for them to soften and darken, for the bloom to come up on the skins like breath on bronze. It had not come. The bunches hung as hard and green as river pebbles, and on every one the snow sat in a little cap.

Yobot reached up and touched one and snatched her hand back.

— They're cold.

— Everything is cold.

He broke a grape from the bunch and put it in his mouth. It was so sour his eyes ran, and he stood in the snow of the harvest month with his eyes running and let the girl believe it was the sourness.

It had been a strange year. Early in the summer, he could not have said which day, a fine grey dust had come down on the rows, finer than milled barley, and the women had carried water up from the canal and washed the leaves one by one with rags. After the dust the sun went pale. Not hidden: pale. It crossed the sky like a lamp seen through linen, and at noon you could look straight at it for a long moment before you had to look away. [[dei:139|Nidikti]] had been thin-handed with the barley; it came in light, and in the market the grain-sellers wanted more [[cur:74|Tyetku]] of silver for a measure than they had wanted in the spring, and weighed it out with long faces, as if it grieved them.

At summer's end the wool-men had come down from the north with their bales. They said a mountain had burned up there, at the far edge of [[pol:119|Tonnga]], in the herders' country. Ashni, the herders called it in their own tongue. The sky over it had gone black at noon, they said, and stones had fallen out of the black, and the herders were driving their flocks down from the high pastures with cloths tied over their mouths. Nunadun had listened as one listens to travellers. What was a mountain in the herders' country to his vines?

— Is it Duemino? Yobot said. Is he angry with us?

— Snow is his, he said. That much I know.

She waited for the rest of it. He found there was no rest of it.

* * *

The priest came down the lane a little after the sun was up, if it could be called up. He was a narrow man of about the age Nunadun's son would have been, with the hem of his robe dark and heavy from the wet. Nunadun knew him by sight, as the whole canal did: Nguttun, the priest of the storm god's house on the hill. He had been in four houses already. You could tell by his feet.

He stopped at the gate and waited to be asked in, though nobody in the lane would have refused him.

— You're the oldest on this stretch of the canal, the priest said.

— The dyer is older.

— I've been to the dyer. He says he has never seen snow at harvest. He says his father never spoke of it either. I'm asking the old men. What about you?

Nunadun looked past him at the rows.

— Not at harvest. Not lying on the ground like this, in daylight. Not once.

The priest nodded, the nod of a man who has heard the same answer four times and has walked through snow to hear it a fifth. He did not write anything. He had no clay with him. He seemed to be keeping it in his head.

— You have the tablets up there, Nunadun said. Has it happened before?

— I don't know.

— You don't know?

— They're many, and they're kept in order, and nobody has read them all. I'll look tonight.

Yobot had come up behind her grandfather and was staring at the priest's wet feet. Nunadun put a hand on her head.

Then he said what he had not meant to say.

— In the market they're saying it's the king.

The priest's face did not change.

— Who says?

— The market. A king builds a fine new house for [[dei:140|Winba]], and two years later the earth gives this. They say the gods remember how a man comes to a throne.

— The market says the price of barley, Nguttun said. Let it say that. And be careful what you say in the snow, grandfather. It carries.

They stood for a moment with the vines dripping between them.

— We should have known by the birds, Nunadun said. In the spring.

— Everyone is saying that now.

The priest turned to go, and Nunadun found his hand had taken hold of the man's sleeve, the way Yobot took hold of his own.

— Will you write it down? This year?

— That's why I'm walking about in it.

— Then write that the vines did not ripen.

Nguttun looked at him.

— Write it, Nunadun said. So whoever has these rows after me knows. After her. Not the hands. Not that we were idle, or mean with the offerings. Write that it was the year.

Among the [[mov:21|Kunelmu]] a good harvest was the gods' favour, plain to every eye, and a bad one was the other thing, plain to every neighbour. The priest knew it as well as he did. He looked at the green bunches under their caps of snow.

— I'll write it, he said.

* * *

The tablets of the storm god's house were kept in a long room behind the shrine, on shelves of old dark wood, in the order in which they had been handed down. The truth was kept by those who had been ordained to keep it, and passed on in order. Nguttun had been taught that when he took the robe, at twenty, and had believed it since in the comfortable way one believes a thing nobody asks one to test.

He tested it now, with a lamp of olive oil and the cold coming in under the door.

He did not know what he was looking for. He went to the sun, to the darkened sun, and so to the long series called When the Moon, the omens of the kings of former days, which for all its name had as much sun in it as moon. His fingers were numb. He held each tablet close to the flame and read it with his lips.

It was on the twenty-fourth tablet, in a hand he knew from a hundred copies: Batdeden's, who had checked it for the great king Dyeyshamtumin fifty years before, and who had died the year before Nguttun was ordained. Of its twenty-seven omens it was the twenty-fourth. He read it twice.

"If the sun is darkened at noon in the month of Tyenmom and stars are seen: the sun will be darkened at noon. It is the omen of the year when the mountain burned and the sun was dark, in the days of Odet."

He sat back.

Odet. The queen who had come to the throne as a child, while Tibuwanan held the seal; it said so in the omen just above. Two hundred and eleven years. A mountain had burned and the sun had been dark, and someone had seen it, and someone had pressed it into clay, and someone had copied the clay, and copied the copy, and here it was under his numb fingers in the storm god's house, and not one old man on the canal had heard of it.

And that was all it said. He read it a third time to be sure. Not whether snow fell. Not the barley. Not the vines, or what a measure of grain had cost, or what the old men of Odet's day had said when they stood in their courts with their mouths open. The sign and the thing it foretold were the same: the sun will be darkened, if the sun is darkened. He almost laughed. Poor Batdeden. Poor whoever had come before Batdeden. They had had nothing to set beside the dark sun except the dark sun. Everything else about that year had gone where the old men go.

He thought of Nunadun's hand on his sleeve.

The clay in the trough was stiff with cold. He worked it by the brazier until it would take a mark, flattened it, squared it with the edge of his palm, cut a reed. Then he wrote the heading as he had been taught.

"Record kept at the temple of [[dei:136|Duemino]] in [[set:79|Talenonoy]], in the 13th year of [[per:5701|Nuetterten]]."

He could have written: as in the days of Odet. He had it there in front of him. But he did not know that it had been as in the days of Odet. The old tablet did not say. What he knew he had been told on the canal, by men with snow on their feet.

"Snow fell in the month of harvest."

"The vines did not ripen."

He held the reed over the clay for a long time before the next. It seemed to him the truest thing he had, and the most useful, and the one that someone would need, two hundred years on, standing in a white court with their mouth open: that the living had not remembered, and the clay had to.

"The old men had never seen its like."

Then, because the whole canal had said it to him, he wrote about the birds; and he set the tablet by the brazier to dry, and went to bed, and lay a long time listening to the snow slide off the roof.

* * *

By morning it was going. It came down off the eaves in long ropes of water, and the lanes ran with it, grey and quick, and the canal had a voice again. Nguttun went down with the hem of his robe tied up.

They were all out in the rows: the old man, the girl, a woman who must be the girl's mother. They had baskets. They were cutting the green bunches with bronze knives and laying them in the baskets as carefully as if they had been ripe.

Nunadun straightened when he saw him, one hand in the small of his back.

— Well?

— It has happened before, Nguttun said. In the days of Odet. A mountain burned and the sun was dark. It's on a tablet.

The old man was quiet. Water ran off the leaves around them in a hundred small voices.

— And after?

— It doesn't say after. It doesn't say anything after. She reigned a long time.

Nunadun considered this as he would have considered a stranger's vine: its age, the way it was trained, whether it had been looked after.

— Did you write mine?

— I wrote yours.

The old man nodded and bent to the rows again.

— What will you do with them? Nguttun said. They're no good.

— Press them. They make something. Sour. You can cook with sour. And it's the month of harvest. In the month of harvest, you harvest.

Yobot had been working along the row toward them. She held up a bunch to the priest, green and wet and cold, the last of the snow still caught in the stalks.

— For Duemino, she said. So he can see.

— Yobot, said her grandfather.

But the priest had already taken it. He thanked her, and meant it, and carried it up the hill to the god in both hands, the way you carry a thing that will not keep.
