title: Moving the Dead: The Tonnga–Nonenga War in Two Palace Chronicles
---
Few eighth-century wars are as well served by their sources as the [[evt:3353|Tonnga–Nonenga War]] (AY 715–721). We have the account of Numalunen, palace scribe of [[pol:119|Tonnga]], set down in AY 724 (PAL·CH·00519); the account of Bipupnan, palace scribe of [[pol:141|Nonenga]], set down in AY 729 (PAL·CH·00520); and a victory stele raised near Nuwkennga in AY 717. Against them stands an unusually firm documentary consensus. My argument is that the two scribes disagree only where disagreement was cheap. Neither could deny four battles and four Tonnga victories, so neither does. What they contest is blame, the butcher's bill and the meaning of the peace, and each contests them in the direction his court required.

## What both concede

The record counts four battles in the districts of [[reg:7|Tunnge]]: the [[evt:3368|Battle of Nuwkennga (AY 716)]], the [[evt:3383|Battle of Nuwkennga (AY 718)]], the [[evt:3403|Battle of Nuwkennga (AY 720)]] and the [[evt:3410|Battle of Nuwkennga (AY 721)]]. Tonnga won all four, and no commander fell. Both chronicles agree. Bipupnan's excuses hardly vary (a great storm three times, treachery once), and neither does the result: the army "fell back to the walls", "could not hold the field" or "gave ground". A defeated court could rename defeat; it could not abolish it.

## Who began it

The record holds that Tonnga began the war, and that its aim was tribute. Bipupnan agrees: "there came word that Agat of Tonnga had gathered an army against Nonenga." Numalunen inverts it: Nonenga's king, "swollen with pride, coveted the lands of Tonnga." A war for tribute is a demand; a war against a covetous neighbour is a duty. Numalunen's closing line, "the land which they had stolen was returned", turns a cession into a restitution. (Nonenga, first recorded in AY 714, was then a kingdom barely a year old; the picture of an upstart menacing an empire would have convinced few people near the frontier.)

## The butcher's bill

Here the record is hard on the vanquished. It gives Tonnga's dead as 366 and Nonenga's as 1,289: the losing side lost three and a half men for every one it killed. Numalunen gives Tonnga 136 dead and Nonenga 3,723. His total, 3,859, is more than twice the record's 1,655; he enlarged a slaughter that had gone his way. Bipupnan does the reverse with more restraint: 703 Tonnga dead against 508 of his own, a total about a quarter short of the record's. He did not invent a slaughter; he moved the dead across the line.

The two scribes cut their own dead alike. Bipupnan declares his side's at a little under two-fifths of the record's figure (508 is 39 per cent of 1,289). Numalunen does the same: 136 is 37 per cent of 366, and for the actions of AY 716 and 718 he admits 27 and 19 dead where the record has 72 and 51. I would not build a law on two cases, but it suggests that a court, defeated or victorious, could cut its own losses by rather more than half without fear of contradiction from veterans, and the enemy's dead could be multiplied freely. Even so, each man is the nearer of the two on his own side's losses, because his rival's inflation strays further still. The stele shows the same habit in a cruder medium: for the first battle it claims a round seven hundred enemy dead, where Numalunen gives 757 and the record 262. (Numalunen's own figure for the Nonenga host that day, 3,323 men, is likewise more than double the record's 1,548.)

## The peace

The [[evt:3411|Treaty of Nuwkennga (AY 721)]] gave Tonnga eight districts. Numalunen claims thirteen. Bipupnan concedes nothing at all ("Nothing of worth was lost"), and he presents the outcome as a draw. Neither names the tribute the war was fought for; Numalunen's "envoys with gifts and pleas" is as near as either comes. Distance in time explains little, since both men wrote within a decade; the courts explain more. Numalunen dated his work by the thirtieth year of Agat, who had commanded at three of the four battles. Bipupnan dated his by the sixteenth year of the king who had agreed to the terms. Each wrote for a living patron whose reputation was in the text.

The lesson for editors is modest. Where rival chronicles agree, as these do on the battles, they are probably right. Where they disagree, the record usually lies between them, but rarely in the middle.
