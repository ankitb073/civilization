title: Moving the Dead: The Tonnga–Nonenga War in Two Palace Chronicles
---
Few eighth-century wars are as well served by their sources as the [[evt:3353|Tonnga–Nonenga War]] (AY 715–721). We have the account of Numalunen, palace scribe of [[pol:119|Tonnga]], set down in AY 724 (PAL·CH·00519); the account of Bipupnan, palace scribe of [[pol:141|Nonenga]], set down in AY 729 (PAL·CH·00520); and a victory stele raised near Nuwkennga in AY 717. Against them stands an unusually firm documentary consensus. My argument is that the two scribes disagree only where disagreement was cheap. Neither could deny four battles and four Tonnga victories, so neither does. What they contest is blame, the butcher's bill and the meaning of the peace, and each contests them in the direction his court required.

## What both concede

The record counts four battles in the districts of [[reg:7|Tunnge]]: the [[evt:3368|Battle of Nuwkennga (AY 716)]], the [[evt:3383|Battle of Nuwkennga (AY 718)]], the [[evt:3403|Battle of Nuwkennga (AY 720)]] and the [[evt:3410|Battle of Nuwkennga (AY 721)]]. Tonnga won all four, and no commander fell. Both chronicles agree. Bipupnan's excuses hardly vary (a great storm three times, treachery once), and neither does the result: the army "fell back to the walls", "could not hold the field" or "gave ground". A defeated court could rename defeat; it could not abolish it.

## Who began it

The record holds that Tonnga began the war, and that its aim was tribute. Bipupnan agrees: "there came word that Agat of Tonnga had gathered an army against Nonenga." Numalunen inverts it: Nonenga's king, "swollen with pride, coveted the lands of Tonnga." A war for tribute is a demand; a war against a covetous neighbour is a duty. Numalunen's closing line, "the land which they had stolen was returned", turns a cession into a restitution. (Nonenga, first recorded in AY 714, was then a kingdom barely a year old; the picture of an upstart menacing an empire would have convinced few people near the frontier.)

## The butcher's bill

Here the record is awkward for the victor. It gives Tonnga's dead as 2,257 and Nonenga's as 865: the winning side lost more than two and a half men for every one it killed. Numalunen gives Tonnga 375 dead and Nonenga 2,498. His total, 2,873, lies within eight per cent of the record's 3,122. He did not invent a slaughter; he moved the dead across the line. Bipupnan does the reverse with less restraint: 4,334 Tonnga dead against 341 of his own, a total half again as large as the record's.

The two scribes did not cut their own dead alike. Bipupnan declares his side's at a little under two-fifths of the record's figure (341 is 39 per cent of 865). Numalunen goes much further: 375 is 17 per cent of 2,257, and for the small actions of AY 716 and 718 he admits 3 and 5 dead where the record has 795 and 474. I would not build a law on two cases, but it suggests that a defeated court could cut its own losses by rather more than half without fear of contradiction from veterans, a victorious one could all but erase them, and the enemy's dead could be multiplied freely. Even so, each man is the nearer of the two on his own side's losses, because his rival's inflation strays further still. The stele shows the same habit in a cruder medium: for the first battle it claims a round hundred enemy dead, where Numalunen gives 29 and the record ten. (Numalunen's own figure for the Nonenga host that day, 73 men, shows how small the engagement was.)

## The peace

The [[evt:3411|Treaty of Nuwkennga (AY 721)]] gave Tonnga eight districts. Numalunen claims seventeen. Bipupnan concedes nothing at all ("Nothing of worth was lost"), and he presents the outcome as a draw. Neither names the tribute the war was fought for; Numalunen's "envoys with gifts and pleas" is as near as either comes. Distance in time explains little, since both men wrote within a decade; the courts explain more. Numalunen dated his work by the thirtieth year of Agat, who had commanded at three of the four battles. Bipupnan dated his by the sixteenth year of the king who had agreed to the terms. Each wrote for a living patron whose reputation was in the text.

The lesson for editors is modest. Where rival chronicles agree, as these do on the battles, they are probably right. Where they disagree, the record usually lies between them, but rarely in the middle.
