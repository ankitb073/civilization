title: Moving the Dead: The Tonnga–Nonenga War in Two Palace Chronicles
---
Few eighth-century wars are as well served by their sources as the [[evt:3353|Tonnga–Nonenga War]] (AY 715–721). We have the account of Numalunen, palace scribe of [[pol:119|Tonnga]], set down in AY 724 (PAL·CH·00519); the account of Bipupnan, palace scribe of [[pol:141|Nonenga]], set down in AY 729 (PAL·CH·00520); and a victory stele raised near the ford in AY 717. Against them stands an unusually firm documentary consensus. My argument is that the two scribes disagree only where disagreement was cheap. Neither could deny four battles and four Tonnga victories, so neither does. What they contest is blame, the butcher's bill and the meaning of the peace, and each contests them in the direction his court required.

## What both concede

The record counts four battles in the districts of [[reg:7|Tunnge]]: the [[evt:3368|Battle of Nuwkennga (AY 716)]], the [[evt:3383|Battle of Nuwkennga (AY 718)]], the [[evt:3403|Battle of Nuwkennga (AY 720)]] and the [[evt:3410|Battle of Nuwkennga (AY 721)]]. Tonnga won all four, and no commander fell. Both chronicles agree. Bipupnan's excuses change from entry to entry ("because the omens had been neglected", "by treachery", "through the cowardice of hired men"), but the result does not: the army "could not hold the field" or "gave ground". A defeated court could rename defeat; it could not abolish it.

## Who began it

The record holds that Tonnga began the war, and that its aim was tribute. Bipupnan agrees: "there came word that Agat of Tonnga had gathered an army against Nonenga." Numalunen inverts it: Nonenga's king, "swollen with pride, coveted the lands of Tonnga." A war for tribute is a demand; a war against a covetous neighbour is a duty. Numalunen's closing line, "the land which they had stolen was returned", turns a cession into a restitution. (Nonenga, first recorded in AY 714, was then a kingdom barely a year old; the picture of an upstart menacing an empire would have convinced few people near the frontier.)

## The butcher's bill

Here the record is awkward for the victor. It gives Tonnga's dead as 2,257 and Nonenga's as 865: the winning side lost more than two and a half men for every one it killed. Numalunen gives Tonnga 838 dead and Nonenga 2,498. His total, 3,336, lies within seven per cent of the record's 3,122. He did not invent a slaughter; he moved the dead across the line. Bipupnan does the reverse with less restraint: 4,334 Tonnga dead against 341 of his own, a total half again as large as the record's.

One regularity deserves notice. Each scribe declares his own side's dead at a little under two-fifths of the record's figure (838 is 37 per cent of 2,257; 341 is 39 per cent of 865). I would not build a law on two cases, but it suggests that a palace scribe could cut his own losses by rather more than half without fear of contradiction from veterans, while the enemy's dead could be multiplied freely. Because both men minimised more timidly than they inflated, each is the nearer of the two on his own side's losses. The stele shows the same habit in a cruder medium: for the first battle it claims a round hundred enemy dead, where Numalunen gives 29 and the record ten. (Numalunen's own figure for the Nonenga host that day, 73 men "and chariots beyond counting", shows how small the engagement was.)

## The peace

The [[evt:3411|Treaty of Nuwkennga (AY 721)]] gave Tonnga eight districts. Numalunen claims thirteen. Bipupnan admits to nothing worth the name: "some villages of no account were given up, which had always been poor", and he presents the outcome as a draw. Neither names the tribute the war was fought for; Numalunen's "envoys with gifts and pleas" is as near as either comes. Distance in time explains little, since both men wrote within a decade; the courts explain more. Numalunen dated his work by the thirtieth year of Agat, who had commanded at three of the four battles. Bipupnan dated his by the sixteenth year of the king who had agreed to the terms. Each wrote for a living patron whose reputation was in the text.

The lesson for editors is modest. Where rival chronicles agree, as these do on the battles, they are probably right. Where they disagree, the record usually lies between them, but rarely in the middle.
