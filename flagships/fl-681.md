At Khuele in AY 4717, says a history of Tushi, "outnumbered three to one, our men could not hold the field." The ratio is about right, and the wrong way round. The record gives [[per:44960|Amin]], general of [[pol:1772|Tushi]], 31,309 men at the [[evt:45614|Battle of Khuele (AY 4717)]] against Bene's 9,453. He lost 5,800; Bene lost 1,597. The historian Yei Yuf, writing in 4763, reversed the odds, cut Tushi's dead to 1,199 and left the general's name out (PAL·CH·07999).

That sentence holds the problem of Amin's life. He led Tushi's armies in thirteen battles between 4713 and 4729; in the kingdom's whole record only one general, Aüyuf, led more. He won five and lost eight. Five surviving documents name him, a muster roll and four chronicles, and every chronicle that names him does so only on days its own side won.

## A kingdom at its height

The record gives Amin a birth year, 4679, and a people, the Nishmam. It names no parents, birthplace, wife or children, and says nothing of him for thirty-four years. They were good years for Tushi. It took Turamtu in the [[evt:45438|Tushi–Turamtu War]] of 4698–4701 and began issuing paper money, the [[cur:1062|Ag]], in 4697. The survey at the end of the 4700s found the kingdom at the largest it would ever be: 188 districts, 7,749,598 people and 90,805 men under arms, ruled from [[set:27|Tuzhi]] by [[per:44859|Nislifaya II]], king since 4708, under the [[mov:69|Kievi]] faith.

In 4711 Tushi opened the [[evt:45571|Tushi–Bene War]] to take land in Baan from [[pol:1850|Bene]]. Bene's king, [[per:44698|Buthniluduen]] the Usurper, had written to Nislifaya that year: "You have not sent the gifts that were promised." (PAL·LT·01618). The first battle, at Nyethatse in 4712, was lost and its general, Izhil Lizil, killed. The record makes Amin general the next year.

His first muster survives, drawn up by the scribe Baallu "under the command of Amin" (PAL·MU·01538): eight towns, 54,397 men, 21,290 of them gunners from the capital. Read today, it is an inventory of what Tushi would lose. Turamtu, annexed twelve years before, sent 5,090 musketeers. Auve and Niazhi would be ceded to Wemu in 4729; Fuiyu would lend its name to a breakaway kingdom in 4739, and Nuidaduth would be capital of Tushi's successor. At the [[evt:45579|Battle of Nirun (AY 4713)]] this army met Buthniluduen himself, aged 54, with 19,942 men, and beat him, losing 1,187 men to Bene's 4,269.

## Two fronts

In 4714 he won again near Bene's capital, at the [[evt:45585|Battle of Khuele (AY 4714)]], though he lost nearly as many as he killed, 1,706 to 1,811. That year the [[pol:1785|Tallin]] empire opened the [[evt:45584|Tallin–Tushi War]], which the record traces to the defeat at Nyethatse. Against Bene he always had the larger army, from nearly half again as many men to nearly five times as many. Against the empires he faced four and a half to seven times his numbers.

At the [[evt:45596|Battle of Viradhuthpi (AY 4715)]] his 38,952 men met [[per:44972|Nguthngakathren]]'s 10,622, and he lost 12,699, a third of his army, against 1,517: more than twice what his five victories cost together. The same year he defended Munu against [[per:44880|Shiluthin]]'s 237,152; at the [[evt:45597|Battle of Munu (AY 4715)]] he lost 6,275 men to Tallin's 2,715. In 4716 he won the [[evt:45601|Battle of Khuele (AY 4716)]], losing 1,614 men to 2,054, and lost the [[evt:45603|Battle of Munu (AY 4716)]], 38,364 men against 238,069, with 11,216 of his killed against 2,947. In 4717 another general, Iümin, lost to Shiluthin at Taalinnuy. The Treaty of Taalinnuy gave Tallin 36 districts, Munu among their towns, and an indemnity of 78,656 kilograms of silver.

Nine years later, at Nivuflimnu, one of the ceded towns, Izhil Imin, "formerly a subject of" Tushi, sued for his father's 80 acres in Tunnge. Counsel for the Crown replied: "The fields were found empty and were given to settlers." He might farm them as the crown's tenant, for 61.8 Nivbe a year (PAL·LS·00759).

Amin lost again at the [[evt:45648|Battle of Nyethatse (AY 4719)]] to [[per:44968|Müniyniyin]], 3,125 dead against 931, then won on the same ground in the [[evt:45680|Battle of Nyethatse (AY 4722)]] and the [[evt:45688|Battle of Nyethatse (AY 4723)]], where, at 44, he lost 282 men to Bene's 1,363. The Treaty of Nyethatse left the border where it was. Twelve years of war had cost Tushi 35,464 dead, 27,357 of them in Amin's eight battles, and Bene 20,133, for nothing. In the capital the papers' price of grain rose from 2.32 Ag the measure in 4717 to 3.33 in 4729 (PAL·NP·00296), in notes losing their silver.

## Linanuniemkhu

The record traces the [[evt:45710|Third Wemu–Tushi War]] partly to the second defeat at Munu. In 4726 [[pol:1769|Wemu]] came for Ayshi, Tuzhi's own region. Amin fought all three of its battles, all at Linanuniemkhu. In the [[evt:45717|Ninth Battle of Linanuniemkhu]] in 4727 he faced Wemu's emperor, [[per:44779|Luexaji]] the Old, in person: 195,427 men against 42,966. Tushi lost 4,887, Wemu 631. In the [[evt:45730|Tenth Battle of Linanuniemkhu]] [[per:45003|Xyimaf]], fourteen years his junior, brought 154,387 against 31,781 and killed 8,693 for 632; in the [[evt:45733|Eleventh Battle of Linanuniemkhu]] in 4729, with 158,751 against 29,164, he killed 7,308 for 2,216.

The Treaty of Linanuniemkhu took 43 districts, with Linanuniemkhu, Myixim and two towns of the Nirun roll, Auve and Niazhi, and an indemnity of 69,987 kilograms of silver. On the record's map every landward neighbour of Tuzhi was now Wemu's: the capital was left an enclave on its own coast. Tushi's historian Mazhi Yuf, writing in 4731, describes only the first battle, lost "through the blunders of the general staff", and closes: "at the urging of the priests, the king accepted the pleas of Vemu, and the Treaty of Linanuniemkhu was sworn. The border was settled." (PAL·CH·08024). In 4734 a court at Linanuniemkhu heard I Faya, whose father's 215 acres in Ayshi had gone to Wemu. The Crown answered: "The plaintiff did not swear allegiance within the year." (PAL·LS·00761).

## Who says his name

Fiamui of Wemu, whose book survives damaged by fire at the edges (PAL·CH·08023), gives Amin 113,480 men in 4727, 83,939 in 4728 and 77,027 in 4729: each the recorded army multiplied by the same factor, about 2.64. Shithshith of Tallin doubles his armies at Munu as regularly (PAL·CH·08003). Baduen of Bene names him three times, each on a Bene victory, and passes over four of his five wins (PAL·CH·08000). Yei Yuf names him in all five of Tushi's victories and in none of the three defeats, which he lays to "a night of rain that bogged the guns" and, twice, to being outnumbered three to one. He wrote in Tuzhi in 4763, nineteen years after the kingdom dissolved, when the record shows Wemu holding the city. Mazhi Yuf never names him. The court annals give Nirun to the gods, "the gods gave the victory" (PAL·AN·00804), and after Khuele in 4717 and again after Linanuniemkhu in 4729 the capital's papers printed the same sentence: "The ministry denies reports of a rout." (PAL·NP·00263).

## Paper, not ash

After 4729 the record is silent about Amin. He died a natural death in 4739, at 60, the year Fuiyu broke away and the [[evt:45803|Second Tushi Civil War]] began. He did not see the collapse of 4744, among whose causes the record lists both battles of Munu, the Tenth Battle of Linanuniemkhu and the treaty that followed it. By the end of the 4730s the survey counted 82 districts, 2,820,255 people and an army of 29,521.

An archaeologist who looks for him in the ground will not find him. The excavation reports for the six towns near which he fought show no destruction layer from his years. Nirun "seems never to have been sacked" (PAL·EX·00175); Linanuniemkhu has a single burnt layer, from 3229 (PAL·EX·00286); at Munu, Viradhuthpi, Khuele and Nyethatse the destruction layers belong to other centuries (PAL·EX·00220, PAL·EX·00099, PAL·EX·00071, PAL·EX·00255). The record holds no siege in his three wars: the towns that changed hands in them did so by treaty, not by fire. The only burning in his story is at the edges of an enemy's book.

What survives is paper. The roll of Nirun counts the gunners of Tuzhi and the musketeers of Fuiyu without naming one of them; the conquerors' courts name the farmers only when they come asking for their fields. Amin's name lies between the two, kept chiefly by the enemies who beat him. They, at least, never pretended he was not there.
