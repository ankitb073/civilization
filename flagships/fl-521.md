*Here begins the confession of Tuhim, written in his own hand at Tsivintuha, for those who ask why he turned from the altars of his youth.*

I. You ask me, friends and enemies alike, why a man past fifty years, who was raised at the altars of [[mov:57|I'ishiu]] and owes to its ordained teachers every letter he can write, should turn his back upon them and gather a company of his own. You ask it kindly or in anger, according to your nature. I will answer both kinds alike, for the question is the same whatever the tone.

II. I was taught, as you were, that all things turn upon a great wheel, and that what ends will begin again. I was taught that there is one divine power, and that the old gods are its servants or its shadows; this I believe still, and have not changed by a hair. I was taught that the good life is compassion and release from suffering. I was taught that truth is kept by an ordained priesthood and passed down in order. I loved these teachings. I do not write to mock them. A man who mocks the house he grew up in has learned nothing in it.

III. But consider what I saw in my thirtieth year. The army of [[pol:1098|Mane]] went down against the city of Ene, and at the [[evt:25559|Battle of Ene]] it prevailed; and after the battle came the [[evt:25560|Fourth Sack of Ene]]. Fourth, the heralds said, as a steward says the fourth jar. Two years later came the [[evt:25601|Fifth Sack of Ene]]. I walked through that city afterwards. I saw a woman sweeping ash from a threshold that her grandmother had swept before her, and her grandmother's grandmother. And I asked myself: if all things turn upon a wheel, then the sixth sack is already written, and the tenth, and the hundredth, and this woman's broom is only the spoke of a wheel that grinds her daughters as it ground her mothers. What is compassion worth, if suffering is a circle?

IV. I did not find the answer in the temple. I found it, if I found it, in the scroll of my own years. A man's life does not come round again; it runs from his birth to his death, and he answers at the end for what he did with it. If this is true of a man, why not of the world? I came to believe that time runs in a line, from its making to a final judgement, and that each sack of Ene is not a turn of the wheel but a deed, done once, to be accounted for once.

V. And if the world is to be judged, then what is asked of us is not only pity, but duty: order kept, sacrifice rightly made, each man doing what his station requires. I came to think, too, that prosperity justly gained is a sign of the divine favour, and not only a snare. When the [[evt:25602|Treaty of Ene]] brought into the treasury of Mane more silver than three hundred and seventy thousand [[cur:592|midnatl]], I did not say the silver was wicked. I asked whether it had been gained by duty. That is the question the judgement will ask.

VI. Last, and this was hardest: I could no longer believe that any priesthood owns the truth. I honour teachers; I was made by them. But truth is not a coin to be minted by one house and lent at interest to the rest. Let the teachers be honoured, and let no one own the truth.

VII. So, two years ago, I spoke these things aloud at [[set:13|Tsivintuha]], and some listened, and stayed. Those who stayed are called the [[mov:94|Udushmamash]]. That gathering others now call the [[evt:25759|Founding of the Udushmamash Faith]]; I call it the day I stopped being silent. Our message is not for one people only; it is for all who will hear.

VIII. I have broken no law of King [[per:25258|Lihmakh]], whom I pray for as I always have. I have pulled down no altar. I have only changed my mind, and said so. If that is a crime, let the judgement find it.

*[[per:25404|Tuhim]], in the year 3677 from the founding of Tsivintuha.*
