title: Raking Light
---
The carver liked the first hour after sunrise best. The light came in through the east door of the shed low and almost flat, and lay across the lid the way water lies across a floor, and in it every stroke he had cut stood up out of the stone with its own thin shadow. That was how you knew. A letter was deep enough when it held a shadow. Until then it was a scratch with ambitions.

He had been at the lid since the end of the mourning. It lay on two trestles under the reed roof, a slab as long as a tall man, and the words were pinned at one corner beneath a pebble, written on a strip of papyrus in a priest's quick hand, though he had not needed to look at them for days:

"Niestlish, king of Tomözel, son of Ipil Toa, dwells in this tomb."

One line. Down in the tombs, over the door of the first king, the same line had been cut a hundred and seventy-six years before: "Momafe, king of Tomözel, dwells in this tomb." The carver had gone down with a lamp to read it before he began, the way a man might look at his grandfather's hands before he takes up his grandfather's trade. The old cutter had been better at curves than he was, and worse at spacing. He had not had to fit in a father.

It was the year 2316 of the [[mov:25|Mietseyeshio]], though nothing on the lid would say so.

This summer the first hour had gone wrong. For some days the sun had come up red and climbed into a haze the colour of thin beer, and hung there all day like a silver [[cur:543|Nuesha]] at the bottom of a cistern, pale enough to stare at. The shadows had gone soft at their edges. He could no longer tell by looking whether a stroke was finished. He had taken to wetting the stone with a rag and reading it as it dried, which was slow, and to running his thumb down each stroke, which was slower.

The boy came most mornings now. One of his mother's men walked him down from the palace and stayed outside to talk with the water-carrier, and the boy sat on an upturned basket inside the door with his knees under his chin. He was twelve. Since the end of the winter he had been king of [[pol:994|Tomözel]], and his mother, Asatlitluee, held the seal, and between those two things there seemed to be very little left over for him to do. His name was [[per:25046|Bozinfoyatl]]. It meant victory reed. His father's had meant hero star. They were names given to babies, the carver thought, which the babies then had to grow into in public.

— You've got to the end, the boy said.

— Nearly. The last word wants another pass.

— Tomb.

— Tomb.

The boy looked along the line for a while.

— It's short.

— It's the old form. The carver tipped his head towards the stair that went down into the dark. The same as the first king's, near enough.

— The rock at Yinishme says much more. He told me what it says.

The carver set down the mallet.

He had cut that rock himself, the summer after the [[evt:25316|Battle of Yinishme]], hanging in a rope cradle over the road with a lad below to send up water in a basket. Seven weeks. He could still find the place in his shoulder. "He killed 39,800 soldiers." He had cut the figures larger than the words, as he had been told to, so that they could be read from the road by a man on a horse.

— Did he? said the boy. Kill that many?

The carver considered the boy's face, which was his father's face with the edges not yet cut in.

— I'll tell you what the soldiers told me, he said. They stood guard under my ropes all that summer, and soldiers talk. They said the whole army Yisamashtsi brought to Yinishme wasn't thirteen thousand men. They said they counted the enemy dead afterwards, and it came to five thousand and a hundred and some, and their general among them. And of ours, nearly fourteen hundred, which isn't on the rock anywhere. And it was the general Shove who stood on the field that day. Your father came after, to swear the [[evt:25317|Treaty of Yinishme]].

— Then why did you cut thirty-nine thousand?

— Because it was written on the papyrus they gave me.

— That isn't a reason.

— It's a carver's reason. He picked a chip off the lid and turned it over in his fingers. A stone costs. Every stroke on it is a morning out of somebody's life. Nobody pays for a small number. A small number on a stone looks like an apology.

The boy took this in slowly, as if it were food that might be hot.

— He was at [[evt:25291|Tunavükhini]] himself, he said at last. When I was seven. He was in the field. He told me about it.

— He was. Everybody knows he was.

— That isn't on any rock.

— No, said the carver. Nobody ever asked me to put it on one.

* * *

It began a little before noon, and at first it looked like weather. The haze in the east thickened and went brown, the brown of the river after rain, and came on without any wind the carver could feel; and the light in the shed did something he had never seen light do. It did not go from one side, as it goes at evening. It went from everywhere at once, as if someone were lowering a lid, very slowly, over the whole city.

In the yard the water-carrier said something sharp, and the boy's man came to the door.

— Lord. We should go up.

— In a moment, said the boy, and did not move.

The dogs started first, all over [[set:3|Eshueethie]], and then the women up on the roofs, and then at the temple of Nieshashta that the dead king had built for her someone began beating the bronze, the slow stroke and then the quick three, which was the stroke for fire. But there was no fire. There was only the dark coming down, brown, then grey, then nearly black, at noon, in summer, and with it a smell like a struck flint; and then on the backs of the carver's hands a touch so light he would not have felt it if he had not been looking at his hands.

He lit the lamp, because that was what you did. The flame stood up small and straight and showed the air in front of it full of something falling, slow as flour shaken through a sieve.

— Is it the sky? said the boy. His voice had gone up into a child's again. Is the sky coming down?

— It's dust, said the carver, who did not know. Ash. Something's burning somewhere.

— Everything?

— Not here. Look. Nothing here is burning.

The boy's man had him by the arm now, and the boy let himself be held but would not be taken. The carver understood. A king could not be seen running from a stoneyard, and there was nowhere to run that was not under the same sky.

So they stood and watched the grey come down on the lid. It came down on everything, on the trestles and the papyrus and the boy's hair, on the water in the jar, where it made a skin, and on the flat of the stone it lay like the bloom on a plum.

The boy leaned over and blew along the stone, the way you blow along a hearth to wake it. The grey went off the flat in a soft cloud. It stayed in the cuts.

And there was the line, all of it at once, grey on pale, every stroke plain as notches on a tally-stick, and not a shadow anywhere. The carver had never seen his own work like that. For weeks he had chased it a morning at a time, a letter's worth of shadow at a time. Now the mountain, or whatever it was, had read it out for him, every stroke exactly as deep as he had made it.

— Show me, said the boy. With your thumb. The way you do.

The carver took the boy's hand. It was only afterwards that he thought whose hand it was. He laid the forefinger in the first stroke of the first letter and drew it along, and the boy read with his eyes shut. Niestlish. King. Tomözel. The finger slowed near the end.

— Son of Ipil Toa, said the boy. That's my grandfather.

— It is.

— Grandfather was never king. Grandmother was the queen.

— This one names the father, said the carver. Somebody wanted him there. I don't ask.

— His name means Seven Rain. He used to say that was why it rained whenever he went out to look at anything. He used to say it was his fault. The boy opened his eyes and looked up, at the black where the sky should have been. Since the sun went red they've been saying in the market it's mine. Because a boy is king. They say that's why.

The carver did not answer straight away. He had a grandson not much younger, who had believed all that spring that if he trod on the cracks between the paving stones of the lower town his mother would die.

— Your grandfather didn't make it rain, he said.

— I know that.

— Then you know this.

The boy rubbed his thumb against his forefinger and looked at the grey on them in the lamplight.

— It's only ash, he said, trying the words, as you test a plank before you put your weight on it.

* * *

His mother came for him herself. The carver heard her before he saw her, not running, but walking fast, with a lamp carried in front of her and two more behind. She stopped in the doorway. Asatlitluee was forty-two that year, and a widow since the end of the winter, and in that light she looked like any woman of the lower town who has gone through the streets in the dark looking for her child.

— There you are, she said.

Then she saw the lid, and came and stood over it, and the carver stepped back. She did not touch the letters. She looked for a long time at her husband's name, written in grey.

— Is it finished?

— The last word, lady. One more pass.

— Don't cut in this.

— No, lady.

The boy took her hand.

— It's only ash, Mother. Something is burning somewhere.

— The priests are in the hall, she said. They are waiting to tell us what it means. They have been arguing since it began.

— It means something is burning somewhere.

She looked down at him, and the carver could not see her face well, only the lamp in her eyes.

— Then you can tell them so, she said. Slowly. They like a thing said slowly.

And she took him out into the dark.

* * *

Word came up from Baan some days later, with a rider whose horse was grey to the belly. A mountain had burned out there in the hill country, the rider said, a mountain called Latlmis, a name in the hill people's tongue that nobody in the city could make mean anything. The [[evt:25354|Eruption of Mount Latlmis]] had buried the villages under it, he said. He did not know how many. Nobody asked him to put a number on it.

The haze stayed. It stayed all that summer and into the autumn, a sun you could look straight at, red mornings and red evenings, and in the fields below the city the grain stood green when it should have stood gold, and then stood grey-green, and was cut in the end anyway, thin, by people who had stopped expecting much of it. The cold came early. Bread cost more by the end of the year than at the beginning, and the old men said they had never known a summer like it. It was the first summer of the [[cli:144|Volcanic Winter of Mount Latlmis]], and it lasted, as the record has it, exactly as long as his mother held the seal.

The carver finished the last word on a morning with almost no shadow, by thumb.

He did not wash the letters out. He told himself it was because the rag would only push the grit deeper, which was true, and because the boy had blown on them, which was also true. In forty years he had cut a great many numbers into stone, and he could not have sworn to one of them. This lid had none at all.

They lowered it in the autumn, in a cold that had no business being in the autumn. The ash of Latlmis was still in the king's name, grey in every stroke, exactly as deep as he had cut it, and not a hair deeper.
