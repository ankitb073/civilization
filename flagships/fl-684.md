## A veiled head

The best likeness of [[per:45832|Lukhukyenmen]] is a corroded silver coin, struck at [[set:100|Khuele]] around AY 4838 and known from a hoard. This first issue of the [[cur:1095|Rarban]] (PAL·NU·00501) shows his "head", "veiled, right", with "a bull walking" on the reverse. It weighs 13.8 grams, about 92 per cent silver. No other surviving document shows his face, and little shows his life.

He was born in AY 4771, a man of the Kunelmu people, and his name means *king lord*. The record names no parent, wife or child, and no office before AY 4826, when he was elected chairman of [[pol:1850|Bene]] at fifty-five. Bene, a kingdom in name, had been a republic since 4734 and a one-party state since the revolution of 4780, when he was nine. Its documents still spoke of kings. The Bene chronicler of his war wrote "So the king went out to punish him", Bene's annalist that "Coin was struck in the name of the king", and his own edict speaks of "the sacrifices owed to the king".

He inherited two quarrels. His predecessor [[per:45731|Khülithun]] had fought the [[evt:46508|Holy War of Bene against Yimla]] (4815–4820) over the same borderland; it ended without a settlement, after a disaster at Rivirnath in 4816: 72,195 Bene troops lost 28,056 men to an army of 17,205. At home, Bene's state faith was [[mov:57|I'ishiu]], which holds that all things turn on a great wheel. Its rival, the Veshethmekh, began with a sermon of AY 2256 (PAL·SC·00120) that answers: "The wheel is a lie told to the comfortable." It called the new faith "the road and not the wheel". Bene's own yearbooks, counting faiths by share of districts, gave I'ishiu 49 per cent and Veshethmekh 43 in 4773 (PAL·YB·00042); by 4803 the order had reversed, 47 to 46 (PAL·YB·00043).

## Nine years of holy war

In AY 4828, two years into his term, the Khuele Courier announced: "The government has declared war on Yishamashki"; "Recruiting offices opened at dawn" (PAL·NP·00530). The [[evt:46627|Holy War of Bene against Yisamashtsi]] struck a wounded neighbour. Ane had sacked the [[pol:1952|Yisamashtsi]] capital, Namosash, in 4821 and taken twenty-six districts in 4822, and the record names that sack among the war's causes. Each side's chronicler says the other's ruler, "forgetting the oaths his fathers swore", started it. The record makes Bene the attacker.

The record places Lukhukyenmen at no battle; his generals wired their reports to Khuele. At Nyethatse in 4829, Nuluwrinin's 104,469 men beat 81,623 under Yisamashtsi's ruler, [[per:45688|Yueni Luisha]], born the same year as Lukhukyenmen. Yisamashtsi lost 23,668 men and Bene 3,868. Nuluwrinin wired "OUR LOSSES ABOUT 3,100" and put the enemy's at 41,200 (PAL·TG·00019). Yueni Luisha's own telegram, which has leaves missing, came nearer the truth: "OUR LOSSES ABOUT 20,000 STOP REQUEST RELIEF" (PAL·TG·00020).

Four of the five later battles were fought near Rivirnath, a Bene city since 4596, on fields the territorial record puts across the frontier. In 4830 the armies were nearly equal, 58,389 Bene to 60,938, and their losses almost identical, 5,333 to 5,323. Yisamashtsi held the field, and Nuluwrinin was killed. The Bene chronicle of 4849 (PAL·CH·08151) says instead that the army was "outnumbered three to one" and "withdrew in good order". Mawnun won there in 4831. In 4835 Nathninin lost there, 12,892 men to the enemy's 7,527. The Khuele Herald said only that the army had fallen back: "The censor has withheld further details" (PAL·NP·00553). In 4836, on the same ground, Nathninin beat Fisfamuevi, who had beaten him. In 4837 he won the last battle, near [[set:186|Tunavükhini]], though he lost more men than the beaten side, 2,862 to 2,493.

Over nine years Bene lost 29,080 men and Yisamashtsi 48,430. The Bene account gives Nyethatse 789 Bene dead against 74,798; the Yisamashtsi account of 4839 (PAL·CH·08152) records only the two battles its side won.

## The terms at Tunavükhini

The [[evt:46718|Treaty of Tunavükhini]] (PAL·TR·02862) was signed in 4837 by Lukhukyenmen and Yueni Luisha, both sixty-six. Mugurlukhnen, a Bene envoy of twenty-four, drafted it. Yisamashtsi gave up four districts, and the two states renounced force for twenty years. Article III read: "Throughout the land of Yishamashki the worship of Gigishyu shall be permitted and its shrines protected; its adherents shall suffer no disability." Gigishyu is the Bene name for I'ishiu. The record calls this term a conversion, and the decade survey shows I'ishiu as Yisamashtsi's state faith by the end of the 4830s. Yisamashtsi's own yearbook of 4833 (PAL·YB·00108) had given it 8 per cent of the districts.

Both historians rewrote the peace. Duedyeyshan, for Bene, wrote that "The land which they had stolen was returned to Bene, 8 districts in all." The treaty says four. "Returned" is not pure invention: the territorial record shows Bene holding those districts from 4596 until 4699, when it ceded them to the Wemu Empire, from which Yisamashtsi descended. Iwshashmamshi, for Yisamashtsi, wrote that "the king accepted the pleas of Bene" and that "Nothing of worth was lost." The record counts the treaty among the causes of the secession at Tiaiosh that began a civil war in Yisamashtsi in 4838. In 4844 Yueni Luisha's state abandoned I'ishiu.

The victory also cost silver. Bene's treasury, 1,010,923 kg in the survey of the 4810s, was 404,543 by the end of the 4820s and 86,698 kg in debt by the end of the 4830s, when Yisamashtsi, the loser, stood 564,962 kg in credit.

## Silver and the edict

In 4838 Lukhukyenmen issued the only two documents that survive under his name. The first created the Rarban, the [[evt:46640|First Coinage of Bene]]. Since the 4570s Bene had used the [[cur:1000|Khürew]], the coin of an older kingdom of Bene, first issued in 4464 and valued in the record at 9.1 grams of silver. The Rarban carried 12.7. "Weighed silver shall no longer be accepted at the treasury," the edict ruled (PAL·DE·00822). The record does not say why an indebted state struck heavier coin. The gazette's bracketed standard, "[13 g of silver]", rounds the hoard coin's 12.7 grams, and his successor's edict gives the old coin the same 13.

The second edict (PAL·DE·00824) is brief:

> The followers of the Veshethmekhu refuse the sacrifices owed to the king. They shall not assemble, nor teach, nor keep their books. Their houses of prayer shall be closed. The Gigishyu alone shall be honoured.

The record's map of faiths shows what was at stake. When he took office, 56 of Bene's 120 districts were I'ishiu and 54 Veshethmekh. Three of the four districts won by treaty were Veshethmekh, and in 4838 Veshethmekh was the faith of 60 of 129 districts, I'ishiu of 56. A war fought in I'ishiu's name had strengthened its rival inside Bene. The founder of the Veshethmekh had preached "Mercy, and not sacrifice"; the edict condemned his followers for refusing sacrifice. Fifteen districts are recorded as turning to I'ishiu under the persecution, and by 4839 the count stood at 73 to 42. A year earlier he had demanded that his own faith's adherents abroad "suffer no disability". Now he shut the houses of prayer of the faith that held more of Bene's districts than his own.

## What he left

His term ended in 4840, and he died a natural death in 4842, at seventy-one. In 4844 [[per:45954|Luwrinuluen]], his successor, cut the Rarban's silver to 10.572 grams, "Because the treasury is empty by the malice of the enemy" (PAL·DE·00826). The record ties that debasement to the defeats at Rivirnath in 4816 and 4835. The debased coin survives in many specimens (PAL·NU·00502), the first issue only from a hoard, as one expects when good coin is hoarded and bad coin spent. Luwrinuluen also renewed the edict against the Veshethmekh. Bene collapsed in 4860. Of the five causes the record gives, three fall in Lukhukyenmen's chairmanship: the defeats at Rivirnath in 4830 and 4835, and his persecution of 4838. Nangash, lecturing in 4950, said the collapse "followed costly wars and two persecutions of a dissenting faith" (PAL·SP·00044).

In 4853 Mugurlukhnen, now forty, passed through Rivirnath, still a Bene city. "The people worship after the Veshethmekhu," he wrote, and "They still speak of the Battle of Rivirnath, 17 years ago" (PAL·TV·00035). Rivirnath had kept its faith through two edicts, and still remembered the battle of 4836. The veiled head on the silver is what remains of the chairman who signed both the treaty and the first of those edicts.
