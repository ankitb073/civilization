[[per:21124|I Ash]], king of [[pol:911|Ihamiw]], and until this month first priest of the altars of the city, to the priests of the old rites who keep the hearths of [[mov:13|Unhatlmum]], greeting.

You will have heard it already in the market, for nothing travels faster in [[set:52|Ihamiw]] than what a king would rather tell himself. I write so that you may hear it from me, and in my own words, before it reaches you in worse ones: I have bowed my head at the altar of [[mov:72|Ishiu]], and the city with me, and the fillet of the old priesthood, which my fathers wore at every new year since the founding, I have laid upon your altar and shall not take up again.

I do not ask you to approve. I ask you to understand, which is a lesser thing and a harder one.

Consider first what you yourselves have seen. When I was a young man the porches of the old temples were crowded at every feast. Last spring I stood at the sacrifice of the first-fruits, and the court was half empty, and the half that came had come to look at the king. Where were the rest? They were in the upper rooms by the potters' quarter, listening to the ordained men of Ishiu read from their book of the end of days. It was not I who led the people there. They went before me, and I have followed them, as a magistrate follows a road the citizens have already worn smooth. A king who sacrifices alone is not a priest. He is a man talking to a wall.

Consider next the years we have lived through. You remember the [[evt:21372|Second Tivinnu–Ihamiw War]]. You remember that I myself held the line at Kivunge, at the [[evt:21376|Battle of Kivunge]], where their general fell and yet the field was theirs; you remember the two summers at Iazhash, the [[evt:21385|Battle of Iazhash]] and the [[evt:21394|Battle of Iazhash]] after it, and the [[evt:21395|Treaty of Iazhash]] by which we gave up fields that our grandfathers ploughed. Before each of these we sacrificed at your altars. The omens were good each time. I do not say the old gods lied. I say that the people no longer believed they had spoken, and a city cannot be governed by oracles its citizens have stopped hearing.

The teachers of Ishiu say that there is one Power over all things, and that the gods we fed are its servants or its shadows. I confess this does not offend me as much as perhaps it should. A shadow is not nothing; it is the shape of something real thrown upon the ground. If the gods of our hearths were shadows, then we were not fools to honour them, only men who looked down instead of up.

They say also that time runs in a line, and that every man will be judged at the end of it. That is a hard teaching for a king, and I do not pretend I find it comfortable. But it is a teaching for citizens, for it makes each man's conduct his own account and not his ancestors'. And they say that the wise withdraw from wealth and appetite. I have seen what appetite did to our treasury in the war. A city that has spent its silver [[cur:491|If]] by the cartload on a war it did not win may learn something from men who own one cloak.

What becomes of you? This is the question you will put to me, and I owe you an answer. Your houses will not be pulled down by my order. Your lands remain your own for your lifetimes. Your daughters may keep the hearth-fires in their own houses as their mothers did. I will not persecute a priest for honouring his father's gods; I have been one too long.

But I will not lie to you either. The altars of the city are now Ishiu's. The new year will be opened by their ordained men, not by me. And if you are wise, you will teach your sons letters and law rather than the reading of livers; for whatever the gods intend, the [[evt:21502|Conversion of Ihamiw to Ishiu]] is done, and the city has chosen.

Farewell, my brothers; I have not stopped thinking of you as that.

*Written at Ihamiw, in the year 2507 from the founding of Ihamiw.*
