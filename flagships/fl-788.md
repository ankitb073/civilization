*The day-book of the keeper of the market well, in the 12th year of [[per:22489|Vvinanudyiyen]], king of [[pol:931|Nivanga]].*

**At the turning of spring.** Let down the knotted cord at dawn, as every morning. Water at the ninth knot. A good depth; better than last year by half a knot. The washerwomen are already quarrelling over the trough, which is how one knows that there is enough water: people do not quarrel over plenty, they quarrel over manners.

**Some days later.** The council's man came to inspect the curb-stones and the bucket-chains, and asked me, as they always ask, why the town should pay a keeper when the water rises of itself. I told him that the water rises of itself, but it does not divide itself. He wrote something down. I think it was my answer, or possibly my name.

**In the heat.** Water at the eleventh knot. The summer is dry but honest. I put the cord away and took out my father's cord, which I keep in the chest with his sandals. His cord has thirty knots, and the last ten are stained darker than the rest, because in his time the water stood there for years together, and the cord sat in the mud at the bottom like an old man in his bath.

That was [[evt:22388|the Great Drought in Baan]]. I was a boy when it began and a young man when it ended; it lasted fourteen years, and I learned this trade inside it. Every morning my father let down the cord, and every morning it went deeper, and every morning he went to the council and told them, and every morning they asked him what they should do, as if he could argue with the sky.

He made the rule himself in the end, and the council only sealed it: one jar for each household at dawn, one at dusk; a second for a house with a newborn or a sick man; none for gardens; none for animals that were not for milk. A rich man of the upper town came with silver to buy a third jar. My father refused him. The man said, "I can pay." My father said, "So can the well, and it will not take your coin." I have heard that answer repeated in the porticoes as if a philosopher had made it. It was my father, and he was tired.

The teachers of [[mov:25|Myigudogüyshuye]] say that the wise withdraw from wealth and from appetite. In the drought everyone was wise, because there was nothing to have an appetite for. The test of a teaching, I think, is whether men keep it when the water returns.

**Late summer.** Grain in the market at about four-fifths of a [[cur:500|Shalnal]] the measure. Cheap. The farmers grumble, which is a sign of good harvests.

**Before the rains.** News from the king's war, though it is eleven years old now and still talked of at the well as if it were yesterday. The old soldiers who fill their jars here like to tell how they were at the [[evt:22701|First Sack of Vimginge]], and how the king, in his first year, set his seal to the [[evt:22716|Conquest of Myinve]]. One of them told the washerwomen that he had carried away a silver lamp from Vimginge. Another said he had carried away only a cough. The second I believe.

**First rain.** The street ran like a river. Children stood in it with their mouths open. I let down the cord, though there was no need. Tenth knot. I tied a thread at the tenth knot of my father's cord, so that the two cords should agree on something.

A well is the first city. Before there were walls or kings in [[set:70|Nivanga]], there was a hole in the ground and a rule about who drinks first. Everything else is commentary.
