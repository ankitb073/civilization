title: Eleven Fields and No Stone: Napizim, General of Puvu

Only one document from his lifetime writes his name, and it is a list of other men. In AY 3639 the scribe Nashiyathu drew up the muster of [[pol:1277|Puvu]] for the [[evt:34281|Battle of Luyügih (AY 3639)]], "under the command of Napizim": eight towns, 54,707 men, and a closing note, "The horses to be counted again at the river." (PAL·MU·00833). The roll, an original on papyrus, survives complete in the university library at [[set:93|Khayagu]].

It is a thin monument for a man who led Puvu's armies in eleven battles, more than any other commander in the empire's 446 years; the next led five. Between AY 4885 and 4925 the excavator Nanjil dug all six towns where he fought and listed 118 inscribed finds. Not one names him.

## A small kingdom among great ones

The record gives [[per:33571|Napizim]] a birth year, AY 3589, and a people, the Khufiènji. It names no parents, no town, no wife. He enters the record in 3616, at 27, as general of Puvu, and until his death it names no other Puvu general.

His first enemy was barely a state. [[pol:1433|Nsinakhizi]] had risen in 3606 from the wreck of Tambulu; the survey at the end of the 3610s found it with 12 districts, 399,626 people and an army of 4,870, against Puvu's 191 districts and 11.7 million. Three larger neighbours fell on it at once. Dagafu sacked its capital in 3616 and burned its archives; Khishkhis attacked from 3614, Puvu from 3615. At Shudhunvidhi two of them fought the same enemy: in 3616, 3618 and 3619 the record calls Khishkhis's battle the First and Puvu's the Second.

Napizim led all three Second Battles of the [[evt:34003|Puvu–Nsinakhizi War]]. In the [[evt:34025|Second Battle of Shudhunvidhi (AY 3616)]] he brought 34,022 men against 3,689 and lost 235 to 805; the enemy general [[per:33572|Nambimbi]], 38, died on the field. In the [[evt:34049|Second Battle of Shudhunvidhi (AY 3618)]], 47,507 men against [[per:33577|Nasunge]]'s 2,011 produced 132 dead on his side and 147 on theirs, a nearly even exchange at twenty-three to one. In the [[evt:34061|Second Battle of Shudhunvidhi (AY 3619)]] his 43,131 killed 1,334 of 2,255, and the king himself, [[per:33467|Nandumbeni]] the Unlucky, aged 45, was among them. That year Nandumbeni also signed a separate peace with Khishkhis, which gave it Shudhunvidhi itself. Puvu's treaty took six districts and a truce of fifteen years.

In 3618 he also held a second front. In the [[evt:34017|Second Banditadu War of Independence]] the rebel kingdom of [[pol:1438|Banditadu]] sent its general [[per:33588|Nanüdnan]], namesake of its king, near Dambali with 5,894 men. Napizim met him with 46,974 and won the [[evt:34054|Battle of Dambali (AY 3618)]], losing 502 to 814. A Banditadu chronicle remembers the place as Tambal: "through the cowardice of hired men, the host fell back to the walls. We lost 328; the enemy, 1,684." (PAL·CH·06103). It cuts its own dead by more than half, more than triples Puvu's, and never names the man who beat it. Its scribe dated it "in the 16th year of Banditadu", AY 3631, twelve years after the kingdom was suppressed, and asked: "Who will now remember the kings of old?"

## Whose victory

The queen made sure she would be remembered. [[per:33380|Nangashu]], ruler since she was sixteen, set up a diorite stele near Shudhunvidhi in 3621: "Nangashu, queen of Puvu, defeated Ngjinakhzi in Shuthunvithi. She killed 10,300 soldiers." (PAL·IN·02585). The battle was Napizim's in 3619. The record gives 1,334 enemy dead from an army of 2,255; the stele kills more than four times the men who were there. Nanjil found it in 4922, its final lines lost, on the same site as a basalt stele of Nangash II, queen of Khishkhis, claiming "3,200 soldiers" at the First Battle of 3618, where the record has 593 (PAL·IN·02584). Two queens, one field, and neither stone names a general. By the separate peace of 3619 the town had passed to Khishkhis; if the stele stood where it was found, Nangashu raised it in a town her empire no longer held.

A royal stele speaks for the crown; that is its genre. But a reader who trusted the stones would not know Napizim had lived.

## The truce runs out

From 3620 to 3634 the record is silent about him. They were hard years for Puvu: the Famine of AY 3624 in Daadüdhih killed 365,401 people. In Nsinakhizi, Nandumbeni's daughter [[per:33617|Nadinethi]] took the throne at 17, and in 3627 the kingdom abandoned Puvu's cult of [[mov:24|Uthungala]] for Langikath. The record calls her the Convert.

The truce expired in 3634. That year Nangashu died at 51, the nineteen-year-old Naluwzhu succeeded her, and Puvu opened the [[evt:34218|Third Khanji Trade War]], which the record traces partly to that conversion. Napizim fought all three of its battles. At the [[evt:34234|Tenth Battle of Khambi]] in 3635 he faced Nadinethi herself, 32,840 men against her 1,262; she lost 519, he lost 84. Twice at Hunjingivji he beat [[per:33756|Nayusu]], a general born the same year as he: 52,300 against 896 in 3636, 42,033 against 1,379 in 3637. In the whole war Puvu lost 230 men. The treaty, sworn "In the name of Uthungala and of Langjiau", ceded Khambi with three districts and ruled: "The merchants of Puvu shall trade in the markets of Ngjinakhzi free of all tolls." (PAL·TR·02214). Nsinakhizi collapsed in 3638, and the record lists three of Napizim's battles among the causes. His army had killed the father; his campaigns ended the daughter's kingdom. What either of them thought of him, the record does not say.

## Even odds

Then came an enemy of his own size. At the end of the 3630s [[pol:1243|Khishkhis]] counted 9.0 million people and an army of 67,546, Puvu 11.3 million and 87,344. In 3638 it opened the [[evt:34263|Holy War of Khishkhis against Puvu]]; its victory hymn later asked, "Did not the One first speak to them in hunger?" (PAL·PO·00015).

Napizim commanded the war's first four battles. At Luyügih in 3639 the young king [[per:33712|Navavun]], 24, attacked with 36,154 men. Napizim, 50, held 54,707, among them 16,960 men-at-arms of Ayagu and 5,255 mounted knights of Athekhizi, and Khishkhis lost 4,653 to his 2,007. It was his last victory. At the [[evt:34302|Battle of Mungu (AY 3640)]] [[per:33789|Nabuli]] brought 44,516 against his 40,920, and Puvu lost 14,790 men, more than a third of the army, to 2,168. The hymn, given to boasting, is exact here: "near fifteen thousand, and no man to bind them". At the [[evt:34312|Battle of Dambali (AY 3641)]] Navavun had 36,316 to his 32,977; Puvu lost 2,568 to 719, and the king's stele claims 13,700, adding: "May Shilim destroy the name of the enemy." (PAL·IN·02608). At the [[evt:34321|Battle of Dambali (AY 3642)]] Nabuli's 41,043 met his 27,970, and Puvu lost 5,173 to 3,277.

Napizim won all eight battles in which he had the larger army and lost all three in which he did not. In the eight victories he lost 3,254 men; in the three defeats, 22,531. It would be easy to call him a general made by numbers. The record allows a fairer reading: in 3643 Naluwzhu herself, outnumbered 27,240 to 23,021, won at Dambali, but at Bandidadhu in 3647 Puvu lost with 48,645 men against 21,596. Numbers were not everything, and what he chose on those fields the record cannot tell us.

## What the ground keeps

After 3642 he commanded no more. The muster for Dambali in 3643 was drawn up under Naluwzhu: 23,021 men, under half the host of Luyügih, "Each man to bring bread for ten days." (PAL·MU·00838). Napizim died in 3646, aged 57, of natural causes; the record does not say where. He did not see the Treaty of Agni of 3655 hand Khishkhis Dambali, Luyügih and forty districts, with 9,962,845 [[cur:698|Nièji]], some 115,569 kilograms of silver (PAL·TR·02222), nor the empire's collapse in 3665, which the record traces in part to Mungu.

Khishkhis kept his best day only as its own grief. Its hymn bids the singers "Sing of the king at Luyügih, where once we had wept", almost certainly 3639, and does not say who made them weep. At Dambali, where he won once and lost twice, Nanjil listed 21 inscribed finds and reported: "No destruction layer was found: the city seems never to have been sacked." (PAL·EX·00151). Navavun's curse held on stone. Napizim's name survives where he might least have expected it: at the head of a roll of other men's names, above a note about the horses.
