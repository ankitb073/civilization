[[per:23440|Khuekhimanim]], a teacher of [[set:91|Pibetie]], to [[per:23042|Ipil Lieze]], King of [[pol:1000|Pibetie]], in the twenty-seventh year of his reign: health, and a fair hearing.

You will have been told, O King, that a man has been speaking in the colonnade by the river gate, that crowds gather to hear him, and that the priests of [[mov:0|Leim]] are displeased. All of this is true. I write so that you may hear from my own pen what I teach, rather than from the mouths of those who fear it; for a ruler who judges a teaching by its enemies' report is like a physician who examines a patient by asking the neighbours.

Let me begin with what we both remember. I was a child of eight when the city was taken, in the [[evt:23189|Fourth Sack of Pivithie]]. I remember the smoke over the temple of [[dei:0|Etlie]], and my mother hiding me in a grain jar, and the sound of men laughing in the street. You were a boy then, scarcely older than I, and not yet king. The next year you ascended the seat, and within three years more you were made to sign two treaties in two years: the [[evt:23244|Treaty of Nyinsitlasa]], after our host was broken at the [[evt:23243|Battle of Nyinsitlasa]], and the [[evt:23258|Treaty of Pivithie]], signed within these walls, after the [[evt:23256|Battle of Onithie]] and the [[evt:23257|Siege of Pivithie]]. We gave up fields. We paid silver. I do not reproach you, O King. No one has reproached you more than you have reproached yourself.

But I ask: in all those years, what did the gods of the city do? We fed [[dei:5|Nieshashta]] of the spear, and our spears were broken. We fed [[dei:2|Muevase]] of the earth, and our earth was given to strangers. The priests said: the gods were angry; sacrifice more. We sacrificed more. Prosperity, they said, is a sign of divine favour; and so, by their own measure, we were not favoured.

I do not say the gods are false. I say they are not first. I teach that there is one divine power over all things, and that the gods our fathers named are its servants, or its shadows cast upon the wall of the world. A servant cannot save a house that his master has not chosen to save; a shadow cannot lift a stone. We looked to the shadows, and wondered why they did not move.

What does the One require? Not a new altar, nor gold. It requires what you, O King, have always required of your soldiers and your magistrates: duty, order, and right sacrifice. The citizen who keeps his post, pays his share, honours his word and gives of himself for the common good is already half a believer, whether he knows it or not. The [[mov:83|Khikhashakhi]] (for so my hearers have begun to call themselves, and I have not forbidden it) is nothing but this civic virtue, understood as worship.

Nor do I claim to own the truth. Teachers are to be honoured, but no one owns it, I least of all. Let the priests dispute with me in the colonnade; I will answer them there, in the hearing of all, and the citizens may judge.

One thing more distinguishes us, and I will not conceal it. The old worship belongs to one people and seeks no converts. The One belongs to all peoples. The men who sacked this city, and the men who took our fields, are also creatures of the One. I teach my hearers to speak to them, not to curse them. You may call this foolishness. I call it the only alliance a small kingdom can make that no treaty can take away.

I ask nothing of you, O King, but this: that the teachers of the One may teach openly, pay the city's taxes in [[cur:547|Odo]] like any other citizens, and be judged by their conduct, not their creed. If they break your laws, punish them. If they keep them better than others, consider why.

Farewell. I remain your subject, and the city's.
