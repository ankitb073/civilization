The kings of [[pol:1078|Luthe]] were not modest people, and in one sense [[per:27700|Najithu]] earned the epithet that later attached itself to him, "the Great". He ruled one of the great states of his age for forty-three years, from AY 3100 to 3143, at the head of an army that the record reckons at around 240,000 men, and in that time he absorbed three neighbouring states. The difficulty for a biographer is that the neighbours were small, two of them tiny, and that the force spent on swallowing them was out of all proportion to the prize. This chapter sets the great king beside the little cities he conquered and counts what the court did not count: what the conquests were worth.

## A boy in Kavu

Najithu was born in AY 3087, a Khumbivi of the royal house descended from Nakambège I. Under the Luthe law of seniority the throne could pass sideways among kin as readily as from parent to child. Through his childhood it rested with his mother, Queen [[per:27556|Nakumbèn]], who had come to it in 3082. She commanded in person at Danuthu in 3095, in the war against Tyimbi, and set up a victory stele for a general's win there the year before and a boundary stone to mark the war's gains. His father, Nashiènile, never reigned, and he is named only in the royal tombs of Kavu: on Najithu's own sarcophagus lid, the Epitaph of Najithu (PAL·IN·05330), and on the epitaph of his brother Naliènigu.

Nakumbèn died in 3100, aged thirty-nine, and the [[evt:28154|Accession of Najithu]] gave the throne to a boy of thirteen. A regent, [[per:27768|Nashinjè]], governed until 3103. Almost nothing is known of those three years. In a court so fond of monuments, that silence suggests they were quiet.

## The war on Pyivi

The young king's first war, the [[evt:28246|Second Luthe–Pyivi War]], began in 3107 against the kingdom of [[pol:1219|Pyivi]], an old land already beset by other enemies. At the [[evt:28279|Battle of Ajü (AY 3108)]] Najithu himself led 113,446 men against 5,164 under the veteran [[per:27713|Natange]]. He won, and the victory cost him 517 men; the defenders lost 1,019. Two further battles under his generals went the same way. The war ended without a treaty in 3111, when Pyivi, broken by its other enemies, fell apart and a rump city-state took up its name.

That rump, the city-state of [[pol:1237|Pyivi]], was the object of the [[evt:28410|Third Luthe–Pyivi War]] (3117–3120). Its figures are lopsided almost beyond belief. At the [[evt:28452|Battle of Ajü (AY 3119)]] the king brought 84,436 men against 1,952. They were commanded by [[per:28042|Natüzi]], a man of twenty-eight, who died on the field. His force lost 640 men and Luthe lost 267. A year later, at the [[evt:28487|Battle of Ajü (AY 3120)]], Najithu led 128,707 men against the 1,030 that the city's ruler, [[per:27962|Nachügü]], could gather, and lost twenty-nine. The [[evt:28489|Conquest of Pyivi]] followed. Eleven districts and the city of [[set:169|Ajü]], sacked twice in two years, passed to Luthe.

The Pyivi account of the Third Luthe–Pyivi War (PAL·CH·05190), written in 3164 by a scribe who still styled himself of the palace of Pyivi, forty-four years after its fall, claims that Luthe lost 1,005 men in 3119 and 109 in 3120, against 195 and 66 of its own. Numbers like these are the ordinary boasting of the defeated, who console themselves that the victory was dear. The record cuts them down and reverses the substance of the boast: over the whole war Luthe lost 417 men and Pyivi 1,282. The chronicle ends: "The royal city was taken. Who will now remember the kings of old?"

## Victories in stone

Why did so few of the king's men die? The record does not say, but the sizes go far to explain it: a city's levy of a few hundred or a couple of thousand could do little against an army forty or several hundred times its size. What the record does show is that Najithu's court counted these victories generously.

The clearest case is the [[evt:28547|Luthe–Shimbeyi War]] (3126–3129) against [[pol:1242|Shimbeyi]], a city-state founded only six years before. One of the king's generals fought both of its battles. Yet near [[set:191|Shimbayi]] the king raised a basalt stele in his own name, the Victory stele of Najithu: Battle of Shimbayi (PAL·IN·02126): "Najithu, great king, king of Luthe, defeated Shimbayi in Shimbayi. He killed 400 soldiers." The record gives the defenders' dead in that battle as seventy-one, out of a garrison of 205, and Luthe's as eighteen. The Shimbeyi account of the Luthe–Shimbeyi War (PAL·CH·05212), set down within living memory, calls him "swollen with pride" and says its army withdrew "when the allies failed to come". The [[evt:28601|Conquest of Shimbeyi]] in 3129 added a single district. The submission was sealed in the name of [[per:28149|Natili]], a boy of six whose father had fallen in the city's defence two years earlier. He died in 3135, aged twelve.

The last conquest repeated the pattern. In the [[evt:28665|Third Luthe–Tambakh War]] the king's armies besieged the city-state of [[pol:1247|Tambakh]]. At the [[evt:28699|Battle of Dambakhu (AY 3137)]] Najithu, now fifty, commanded 146,063 men against 182 under [[per:28259|Nangatsu]]. Thirty of the defenders died, and eleven of the attackers. The [[evt:28701|Conquest of Tambakh]] of that year, sealed by the city's ruler, a second [[per:28180|Natange]], brought one more district. For the whole war the record gives Luthe 26 dead and Tambakh 88.

## The ledger of greatness

Taken together, the four wars of conquest he saw through cost Luthe some 1,250 soldiers in battle, against more than 3,200 of the enemy, and gained it thirteen districts. The loss was trifling for so large an empire: its people grew from about twenty-three and a quarter million in 3100 to some twenty-four million by 3140. But the treasury fell by nearly a third in the first thirty years of the reign, and the record's measure of the realm's cohesion stayed below where it had stood in his mother's last decade. The men who died at Ajü and Dambakhu were for the most part conscripts, and their names survive nowhere.

Najithu died of natural causes in AY 3143, aged fifty-six. His three sons did not succeed him. Under the law of seniority the [[evt:28757|Accession of Najige]] gave the throne to his younger brother [[per:27701|Najige]], who was fifty and died two years later. The epitaph on the king's tomb says only: "Najithu, king of Luthe, son of Nashiènile, dwells in this tomb." In its plainness it is the most truthful document of the reign.

The empire he enlarged held its shape for three-quarters of a century more. A secession in 3169 began its fracture, and in 3219 it dissolved into six successor states. "Great" is the court's word. On the record, Najithu was a long-lived and persistent king who overwhelmed his weaker neighbours at little cost and gained less from them than his court's monuments suggest.
