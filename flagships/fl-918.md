On the 13th of Ushuth in the year 2102 of the Vishithmikh, which is AY 4357, an orator rose before the court at [[set:0|Taulin]] to praise the Empress [[per:40936|Kuluftungath]] on the thirtieth anniversary of her accession. Her name, he reminded his hearers, meant "the green lady" in the old tongue of the Imu, and her reign had been nothing less than "the greening of Yidhe". He listed her victories and then her harvests. "The granaries are full," he said. "The churches are full. The barren field is green." (PAL·PO·00032, "Panegyric on the Empress Khuluftungath in the Thirtieth Year of Her Reign").

Eight years later a palace clerk in the same city, a man named Nifpun, made an entry of three sentences in the record of the season: "The herds were driven to the hills for water. Grain stood at 1.13 Rimi the measure. Water was sold in the market by the jar." (PAL·RE·00181, "Record of the dry years from AY 4363"). The Rimi held its weight of 8.4 grams of silver throughout these years, so the clerk's grain cost about 9.5 grams a measure. That was cheap for the age, because the capital ate first: across the three hundred markets the record follows, the average measure of grain in the 4360s cost nearly 13.7 grams. The jar of water tells us more than the price does.

Neither the orator nor the clerk was lying. This chapter has to explain how both could be true at once. Between AY 4250 and 4500 the number of people living under states barely moved: 232.7 million at the start, 228.6 million in 4370, 236.9 million in 4490. Underneath that flat line the age destroyed six great states, among them the largest empire on the map. It was also the century and a half in which gunpowder, deposit banking and the ocean-going ship first appear in the record.

## The empire at the top

The previous chapter left three giants standing: Fièvi, Binge and Yidhe. Fièvi fell almost at once. The [[evt:40638|Collapse of the Fièvi State]] in AY 4255, during the eight-year reign of [[per:40142|Nambave II]], whom later writers called the Last, split it into six successor states, and a run of provincial revolts stands among its recorded causes. Fièvi had numbered 28.6 million people in 4250. The [[evt:41044|Collapse of the Yisamashtsi State]] followed in 4289, its emperors worn down by wars and a secession. That left [[pol:1562|Yidhe]] with no rival of its size.

From Taulin, [[per:40105|Pimpavbukhin I]] and then [[per:40126|Pimpavbukhin II]] ruled a realm that grew almost every decade: 43.8 million people in 4250, 49.4 million by 4290, 56.0 million and 532 districts at its height in 4340. By 4370 it held a sixth of the world's land. The treasury in the 4290s held about 4.1 million kilograms of silver. Yet the record's measure of internal order shows a slow decay behind the growth. It fell by a third between 4260 and 4300, and by two thirds by 4340. The empire was getting bigger and harder to hold.

Kuluftungath came to the throne in 4327 at twenty-seven. The panegyric preserves the court gossip that "the Shidhilath had produced a daughter where a son was needed". She answered it with war. A holy war beginning in 4339 brought a frontier province and a vassal prince. The Second Yidhe–Ashash War of 4350 to 4353 brought more. Its battles show the scale of the empire's armies and their strange costs. At the fourth battle of Linririnu in 4351, 870,985 men of Yidhe attacked 13,440 and lost 152,403 of their own. At Khash in 4352, which the orator called "the most complete victory of the age", nearly 1.4 million came against 12,477. Yidhe killed 5,200 of the enemy and lost 58,125. Over the whole war Yidhe lost 257,448 men and Ashash 10,237. In the 4350s the imperial army passed two million men for the first time. Yidhe had gunpowder weapons by 4357, thirteen years after they first appeared at Pinnga. The enemy cannot have killed most of these men. The march, the camp and the fever did.

## A century without rain

The record names thirteen great droughts in these two hundred and fifty years, and several of them lasted for decades. The [[evt:40593|Onset of the Great Drought in Luthe (AY 4251)]] began a dry spell over 220 districts that did not lift until 4340. The [[evt:40807|Onset of the Great Drought in Mikhukhizhi (AY 4276)]] struck 285 more. At Lininadhe that year a recorder of the Vishithmikh wrote, "Year after year the rains failed," and set grain at 1.37 Khathbi the measure, about 9.3 grams (PAL·RE·00176). The worst came in the middle of the period. The [[evt:41712|Onset of the Great Drought in Ng'jinaguh (AY 4350)]] spread across 599 districts and lasted, by the record's reckoning, until 4430. The [[evt:41825|Onset of the Great Drought in Yimla (AY 4365)]] touched 615. The almanac-makers of Ashash printed a blunt warning under their calendar for 4361: "The rains have failed these years. Sow the low fields first, keep back seed-corn, and trust no promise of rain." (PAL·AL·00450, "Almanac of Ashash for AY 4361").

Prices followed the weather. The average measure of grain stood at about 9.3 grams of silver in the 4250s. It reached 13.8 grams in the 4350s and 14.9 in the 4410s, the dearest decade of the age. The record counts seventeen famines in the period. The famine in Ayshi in 4344 killed or drove out 577,930 people, one in nine. The famine in Baan in 4367 took 895,274, and the famine in Yizamashti in 4396 took 1.72 million. The worst, in Yiutl in 4440, took 2.38 million.

One source for these years needs a warning. "Notes from a Dry Year in Mbambanluli" (PAL·DI·00016) is catalogued as a diary of 4365, but it comes from a private collection of uncertain origin, and its radio forecasts and relief lorries belong to a world five centuries later. Most scholars now read it as a later reconstruction. Its arithmetic is true of any dry year all the same: goats sell at half price while maize meal doubles, "and you can do that arithmetic in your head." The famine it describes is real. The record counts 7,401 people dead or fled in Mbambanlule, seventeen in every hundred of the district.

Hunger also started wars. In 4347 the Kingdom of [[pol:1655|Ölie]] attacked the Kingdom of [[pol:1521|Ayshi]] for the province of [[reg:10|Mikhukhizhi]], three years after the famine had weakened Ayshi. An Ölie hymn printed in 4355 admits that "some have said that we went to war in appetite, because Ayud lay weakened by the famine" (PAL·PO·00016, "A Hymn of Thanksgiving for the Victory at Unushti"). The [[evt:41693|Ölie–Ayshi War]] began well for the attacker. At Unushti in 4349 an army of 182,898 killed 58,027 of the enemy, and the hymn's preface gives these figures correctly; "they are the clerks'," the author insists. The preface also credits the victory to the king [[per:41061|Isyi Athe]]'s return to the old gods a year earlier, after twelve years under a new faith. Every later battle went to Ayshi. Ölie lost 455,671 men to Ayshi's 221,612, and at Zhaalakhazhash in 4353 it signed away an indemnity of 12,403,916 Nyinan, some 177 tonnes of silver, along with trading rights and twenty years of peace (PAL·TR·02604).

Victory saved neither side. The [[evt:41987|Collapse of the Ayshi State]] in 4376 lists among its causes the battles of the war and the famine in Baan of 4367. Ayshi broke into six states, among them [[pol:1772|Tushi]] and Iith. Ölie suffered three secessions between 4361 and 4382, and the [[evt:42216|Collapse of the Ölie State]] followed in 4393. Ayshi, the winner, fell first and Ölie, the loser, fell second. The old king [[per:40013|Nistlushimin]], who reigned more than sixty years and made Ölie great, had written in his testament of 4312 that he "could not command the rain" but "commanded the granaries, and they were opened" (PAL·DE·00928). His successors had the rain and the war to deal with together.

## How Yidhe fell

Yidhe's ruin began with a rebellion that the empire won battle by battle. In 4364 the province of Biniynge broke away under its own king, Bubiruyin, and the [[evt:41870|Third Yidhe Civil War]] began. Few wars in this book have stranger figures. At Linidhagshal in 4365 the rebel captain Khuthnathdan came out with 28 men against an imperial host of 1,104,377 under Tuvbilkhun. The rebels lost four men and Yidhe lost 85,334. At Riniy in 4367, 34 rebels faced more than 1.4 million and lost six, and Yidhe lost 118,421. Yidhe won every battle. A rebel officer wrote to his king from Riniy:

> We were thirty and four. Thirty and four we were, the king's men, no more. … Their dead were more than a hundred thousand; the vultures could not eat them all. Of the servants of the king my lord, six fell. Six fell, and the rest stand. But the field is not ours.

His dispatch (PAL·LT·01671, "Dispatch from Riniy to the King Bubiruyin") is a plea for men and bread, and it ends with news that the enemy had taken his king's city and that "the house of tablets was burned". Its numbers are close to the record's. The imperial account, set down in 4428, is the less trustworthy. It gives the rebels 80 men at Linidhagshal and 97 at Riniy, gives Yidhe two dead in each battle, and in the final battle of 4370 nearly triples the rebel army (PAL·CH·07380, "Yidhe account of the Third Yidhe Civil War"). The record puts Yidhe's losses for the whole war at 474,488, against 21,341 for the rebels. As in the wars of earlier chapters, an empire too large to be defeated in the field was bleeding to death on the road.

The rebellion was crushed in 4370. Kuluftungath and the defeated king, remembered as Bubiruyin the Ill-Starred, signed its end at Riniy: fifty-eight districts went back to the crown, and the rebels received an amnesty. The damage had been done. The treasury fell from 4.1 million kilograms of silver around 4300 to 1.1 million in the 4380s, and the measure of order sank to about a fifth of what it had been under Pimpavbukhin II. Kuluftungath died in 4377. The [[evt:42128|Collapse of the Yidhe State]] came in 4387, and the only cause the record gives is the secession of Biniynge twenty-three years before. The empire had never recovered from it. Six states came out of the wreck. One of them took the old capital and a new name, and with it the next century.

So, within seventeen years, three of the four largest states by territory in 4370 were gone: Ayshi in 4376, Yidhe in 4387, Ölie in 4393. The drought of Ng'jinaguh was still running when the last of them fell.

## Heirs and empresses

The [[pol:1785|Tallin]] Empire was founded in 4387 at Taulin. It kept Yidhe's capital, its faith and even its coin. In the 4390s it was a modest kingdom of 17.7 million people and 113 districts. Its first ruler, [[per:41722|Balshiymaanin I]], spent the next decades fighting the other heirs of Yidhe. In the [[evt:42347|Tallin–Zhinontunu War]] of 4401 to 4406, the general Tupufnen fought four battles at Puthnimdu and then took Zhinontunu itself. The peace gave Tallin the province of [[reg:9|Tuvizhinge]], twenty-seven districts and an indemnity of about 76 tonnes of silver. A court epic printed in 4434 meets the critics head-on: "Certain chroniclers, counting heads like tax-farmers, have written that Tallin bought Tuvizhinge too dearly … I do not dispute their arithmetic; I dispute their scales." (PAL·PO·00018, "The Tupufniad, Canto the Fifth"). Tallin did lose more men than it killed, 277,311 to 165,181. The [[evt:42606|First Tallin–Pienpedhzhe War]] of 4425 to 4434 took two more towns.

The reconquest was finished by a woman crowned at the age of two. [[per:42203|Shithkhukhuth]] came to the throne in 4428 and reigned sixty-two years. In 4453 she opened the [[evt:42969|First Tallin–Tunnge War]] against [[pol:1788|Tunnge]], another heir of Yidhe. The war went badly at first. At Shiniy in 4455 Tallin lost 162,680 men and its general, Shinlilalin, was killed. Her panegyrist did not hide it: "The fog came down; the general fell; the losses were very great, and the city mourned." (PAL·PO·00043, "The Star and the Moon"). In 4459 she took command herself at Ture and won. In 4461 the treaty of Togannge gave Tallin sixty-five districts, six towns and a river. By 4490, when her son Pavbukhshifin I succeeded, Tallin held 38.9 million people. The poet ended with a "corollary for the merchants": in all her reign the Rimi "has not lost a grain of its weight." The record agrees. The coin weighed 8.4 grams when Yidhe issued it and still weighed 8.4 grams under Tallin.

The Tunnge war was fought in the coldest years of the age. The [[evt:42770|Eruption of Mount Bavmumungra (AY 4449)]], a blast of the seventh magnitude, cooled the world by about a degree. The 4440s were the only decade of the chapter in which the mean temperature fell more than a degree below the long-run mean. Grain at Taulin reached 16.7 grams in the 4460s, the highest price the capital had paid since at least AY 4000.

Elsewhere the great powers fought small wars at great cost. [[pol:1670|Dagafu]] reached 37.1 million people under [[per:42478|Nakhange]], crowned at six in 4451 and called the Great. An anonymous satire nailed to a tavern door in Dagafu in 4477 mocks her victory at Gandagi: "three hundred thousand of us went out to catch seven thousand of them, / and forty-four thousand of ours came home in the carts, and she called it a triumph." (PAL·PO·00046). The record gives 302,246 men against 7,170 and 44,076 lost, so the satirist's arithmetic is exact. His envoi reads: "take a smaller army, Lady. You will win it all the same." At [[pol:1710|Binge]], [[per:41070|Livdun]] reigned seventy years. A song for his forty-third year boasts of sixty thousand enemy dead at Lunge in 4351; the record says 65,499. The same song praises a persecution of the I'ishiu (PAL·PO·00044). The new Kingdom of [[pol:1795|Tomzel]], seated at [[set:264|Ölye]] in Ölie's old capital, beat [[pol:1656|Ane]] in the [[evt:43236|Second Ane–Tomzel War]] of 4478 to 4483 and took an indemnity of about 182 tonnes of silver.

## Powder, paper and new gods

The age's lasting changes came from workshops and counting-houses more than from courts. Gunpowder weapons first appeared at Pinnga in 4344, credited to Vuthshuth, and were invented again at Bige in 4493. Deposit banking and bills of exchange appeared at Nesh in 4375, credited to Aatlege, and were invented again, separately, at Fuvu in 4450 and in Khishkhis in 4462. Ocean navigation began at Bige in 4491. Merchants founded 208 houses in the period, and states issued fifty-eight new coinages and weight standards. Banking that arose three times over in three places was no accident of genius. It answered a need: a merchant's money had to cross a world of falling states, and a paper bill was easier to carry than silver.

The [[mov:58|Veshethmekh]] faith, the Vishithmikh of the Taulin panegyrists, held about a quarter of the world's land in 4370, and its "One" and its "Light" fill their verses. Below it the map of belief kept changing. The [[evt:40695|Khaleneth Schism]] of 4256 was led by [[per:40280|Magunukhueven]] at Ngiunge. Ayshi adopted the Sinimu faith in 4288 and then made the [[evt:41190|Conversion of Ayshi to Kievi]] six years later. In 4448 Nazhige founded the Khunzhelathle faith at Dagambi. The Akhkun school of philosophy, founded by Ninun, dates from 4485. Languages were changing too. Shitlu emerged from Fietlte'ma'im around 4299; Ashmem and Fivi around 4399; Kutumbivi, Zhizhin and Imam by 4499. The record dates ninety sound changes to the period.

We should keep in mind how we know all this. The record keeps 1,154 everyday documents from these years, mostly chronicles, inscriptions and ledgers. Of eighty-five recorded sacks, thirty-five burned the city's archives. Biniynge's house of tablets was one of them, and its loss is why the rebel side of the civil war survives in a single dispatch. The literary voices are those of court orators, with one tavern satirist and one doubtful diarist. The panegyrists wrote to be believed, and on the numbers they are often more honest than the court chronicles. The clerks wrote to be useful. The two kinds of source, read together, give us this chapter.

In AY 4500 the armies of [[pol:1769|Wemu]] were still at war with Tushi over Iith. In the [[evt:43383|First Wemu–Tushi War]] the field of Ash would be fought over nine times. At its first battle, in 4492, Wemu lost 127,671 men and its general, though its king assured his council that the enemy had lost "some forty thousand" (PAL·LT·01672); the record gives 17,769. Tallin, Dagafu and Binge stood at their height, and the first ocean-going ships had just sailed from Bige. The next chapter follows those ships, and what came back on them.
