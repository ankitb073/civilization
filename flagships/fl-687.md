## Two men called Nambili

Readers of the excavation archives of [[pol:1638|Khishkhis]] must learn early to keep two men apart. Thousands of finds carry the same provenance line, excavated at such a site, in such a year, by Nambili, and there were two archaeologists of that name. The younger, born in AY 4874, opened his first site in 4899 and is still in the field as this chapter is written. The elder, my subject, was born in AY 4857 among the Khunilile people and held the post of archaeologist of Khishkhis from 4882 until his natural death in 4931, at seventy-four. For twenty-eight years both men were digging at once, and only the site and the season tell their work apart.

Beyond those dates the record is silent about the elder [[per:47267|Nambili]]. It names no parents, no wife, no children, no teacher. No letter of his survives, and no portrait. What survives is 106 printed reports, each published by the excavator exactly two years after its season, and in none of them does he mention a colleague, a workman or a patron. His world we can see: a one-party state governed from [[set:85|Nambi]] by elected chairmen, whose population roughly doubled in his working life, from about 87 million at the end of the 4870s to about 174 million at the end of the 4920s. Of the man inside it we have only his trenches. I took up the same post in AY 4935, four years after his death, and this is the life his trenches allow.

## A hundred and six trenches

In 4882, his first year in the post, he dug three sites at the age of twenty-five; the greatest was Ishwyithie, then a city of the Kingdom of Tomzel. His report, printed in 4884 as "Excavations at Ishuwuyithuya: preliminary report", gave the city three names at once: Shue'ethie on its own bricks, Ishwyithie in our catalogue, Ishuwuyithuya on his title page. He recorded that "18 destruction layers were identified" and took out 181 inscribed finds, more than he would draw from any other site. Among them was a brick of AY 6 (PAL·IN·03168) on which a queen declares that she "restored this palace in Shue'ethie".

The reports that followed are almost liturgical. Each counts the destruction layers, if any, describes the most recent, and states its method in one of three set phrases: the trench "followed the line of the old city wall", or was cut "across the palace quarter and the lower town", or "concentrated on the citadel mound and the temple precinct". He dug in forty-three of the forty-six years from 4882 to 4927; his busiest year was 4911, with six sites, and four of his digs yielded a single inscribed find each. When he closed his last season, at seventy, he had opened 106 sites and recorded 3,746 finds, the most excavations credited to any one person in the record, though only by one. Nanjil, born two years before him, dug 105, three of them without a find or a report; the younger Nambili had reached 104 by 4955. Across those 315 excavations no site was opened twice. It looks like a map divided among three men; the record does not say who divided it.

Every report calls itself preliminary. Thirty-two of his end their lists of finds with the rest "catalogued in the final report". No final report, by him or by anyone, is in the record.

## Digging in other men's kingdoms

The most surprising thing the record shows is where he dug. Of his 106 sites, only one lay inside Khishkhis when he opened it: Lithi, in 4918. The other 105 lay in twenty-two other states, nineteen in the Muvush Empire and fourteen in the Kingdom of Yiethithi.

His working life ran beside four wars, all won by Khishkhis: the holy wars against Nuidaduth (4881–85), Yiethithi (4891–96) and Fuvu (4903–08), and the [[evt:47690|Khishkhis–Luthe War]] (4922–26), fought for territory. He dug at Nuvun, inside Nuidaduth, in 4885, the year the [[evt:47269|Holy War of Khishkhis against Nuidaduth]] ended by treaty. In 4892, the second year of the [[evt:47389|Holy War of Khishkhis against Yiethithi]], he was at Bathshi, inside the kingdom his state was fighting. Who allowed a scholar of the attacking power to work there in wartime, and who protected his diggers, the record does not say.

Lithi came to Khishkhis through that war. The general Navandi beat the Yiethithi army near the town in 4893 and again in 4895, and in 4896 [[per:46391|Napanjif]] signed the treaty that ceded Lithi and also Nimxi, a town Nambili had dug six years before. When he opened Lithi in 4918 he found two sacks, of AY 2688 and 2714, and fifteen inscribed finds, eleven of them victory steles for earlier battles near the city. Of the battles of his own lifetime his trench held nothing; those survive on paper. From the field of 4895 Navandi wired the ministry of war: "OUR LOSSES ABOUT 12,900 STOP ENEMY LOSSES ESTIMATED 35,500" (PAL·TG·00194). The record counts 14,478 Khishkhis and 26,718 Yiethithi losses. The latest stele in Nambili's list, set up by a queen of Yisamashtsi after a battle at Lithi in AY 4071, claims "She killed 2,100 soldiers" (PAL·IN·02955); the record gives the enemy's losses as 740. The telegraph had replaced the stele, not the habit.

In 4925 he reached the city of Yiethithi itself, the defeated kingdom's capital, and wrote: "No destruction layer was found: the city seems never to have been sacked." The holy war had taken sixteen districts, Lithi and Nimxi among them, but never the capital.

After his death his map became a map of war in a plainer sense. In the holy war against Bakh (4933–37) battles were fought at Yyixi, Lithi, Bau and Namosash, four places he had dug between 4918 and 4926. Nothing in the record connects his reports to any army, and I do not suggest it. His own Lithi report, with its eleven steles, is the better explanation: old cities and old battlefields share their ground.

## What the clay kept

Most of what he lifted was the speech of power: inscriptions, chronicles, treaties. But the trenches also kept people whom no stele names. At Ane in 4920, among 163 finds, lay the tally of a gravedigger of the gate quarter during the Shaking Pestilence of AY 739. He counts a potter, a seller of onions, the midwife who delivered his son, and then stops: "I do not write the names of the children now. There is not room on the tablet." His last note reads: "My hands shake tonight. It is the cold. It is the long work. It is the cold."

Such voices reached us through translators as much as through the excavator. Nazhigi (translator, AY 4883–4950) rendered more than fifteen hundred texts from Nambili's sites; Nalilimi, Nabuli and Nami did much of the rest. Nami died in 4931, the year Nambili did.

The same season at Ane yielded the oldest dated document in the entire archive, a hymn of AY 3 in praise of writing, spoken in the name of King Shuesh'ayueve of Ana. Nambili lived in an empire whose faith, [[mov:70|Vimekhulu]], teaches that all things turn on a great wheel and that what ends will begin again. The hymn, made nearly five thousand years before his trench gave it up, knows a doctrine of that shape and answers it:

> The priests say: all things turn on the wheel.
> What ends will begin again.
> But the signs say: what was spoken will remain.
> Let the wheel turn. The clay remains.

Of the man whose trench gave up that tablet the record keeps a name he shared, two dates, and 106 reports that call themselves unfinished. It is not much of a life. It is, line for line, more than most of the people in his trenches were given, and he is the reason we can read them.
