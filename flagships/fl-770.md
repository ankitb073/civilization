To my mother, lady of the house, from your son in the garrison at [[set:189|Wineninu]], in the third year of the queen [[per:15715|Ninsinim]]. Greeting. May [[dei:6|Nili]] keep the canal full at home, and may the goats not get into the beans.

I am well. The bread is bad. The wall is long. That is the whole news of a garrison, but you asked for more, so here is more.

This is the city my father helped to take. He told it at the hearth so often that I can say it with him, word for word: how the host of [[pol:526|Los]] came up the river road in the spring; how they broke the gate; how the city burned for two days, which the priests now call the [[evt:15677|Fifth Sack of Viririnu]]; how the boy-king of Wineninu, [[per:15517|Makhonutiwin]], no taller than a plough-handle, came out and set his seal beside the seal of old [[per:15494|Wa'aliluesh'a]], the father of our queen; and how the land was ours from that day. The priests read out the [[evt:15678|Conquest of Virrnu]] at the festival every year, and the people of this city stand and listen to it with their mouths shut.

I looked for the gate my father broke. It has been mended. The new stones are a different colour from the old, paler, like a scar on a brown arm. I put my hand on them, and an old woman selling onions spat on the ground. I did not blame her.

Nine years ago our men beat the men of Nidinga under these walls, at the [[evt:15768|Battle of Viririnu]]. When the farmers plough the low field by the river, they still turn up bones and arrowheads. The children here sell the arrowheads to new soldiers. I bought one. I will bring it home for my sister.

There are omens. Last month a hawk fell dead into the courtyard of the garrison, with no wound on it. The priest of [[dei:11|Nizhashte]] said it was a good sign, that the courage of our enemies has fallen out of the sky. The sergeant said it was a sick hawk. I think the sergeant is right, but I gave the priest a copper anyway, because one does.

Grain here costs one [[cur:274|Ikhal]] and a fifth the measure, which is dearer than at home, because the carts take the province's tax-grain away to Los and the people here must buy back what they grew. They watch the carts go. Nobody says anything. I think they say it at night.

There is a woman by the north gate who sells bread, and she calls me "son". Her own son died in the year of the burning, the year my father came. She knows who I am and whose son, and she still gives me the end of the loaf for nothing. I do not understand it, Mother. Perhaps you will.

I send you two ikhal with the carrier, sewn into the cloth. Do not let my uncle borrow them. Give the priest at home a small cake for [[dei:10|Masto]], for my sake, so that the sun keeps looking at me here.

Your son, at the long wall.
