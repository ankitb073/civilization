title: A Family of One: What Pakhi Can and Cannot Tell Us about Proto-Telnu
---
The [[lan:2|Telnu]] family is among the smallest in the archive's classification, with one recorded daughter, [[lan:32|Pakhi]], which separated from the parent about AY -1284 and is still spoken. That fact shapes everything that can honestly be said about it. The comparative method works by triangulation: when two or more daughters agree, we infer the ancestor; when they differ, we ask which differs by rule. With one daughter there is nothing to triangulate. Every asterisked Telnu form is therefore weaker than its Ngano or Tasemem equivalents, and I want to set out plainly what supports it.

## The evidence

The standard list gives five words:

- 'water': *gewe, Pakhi gyib
- 'king': *hā, Pakhi ha
- 'god': *lūze, Pakhi zarzi
- 'mother': *nāṭa, Pakhi nad
- 'silver': *dostā, Pakhi duista

Some correspondences are clear. Pakhi has lost the long vowels of the reconstruction: *hā gives ha, and the final *ā of *dostā is short in duista; the long a of nad is a later lengthening of Pakhi's own. The emphatic consonant of *nāṭa survives as a plain d. Pakhi has lost final vowels after a consonant (nad, gyib) and has strengthened *w to b (gyib). The front glide in gyib, written y, is of the kind that breaking of e after an initial consonant produces elsewhere. Each is reasonably secure.

One word does not correspond at all. Pakhi zarzi cannot be derived from *lūze by any regular change I can identify; the initial consonant alone defeats every attempt. Either Pakhi replaced its word for 'god', as languages often do with sacred vocabulary, or *lūze is wrongly reconstructed. With a single daughter the list alone cannot tell us which; the record's dated history settles it as a replacement, about AY -243, of an inherited luri.

## The dated laws

The record dates twelve changes in the parent language before Pakhi separated, and our tables give eight of them: intervocalic voicing (p t k ʃ > b d g ʒ between vowels), c. AY -3179; loss of final n after a vowel, c. -3047; postvocalic spirantization (b d g p t k > v ð ɣ f θ x after a vowel), c. -2613; open-syllable lengthening (e o a > eː oː aː in the first syllable before a consonant and vowel), c. -2573; lowering of u to o before a final consonant, c. -2303; spirantization of k to x, c. -2068; palatalization of s before front vowels, c. -1979; and the weakening of the pharyngeal ħ to h, c. -1757.

All eight precede the separation of [[lan:32|Pakhi]], so all eight are part of its inheritance. Together they span some fourteen centuries of Telnu history before its only recorded daughter appears. Yet test the eight laws against our five words and almost none of them can be seen at work. None of the five has a plain voiceless stop between vowels, a final n, a plain stop after a vowel, a u before a final consonant, a k, an s before a front vowel, or a ħ. Only open-syllable lengthening could have touched one of them, *gewe, and its effect has since been erased by Pakhi's own loss of vowel length. Of the four the tables omit, rhotacism (z > r) and a merger of e into i did reach *lūze and *gewe, but Pakhi's later losses hide them.

The laws, in other words, are not established by this list, and the list does not test them. They rest on fuller evidence, which our short comparative tables do not reproduce. That is no criticism of the record, but a warning to readers who take the tables as proof. A five-word list from a single daughter can illustrate a history; it cannot demonstrate one.

## What we may fairly claim

Three claims seem to me defensible. First, Pakhi preserves the consonant skeleton of the parent remarkably well where it can be checked: g, h, n, d and st all survive in recognisable form. Second, the main changes visible in these words, the loss of length and of emphasis, the loss of final vowels and the strengthening of w, belong to Pakhi's own history after AY -1284, not to the parent's. Third, the parent's dated laws, though real, are almost invisible in this vocabulary, and the asterisks on our five Telnu forms should be read as markers of hypothesis, held more loosely than elsewhere in the archive.

A family of one is not a failure of scholarship; it reminds us how much our confident reconstructions elsewhere owe to the accident that more daughters survived. For [[lan:2|Telnu]], one did; we should be grateful for what it tells us and modest about the rest.
