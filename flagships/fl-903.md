"Do not send the wool. Do not send the oil. Do not send the donkeys on the river road." So a wool merchant of Taulin wrote to his brother at Pumi in AY 511, on a clay tablet that survives almost whole (PAL·LT·01786). The sickness began, he says, "in the lower town by the river, after the barley harvest": first the fever, then red marks on the arms and neck, then the bloody flux. "On the fifth day he goes down to the wheel." The dead were carried out at night, "so the living do not count them." His advice is a merchant's: sell the wool even at a loss and keep the silver under the floor, because barley is cheap now, less than a Tunrun the measure, and "when the sickness has gone, the barley will be dear." He signs off "with a cloth over my mouth."

He was right on both counts. [[evt:1784|The Scarlet Flux]] broke out in [[set:0|Taulin]], capital of the [[pol:61|Tallin]] empire, and ran along the trade roads to thirty-six more cities. The record puts the dead at about 1.32 million, a quarter of the people in the cities it reached. Taulin, a city of some 41,000 in AY 500, held fewer than 30,000 a century later, and the empire fell from 3.2 million people to under 2.7 million by 560. Barley in Taulin cost about 6.5 grams of silver the measure in the 510s, 10 grams in the 580s and more than 13 in the 650s. The letter matters because it is so rare: the temple record of the same plague (PAL·RE·00004) runs to four sentences.

This chapter covers AY 500 to 750. The people living under states grew from 43.8 million to 52.3 million, while the states themselves fell from thirty-one to twenty-seven, and as late as 620 a third of the land lay outside any state at all. What marks the age is the endurance of its capitals. The record counts more than thirty collapses of states in these years, most of them small. Six stand out, and every one of them left a successor ruling from the fallen state's own capital. The wars were small by later standards; the worst killed fewer than 44,000 soldiers in twelve years. The great killers were fevers, droughts and the hungry years that followed armies.

## The red sickness and the house of tablets

The merchant's letter shows Tallin's queen, [[per:2097|Unath II]], speaking in the gate: "Let the dead be burned outside the wall." She reigned from 508 to 558, and later ages called her the Old. Hard on the plague came the [[evt:1783|onset of the Great Drought in Baan]] in 513, across 220 districts. Some 160 of them lay beyond any state's borders, and the drought has left almost no trace in the court records, the first of many reminders that people without states left no tablets. Mount Rudirba erupted in 535, again in country no king claimed, and the 530s were the coldest decade until the 740s, three-quarters of a degree below the long-run mean.

Faith moved with the people rather than ahead of them. [[pol:60|Duegowinga]] adopted [[mov:21|Kunnelmu]] in 501, and the [[evt:1843|Conversion of Buneynga to Kunnelmu]] followed in 512, the year after the flux reached Buneynga's towns. Then came the [[evt:2035|Conversion of I'iti to Ya'cuam]] in 536 and the [[evt:2094|Conversion of Chinontonu to Ligay]] in 544. In every case the record gives the same reason: most of the realm already held the new faith, and the court followed. Chinontonu needed what unity it could find, since pretenders split it in the [[evt:1999|Secession of Ramtulnga]] in 536 and again in the [[evt:2114|Secession of Ramtulnga]] of 552.

The age's most lasting invention was paperwork. In 526 the scribe Piscualosh'a of Tilcui proclaimed it at the market gate (PAL·NP·01078): "I set the scribes in rows, ten and ten," each tablet sealed and copied for palace and temple, and "if the two do not agree, the scribe is beaten." His boast became the motto of every bureaucracy since: "the gods favour the house that counts." A man named Nangashu invented the same thing independently in the kingdom of [[pol:74|Luthe]] in 611. It did not save Luthe. The [[evt:2626|Collapse of the Luthe State]] came in 622, and its heirs, the city-states of [[pol:110|Kavu]] and [[pol:111|Luyulule]], lasted less than a decade. Counting a kingdom was not the same as holding it. Writing spread in the same years: a syllabary adopted in Kavu in 512 had a daughter script in use at [[set:5|Nilu]] by 535, three years after [[per:2289|Nakatha]] raised the [[pol:80|Nelo]] empire there.

## Old palaces, new names

[[pol:6|Ana]] was one of three kingdoms that had lasted since AY 0, and it had always ruled from [[set:7|Ane]]. Under [[per:1883|Nuezanieme]] (479–514), [[per:2143|E'a Nieme]] (514–539) and [[per:2341|Esiesh Foyatl]] (539–549) it held 144 districts, and by 570 it had 5.2 million people. In the twenty-nine years after Esiesh Foyatl died, four rulers held the throne, the last two of them usurpers. The [[evt:2265|Collapse of the Ana State]] followed in 578.

Ana did not starve. Barley at Ane cost under 7 grams of silver through the 560s and 570s, below the average of the world's markets, and the only causes the record names are the two usurpations. [[pol:94|Mwehosh]] and Tlalasyi took provinces, and the new state of [[pol:93|Mii]], whose name means "high desert," took Ane itself under a woman of forty-seven named In'a. Her successors [[per:2736|I'e]] (590–623) and [[per:2807|Ietl'es]] (623–657) spent two generations winning back Ana's lands, beating Tlalasyi in 592–602 and Mwehosh in 650–657, and Mii grew from 110 districts in 600 to 182 by 720. Ane itself grew from about 41,000 people to 48,000 between 500 and 700.

The pattern held across the age. The [[evt:2602|Collapse of the Wa'mi State]] in 619 left a single heir, Va'mi, in the same city. The [[evt:2822|Collapse of the Talenonoy State]] in 652 left [[pol:119|Tonnga]] in Talenonoy's own capital, with [[pol:120|Nonenga]] and Tungarvikhrim beside it. Tagath's fall in 700 left the Luthe empire at Dagafu, Kavu kept Luthe's old capital, and Duvegobuvinga kept Pumi's. In each of the six great collapses the house and the name changed, and the palace stayed where it was.

Talenonoy's end produced the finest poem of the period, the "Lament for Talenonoy, Mother of Lands" (PAL·PO·00024) of 667. The city's name means "bright well." Its saddest line is "The grass comes up between the bricks of the processional way." The poet blames heaven: "the storm-lord has turned his face; the rain is late." Here I part company with a poet I admire. Barley at Taalinnuy sold for about 6.4 grams of silver in the 650s, when the world's average was above 10. The record names other causes, and so does the poet, a stanza earlier. Nonenga seceded in 597 and again in 616, Myinive revolted in 617, and in 636 Talenonoy lost a battle at Linidhagshal and paid Tallin 26,478 kilograms of silver: "your silver went out on the backs of asses, and it did not come back." Talenonoy did not dry up. Its own wars ruined it.

[[pol:3|Tagath]], at [[set:9|Dagafu]], was as old as Ana. It survived [[per:1992|Nanangi]] the Usurper (495–526), prospered under [[per:2416|Navande II]] (551–593) and [[per:2750|Navavun]] (593–641), and reached its height of 3.8 million people and 137 districts in 690. Tagath invaded Nelo in 684, and at Ajü the next year it lost 4,059 of 9,314 men to Nelo's 654. A famine in Dyimbi in 687 cost it about 130,000 people, dead or fled, and in 692 Kaküni seceded. The [[evt:3241|Collapse of the Tagath State]] in 700 gave Dagafu to the new [[pol:134|Luthe]] empire, beside the city-state of [[pol:135|Unjinlathu]] and the kingdom of Kagüne, and barley at Dagafu doubled to nearly 16 grams. The sixth collapse, the [[evt:3021|Collapse of the Pumi State]] in 675, divided that kingdom between [[pol:128|Duvegobuvinga]] and [[pol:129|Nuvi]].

## The ford at Bami

Few places suffered the age's wars as often as Bami, a town of some 14,000 by a river in [[reg:6|Iith]]. Its own people called it Wa'mi, the name that the kingdom ruled from it also bore. Between 511 and 741 eighteen battles were fought there. The attacker was nearly always [[pol:50|Tantöti]], an empire with an elective throne, ruled from [[set:152|Tanshedi]]. Its king [[per:2211|Al'ashmo]] (521–549), after taking eighteen districts from [[pol:49|I'iti]] in 533, raised a boundary stone in Iith (PAL·IN·06439) with a single curse, "May Nili curse the enemy."

In the [[evt:2141|Second Tantöti–Wa'mi War]] of 559–570, Tantöti marched on Bami eight times, and a lament of 582 keeps the count (PAL·PO·00003): "Eight times they went down to Bami." Its account of the opening battle, at Veümin in 560, is exact: the queen Ashada brought 11,492 men against fewer than 2,900 and lost 2,119 to 298, "the many were broken by the few." The war killed about 17,000 soldiers and ended with the frontier where it had been: "No land was given. No land was taken. The dead were given." The poet will not blame the enemy: "The Wa'mi did not conquer them. The year conquered them." The record may bear the poet out. The [[evt:2142|onset of the Great Drought in Tunnge]] in 568 began the largest drought of the age, across 622 districts, thirty-one of them Tantöti's and thirty-two Wa'mi's, and it lasted ninety years.

[[pol:4|Wa'mi]] lost a war with Yimola, paid 14,870 kilograms of silver at Hiviyu in 587, and broke in 619. Its heir, [[pol:108|Va'mi]], ruled from the same town, and the war resumed under the new name. In the drought that began in Pyivi in 683, Tantöti's province of Iith lost some 290,000 people to famine, dead or fled, and barley at Tanshedi reached 30.5 grams of silver, more than four times its price in the 520s.

The [[evt:3337|Holy War of Tantöti against Va'mi]] (712–719) was fought between two queens. A lament of 740 (PAL·PO·00004) has Tantöti's empress [[per:3528|I'a Ash II]] sending her men to "make the land clean for the gods of Nishmam." At Veümin in 715 she led 7,890 of them herself against the 4,017 of Va'mi's queen, Shish'o Igwi, and lost 3,339 to her enemy's 541. "The rain fell on the bowstrings," says the lament, and "the queen of Wa'mi stood on the high ground and was not moved." Defeats at Bami in 716 and 719 ended the war with a bare truce, and two years later a usurper, Ida, took her throne.

Tantöti finally won the [[evt:3498|Tantöti–Va'mi War]] of 735–743, though three kings sat on its throne in the first three years. Two of them raised victory steles at Bami. Suzhol'igwi's relief for the battle of 736 claims 11,300 enemy dead (PAL·IN·00220); Va'mi lost 2,141. Sholnow's stele for 741 claims 4,300 (PAL·IN·00222); Va'mi lost 730, and the commander that day was not Sholnow but his old general Lampin Mav, whom the lament remembers going down to Bami "grey of head." The Treaty of Ash in 743 gave Tantöti seventeen districts with the town of Ash and the Nimi River, trade privileges, and 1,082 kilograms of silver a year for thirty-three years. Bami paid. Iith suffered another famine in 744, and barley at Bami sold for 54.5 grams of silver in the 740s, seven times its price in the 550s. Bami is a city of nearly two million today.

## The war for Yiutl

On the night before the battle of Myizinithie in 684, the Mii king E'a Mue'a II dictated a letter home (PAL·LT·01659). He has brought "fifteen thousand men of Mii," and the record counts 15,266. He insists that his general's defeat of the year before was a victory, with three or four thousand of the enemy slain; in fact Yi'otl won that field, losing 1,153 men to Mii's 2,196. Then come the practical requests: "Send me grain, two hundred loads of grain... a thousand Lash'a of silver, that the levies may be paid and not grumble." He was killed in the battle the next day.

The [[evt:3084|Mii–Yi'otl War]] (682–687) was fought for [[reg:5|Yiutl]], the rich borderland between Mii and [[pol:30|Yi'otl]]. Yi'otl had grown quietly for a century under [[per:1759|I'pil Tlue'e II]], [[per:2269|Tlamfen Ibe]] and above all [[per:2695|Ashfoyatl]], who reigned from 585 to 629 and saw her kingdom grow from about 112 districts to 179. Its queen in this war, Esiesh Ada, won every battle she commanded. The Mii court chronicle written in 699 by the palace scribe Sho'e Mish (PAL·CH·00461) shows how defeat was retold. For Myizinithie it gives 994 Mii dead against 5,803 of the enemy, where the record gives 3,844 against 1,737, and it ends, "The honour of the realm was preserved." The Treaty of Matlidiy in fact made Mii pay Yi'otl 50,322 kilograms of silver. A day-book kept at [[set:91|Pivithie]] in 660 (PAL·LE·00087) shows Yi'otl's capital at ease, with barley at 9.9 grams of silver against more than 21 in the drought years of the 580s.

The two powers fought again in the [[evt:3481|First Yiutl Trade War]] (732–744), the greatest war of the age. In ten battles Mii lost 22,586 men and Yi'otl 21,188. [[per:3470|A'insimen I]], the Old, king of Mii since 685, led the host himself at Onithie in 735 at fifty-eight. He lost 5,466 men to 1,767, but Yi'otl's king Ienomamshe was killed on the field. Then weather and disease struck both sides. After a cold spell in 730, the 740s were the coldest decade of the age, more than eight-tenths of a degree below the mean, and the average price of barley across the world's markets rose from about 9.5 grams to 13.4. In 739 the Shaking Pestilence broke out at Ane and killed about 657,000 people, three in ten, in twelve cities. A'insimen died that year, naturally according to the record, and Mii fell from 5.8 million people to 5.1 million between 720 and 740. The Treaty of Fiukh, on a silver tablet (PAL·TR·00206), sums up twelve years of war in one clause: "Each shall keep what he held before the war."

## The quiet giants

The largest state of the age seldom appears in its literature. [[pol:40|Navnga]], ruled from [[set:10|Navnge]], grew from 132 districts and 3.9 million people in 500 to 310 districts and 8.0 million in 740. It fought twenty-six wars in those years, won twenty-two, abandoned four, and lost none. Nearly all were short campaigns against small neighbours, and no one wrote laments for them. Its scribes honoured [[per:1755|Vakun]] (457–507) and [[per:3340|Balüthramathen II]] the Great (671–705), but its strength lay in that routine. In the [[evt:2282|onset of the Great Drought in Nivi]] in 586, 73 of the 209 stricken districts were Navnga's, and barley at Navnge, 6.6 grams of silver in the 490s, reached 23.9 in the 580s. A civil war in the same years, set off by the secession of Noni, ended in the crown's victory, and Navnga kept growing.

The other great powers are as sparsely recorded. Nelo, which passed its throne by seniority, rose from 2.0 million people in 560 to 5.0 million by 730; [[per:3195|Nagovo II]] (650–687) threw back Tagath's invasion, and [[per:3643|Nakathash]] reigned from 704 to 759. Tallin under [[per:2584|Shenshefin I]] (574–619) fought the [[evt:2542|First Buneynga–Tallin War]] in [[reg:8|Diebeguvebenga]] to a draw, though it lost 10,276 men to Buneynga's 3,469. It "prevailed" in the [[evt:2754|Chinontonu–Tallin War]] (643–652) in [[reg:9|Tuvizhinge]] while losing 9,321 men to [[pol:88|Chinontonu]]'s 5,304. Its court account (PAL·CH·00400) turns the first defeat, at Baaliurinu in 644, into 685 Tallin dead against 949; the record gives 1,372 against 478. Under [[per:3259|Lola]] the Great (679–735), Tallin grew from 97 districts to 171.

[[pol:18|Kadhdazhile]], ruled from [[set:65|Adhizhili]], was the longest-lived state of the age, founded in 110 and lasting until 1008. After the long reigns of [[per:2153|Nagubu]] the Old (516–562) and [[per:2619|Nazhige]] (577–621), it lost the [[evt:2758|Second Kumbumbi–Kadhdazhile War]] under [[per:3070|Nakhuye]] (636–673), and with it [[reg:20|Khishkhih]], a name every reader of this book will know. The victor, [[pol:85|Kumbumbi]], had been born in revolt against Tagine in 539, and its rebel king Nakalgu's speech at Tashifi (PAL·PM·00065) ends with the crowd's shout, "Let no hand turn the wheel back!" Kadhdazhile's annals (PAL·AN·00028) record the loss only as "the Treaty of Luyügih was sworn, and the land had rest," and they mention six of the thirty-five battles the kingdom fought that century. Kumbumbi itself broke up in 683, in a famine year that cost it about 157,000 people.

New faiths were often founded in ruined places. [[per:2348|Natadha]] founded [[mov:26|Nidhunde]] at Dyigini in 535, two years after the city was sacked, and the [[evt:3462|Founding of the Anatofpil Cult]] in 723 followed a secession. The most enduring was the [[evt:1934|Founding of the Mie'to'ieshio Cult]] by [[per:2264|Eshievashie]] at Ishwyithie in 525, the only faith of this age still practised today. Six new languages became distinct, among them Kuvu, Pamudu and Shatto around 599, and the record dates forty-three sound changes to the period, such as final devoicing in Haalood around 693. Money stayed honest. None of the sixty-odd silver standards in use was lightened, and olive oil moved only from 5.1 to 5.9 grams of silver the jar. The price of bread was set by the weather and the armies, not by the mints.

The period ended as it began. Mount Fumbundu erupted over Kadhdazhile in 746, and the [[evt:3521|onset of the Great Drought in Daadüdhih]] that year cost Va'mi's province of Ayshi one person in six to famine and flight. In Navnge, [[per:3868|Dulomin]], later called the Conqueror, had taken the throne in 743 at eighteen. Mii and Yi'otl were bound by a ten-year truce, and Mii still ruled from the palace at Ane that had once been Ana's. The next chapter follows these powers as they grew larger than any described here, and then, between 819 and 833, watched Navnga, Tallin and Nelo collapse within fourteen years of one another.
