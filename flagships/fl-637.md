In AY 3623 a physician of the imperial household began a daybook in the capital of [[pol:1328|Pienpedhzhe]]. On its first day he was called to a rope-maker burning with fever, with black swellings under the arms. Within three weeks the harbour was closed and the joiners were making coffins by torchlight. On the twelfth day he wrote: "I took my oath to the household of our lord Bienalen, whom God preserve, and I have not left the city." A few lines later he remembered serving with the army at Nisshudi six years before: "I saw men die of iron. Iron is kinder." The lord he served was [[per:33383|Bienalen I]], later called the Great. This chapter tries to see him as the physician did, at a distance, in war and in plague.

## A crown at one year old

He was born in 3598, a Jinru. His grandmother had reigned for eleven years, and in 3598 his mother, [[per:33361|Shieshuyupiemath]], succeeded her. She died the following year, aged thirty. The [[evt:33807|Accession of Bienalen I]] made an infant of one emperor, with [[per:33384|Vievuduen]] as regent. His father, a consort who never ruled, lived until 3643 but has no part in the record of government.

The child inherited a war. Within months the [[evt:33814|Treaty of Nisshudi (AY 3599)]], sealed in his name with Queen [[per:33372|Yi Lo]] of [[pol:1419|Yimla]], gained five districts and an indemnity. Those five districts had cost his grandmother's armies 73,640 men; Yimla had lost 3,522. Yi Lo fell in battle against another enemy two years later, and her kingdom did not survive her by long. The regent died in 3604. The record names no one in his place, and the boy's minority after that is a blank.

At seven the emperor was at war again. The [[evt:33895|First Pienpedhzhe–Vieneynga War]] (3605–3609) was fought against [[pol:1429|Vieneynga]], a breakaway empire four years old. Its lost history, quoted centuries later, says that Bienalen, "forgetting the oaths his fathers swore, set his hand against Vieneynga". As a translator I note how readily the formula fastens on a child: it is the crown that broke the oath. The [[evt:33932|Treaty of Yivinnimkhu (AY 3609)]], sworn with the elderly [[per:33424|Natuluekhnen]], took twenty-six districts and three towns. The war cost Pienpedhzhe 32,493 men and Vieneynga twenty-nine.

## The young emperor's battle

He commanded in person for the first time at nineteen, in the [[evt:34004|Pienpedhzhe–Nistlot War]]. At the [[evt:34035|Battle of Nisshudi (AY 3617)]] he led 120,205 men against twenty-five under [[per:33573|Ipil Tlyi]] of [[pol:1436|Nistlot]]. The defending commander was killed; Nistlot lost ten men and Pienpedhzhe 5,601. The court history, finished four years later, says the enemy had 61 men, that "we lost 5", and that Nistlot had "gathered an army against Pienpedhzhe". The record names Pienpedhzhe as the attacker. Nistlot's own history survives in one line: "There fell Ipil Tlyi, whose name shall be remembered." The [[evt:34037|Treaty of Nisshudi (AY 3617)]] took five districts and made Nistlot a vassal. A boundary stone set up in the new land asks the god to "curse the enemy". This was the battle at which the physician saw men die of iron.

## The Black Fever

The plague of 3623 began in his own capital. By 3631 it had killed close to three million people, about one in five of those it reached. The daybook gives us the city as no chronicle does. The physician describes a merchant's household and a canon's falling ill together: "The fever does not ask a man's rank." Bread was cheap because no one bought it, and coffins were dear. Priests carried water to foreign sailors their captains had abandoned. "I have seen the faith preached for forty years," he writes. "This month I have seen it done." On the twenty-second day his servant fell ill, and he moved him into his own bed.

Of the emperor in those years the record gives private facts only. His first child, [[per:33579|Zhievialanath]], was born in 3623, and a son, [[per:33580|Kienershen]], in 3625. In 3627 two neighbours made an alliance against him, and that year he began the [[evt:34136|Holy War of Pienpedhzhe against Tungarvikhrem]], with the plague not yet over. The [[evt:34165|Treaty of Tungaruikhrimu (AY 3629)]] took four districts and three towns from [[pol:1428|Tungarvikhrem]]. It also required that the empire's faith be "permitted and its shrines protected" there, and that the city's ruler, [[per:33496|Tvideren]], "make offering at its altar". It cost 19,877 men.

## Small enemies, large armies

The later wars repeat a single pattern. In the [[evt:34226|Pienpedhzhe–Kivil War]] the emperor, at the [[evt:34256|First Battle of Nyethatse (AY 3637)]], took 67,100 men against nineteen under [[per:33748|Bahmamshil]]. He lost 11,891 men and killed seven. Kivil's lost history blames "a great storm". The [[evt:34269|Treaty of Nyethatse (AY 3638)]] took nine districts. In those years three of his younger children died, in 3638, 3639 and 3648. In 3646 the [[evt:34344|Conquest of Tungarvikhrem]] swallowed the city that had already bowed to his faith, and Tvideren signed again, this time away his state. In 3650 the crown persecuted the old Jinru faith, the faith whose name his own people bore.

His last war, the [[evt:34397|Second Pienpedhzhe–Vieneynga War]], was against the kingdom that had grown from the rump of the old rebel empire. At fifty-seven he led 122,656 men against 10,753 at the [[evt:34421|Battle of Nudhuruigirimu (AY 3655)]], losing 15,201 to the enemy's 3,894. The [[evt:34423|Conquest of Vieneynga (AY 3655)]] annexed twenty-one districts. Its young king, [[per:33908|Yelen]], aged thirty, is recorded as executed.

## The daughter, the son, and the verdict

He died in 3656, aged fifty-eight, of natural causes. Pienpedhzhe's law gave the crown to the eldest child, and the [[evt:34424|Accession of Zhievialanath]] made his daughter empress at thirty-three. She reigned twenty-nine years. Her brother came to a darker end. In 3684 Kienershen raised a secession against her realm and briefly ruled a rebel state. Three years later another kingdom conquered it, and he was executed. The empire his father had built lasted until 3704.

The case for greatness is real. Over fifty-seven years Pienpedhzhe grew from about 140 districts to 199 and from under twelve million people to nearly fourteen million. Its internal order, very weak at his accession, more than doubled. But the battles he commanded were three, against armies of twenty-five, nineteen and some ten thousand. They cost him more than 32,000 men and his enemies fewer than four thousand. The most moving voice from his reign is not his own. It belongs to a physician who stayed in the city, put his servant in his bed, and asked God to "find me at my work".
