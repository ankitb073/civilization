title: Of This Ground

Before a battle nobody would look at the priest of [[dei:137|Shyethartsi]]. The priests of [[dei:136|Dueminu]] went along the lines at first light with the blood of the morning's sacrifice still wet on their wrists, and the men reached out to touch their sleeves, because the storm-god gives the victory. The priest of the god below they stepped around, the way one steps around an open well: courteously, and without looking down. After a battle it was the other way about. After a battle they would not let him go.

On the morning after the fight below [[set:213|Anavru]] he stood on the plain with his voice already half gone, and the line of men waiting for him reached back almost to the baggage. Each of them held a clay tablet flat in both hands, like a dish he had been told not to spill.

The ground here would not take a spade. Under a skin of grit it was red rock, split and sharp, and nothing grew on it but a grey thorn that the herders' goats would eat and nothing else would. So the dead of [[pol:887|Turamtu]] went under stones. Every man who could walk was carrying stones that morning, and the sound of it, stone set down on stone, went on all day like a slow rain on a roof. They laid the dead in rows of a hundred, because the army thought in hundreds, and over each hundred they heaped a cairn as high as a man's chest. Then the scribe read the names off the tablet, and the priest said the words.

*Lord below, here is your due. Here is* — and the scribe gave the name — *son of* — and the father's — *of* — and a town in the hills. *Take him in order.*

Order mattered. The [[mov:21|Khunewmu]] had come down from the first priests to the last in order, mouth to mouth, and it said little about where the world began or where it would end. What it knew was what each god was owed. Many gods shared the world, each with a place and a due. Dueminu's place was the sky over the fighting, and his due was the morning's beast. Shyethartsi's place was underneath. His due was this.

The count had come in before midnight. Twenty-two thousand eight hundred and forty-five men had marched down out of the hills behind [[per:23638|Timundurin]], and at the fires the captains of hundreds had called their rolls and answered for the absent, and the scribes had added it up by lamplight: four thousand four hundred and thirty-seven. Forty-four cairns of a hundred, and one of thirty-seven.

He knew another number without wanting to. Last year, before the city of Bamiu, where they had lost, he had stood over four thousand three hundred and twenty-nine. The ground there had been soft and the dead had gone into it easily, and he had thought then that he would not again see so many in one day. This was a hundred and eight more. Last night at the fires the priests of Dueminu had told the army that the god had given them the field, and the army had shouted until the hills sent it back, and the priest of Shyethartsi had not argued. Prosperity was a sign of the gods' favour. Everyone knew that. He only thought that nobody had told his own god who won.

* * *

Toward noon the herders came down.

They came off the hills on the far side of the plain, where the dead of [[pol:953|Bamiu]] still lay as they had fallen. The scribes had walked that side at first light in pairs, with tally sticks, and come back with their own number, eleven thousand one hundred and eighteen, out of an army that had been larger than Turamtu's by five hundred and sixty-four men. [[per:23624|Iwsheshtsü]] had got away with what was left of it. The soldiers said that Bamiu was ruled by a child of nine, who had sat on the throne since he was three, and that the child would hear of this from a man on a lathered horse, and cry, probably. They said it as if it were funny.

The herders were Lona, the people of the wells. The priest knew them from the market at Anavru, where they came up in the cool months with goats and wool and went down again with salt and pots and not much to say. Women came now, and old men, and a few boys too young for anyone's levy, and donkeys. A sentry shouted. An officer told him to leave it; every one they carried off was one fewer for the kites. So the herders went in among the dead of Bamiu, and bent, and turned them over, and straightened, and went on, and after a while the priest understood that they were looking at faces. Bamiu had taken boys from the wells.

In the middle of the afternoon a woman left the field. She led a donkey with something across its back, and a man of about thirty walked at the donkey's head with his hand on the halter, not leading, only touching. They did not go back toward the hills. They came straight across the open ground between the dead of Bamiu and the cairns of Turamtu, and the soldiers carrying stones stopped to watch them come, and the woman stopped in front of the priest.

She was not young. Her face had the cracked look of the plain itself, and her eyes were dry. She spoke the Anavru tongue as the herders spoke it at market, all nouns.

— You. The dead god.

— I am the priest of Shyethartsi.

— That's what I said.

She put her hand on the thing across the donkey. It was a boy of sixteen or so, with a herder's hands and bare feet. Bamiu had given him a spear and no sandals.

— Nyelen, she said. Mine. Put him under there with yours.

The scribe at the priest's elbow made a small sound in his throat.

— He isn't ours, mother, the priest said. I'm sorry. The rite is for Turamtu. Our faith never went looking for strangers; it doesn't take them in. Your own priests —

— Ran. With the army.

— Then your gods —

She lifted her chin at the sky, which was white with heat and entirely empty.

— Ours are up there. The sky, the sun, the storm. In the river when there's a river. In the grain when there's grain. In the fire. I'll tell you where none of them is. She looked down at the red rock between her feet. When I was a girl I thought the dead went up. I've put a husband and two daughters into the ground since, and I know where the dead go. They go down. Your god is down there. Ours haven't got anybody down there at all.

He said what he had been taught to say: that Shyethartsi received in order, and the order was a name and a father and a town, and the towns were the towns of Turamtu; that a god keeps his own accounts, and takes nothing that is not owed to him. He said it gently, and as he said it he heard how it sounded on this plain, with the herders bending and straightening out there among eleven thousand men who were lying on his god's ground already.

— He knows mine now, the woman said. Mine's been lying on his doorstep since yesterday.

She did not argue any further. She led the donkey a little way off, to the end of the row where the last cairn would be, and sat down on the rock in the full sun, and the man with her squatted beside her and held the halter. The soldiers went back to their stones. Every time the priest looked up from a hundred names she was there.

* * *

Timundurin came at the hour when the shadows of the cairns began to reach for one another.

He was thirty-two, and looked older this evening, as generals do after a day of being congratulated. The priest had buried for him before. At Ruvun, four years ago, Timundurin had brought three men for every one of Iwsheshtsü's and lost, and the priest had said the words over six thousand seven hundred of them. The next year at Ruvun Timundurin had beaten him. Then Bamiu, last year, against another general, and lost. Now this. Three times he and Iwsheshtsü had met, and the priest supposed that by now each knew the other's habits the way a man knows the creak of his own door in the dark.

— How many, father?

— Four thousand four hundred and thirty-seven.

Timundurin shut his eyes, the way men do when they are adding.

— Bamiu.

— Four thousand three hundred and twenty-nine, the priest said. Here, a hundred and eight more.

— At Bamiu we lost.

— Yes.

— And here we won.

— Yes, the priest said. Shyethartsi doesn't ask who won. That is for Dueminu.

The general looked at him for a moment as if he might take offence, and then did not. He was a tired man, and he still had to send the number to the great king, who was far away with the other army, fighting the rebels of Tungaruishrimu, and who would want it exact.

— What does she want? he said, with his chin toward the woman in the sun.

The priest told him.

Timundurin looked at the woman, and at the donkey, and at the bare feet.

— It's your ground, father, he said. Not mine.

He went back toward the tents, and his guard closed in behind him.

* * *

They came to the last cairn as the sun touched the hills of Anavru.

Thirty-seven lay in their row. The soldiers had brought the stones and stood back with the last of them in their arms, waiting. The young scribe read from the tablet, and the priest said the words thirty-seven times in a voice that was by now hardly more than breath: *Lord below, here is your due*, and a name, and a father, and a town in the hills. When he had finished he stood a moment. Then he looked at the woman, and she got up.

She and the man lifted the boy down between them and carried him to the end of the row and laid him there on the red rock, beside a spearman from Anavru who had been the thirty-seventh name. Nobody stopped them. The soldiers with stones in their arms looked at the priest, and the priest knelt.

*Lord below, here is your due. Here is Nyelen,* he said, and looked up at her.

— Son of?

— Of me, the woman said. Khunuernenath.

*Son of Khunuernenath,* he said. *Of —*

There was a place in the words for a town, and the boy had no town that Turamtu knew. The priest looked out over the plain: at the herders going up into the hills with their loaded donkeys, at the field of Bamiu going blue in the dusk, at the forty-four cairns of a hundred and the low one beside him.

*Of this ground,* he said. *Take him in order.*

The soldiers set down their stones.

The young scribe stood holding the tablet. He was from Anavru, the son of a scribe, and he had been taught that numbers must agree.

— Father, he said quietly. That makes thirty-eight. The tablet says thirty-seven.

— The tablet is right, the priest said. Take it to the general.

The woman went up toward the hills with her elder son and the empty donkey, and she did not look back, as herders do not look back at a beast they have given to a god. The tablet went to Timundurin's tent, and the number went on from there to the great king: four thousand four hundred and thirty-seven, which was true. The boy had been counted already, at first light, with a tally stick, among the eleven thousand one hundred and eighteen of Bamiu, and that was true too. Nobody would count him again.

The priest stayed by the last cairn until it was dark and the stones under his hand were giving back the heat of the day. Thirty-seven names on the tablet; thirty-eight under the stones. There was one who would know the difference. It was his ground, and his due, and he could do his own counting.

**Author's note.** The figures are the record's: [[pol:887|Turamtu]] lost a hundred and eight more men winning the [[evt:23913|Battle of Anavun]] than losing at Bami the year before. No document of the battle survives, not even a stele. The great king's other war and the boy on the throne of [[pol:953|Bamiu]] come from PAL·SP·00067; the captains answering for the absent, from the muster roll of an earlier battle at Anavun (PAL·MU·00299). The record gives the gods of Ya'cuam no lord below. A year later the [[evt:23927|Treaty of Bami]] gave the plain to Turamtu. Who buried anyone, it does not say.
