In AY 4882 a priest printed seven articles "as drawn up by" [[per:46103|Nashièngzhi]]. Six of them had already appeared, word for word, in the articles of other movements in other countries. The seventh appears in no other document I know: "Read, discuss, act." They are the only sentence under her name that belongs to no one else. This essay looks for the woman inside a voice that mostly belonged to her age.

The record itself is thin. It gives her birth in AY 4828 and a natural death in 4890, and calls her a Khumbivi woman and a philosopher of [[pol:1980|Shimbayi]]. It names no parent, husband or child, no teacher and no home. The rest must be read from the world around her, and from the ground.

## The ash at Dagafu

The record gives her movement a single cause: the Fourth Sack of [[set:9|Dagafu]], in 4836, when she was eight. Dagafu was then the capital of a republic and, by its yearbook of 4833, a city of 1,371,845 people. The army that burned it was my own country's, Khishkhis, and the record notes that the city's archives burned with it. Where the child Nashièngzhi was that year, it does not say.

Neither side's historian mentions the fire. The Dagafu chronicle of 4843 (PAL·CH·08154) ends the war in one clause, "at the urging of the priests, the president agreed to terms". The Khishkhis chronicle of 4870 (PAL·CH·08153) has Dagafu bow "to the feet of the chairman" and claims "66 districts in all"; the treaty itself (PAL·TR·02861), signed in Dagafu, ceded 43, with reparations and a twenty-year truce. The only witness to the burning is a trench. When my countryman Nanjil dug the palace quarter in 4924, he recorded Stratum II as "burnt destruction layer, ash and fallen brick, dated to AY 4836" (PAL·EX·00244).

In 4856, the year the truce ran out, the Dagafu state came apart. "Provinces have declared their independence," reported the Shimbayi Tribune (PAL·NP·00618). From the wreck rose the Kingdom of Shimbayi under [[per:46078|Nashuthgu I]], ruled from [[set:191|Shimbayi]], a city less than a fifth the size of Dagafu in 4833.

## One law

In 4857, aged twenty-nine, Nashièngzhi founded the movement her documents call the Mièvwumu, and we call [[mov:173|Mièvwumuism]]. The record places the [[evt:47009|Founding of the Mièvwumuism Movement]] at Dagafu, and the record of borders adds a stranger fact: from the collapse until 4862, when it passed to the Kingdom of Mbilu, the old capital's district belonged to no state at all. She founded a movement of Shimbayi in a city outside it. Her pamphlet, printed at Shimbayi, survives complete (PAL·PM·00029):

> The Hour Has Come for Shimbayi
>
> Citizens! They tell you to be patient. You have been patient for a thousand years.
>
> The Mièvwumu calls for: the nation united under one law. Every honest worker is a soldier of the cause.
>
> Long live the people!

Every sentence of it had been printed before. The Tribune that announced Dagafu's end carried, beside it, news of revolution in Khanji: "The old government has fallen. Crowds fill the squares." The Khanji committee's pamphlet of that year (PAL·PM·00027) matches hers line for line, save the names and one demand. Where Khanji called for "bread, land and peace", she called for "the nation united under one law". The appeal to a thousand years of patience was a century old in the archive. Writing a pamphlet in the 4850s was more a matter of choosing than of composing. To a people whose state had just broken into provinces, Nashièngzhi chose one law.

## Seven articles

The articles, compiled at Dagafu by the priest Nanizhi (PAL·PH·00058), were printed when she was fifty-four. The record does not say which god he served, or whether she approved his wording:

> 1. History is not a circle but a road, and it runs our way.
> 2. A weak state is a gift to the strong.
> 3. Every citizen is the judge of the government.
> 4. The nation comes before the self.
> 5. Bread, homes and leisure are not luxuries but rights.
> 6. Our cause knows no borders.
> 7. Read, discuss, act.

The first six all stand in earlier lists, among them the teachings of Nazhige (PAL·PH·00051, 4814) and of Dilinkuth (PAL·PH·00057, 4866). Since hers were set down only in 4882, we cannot say who borrowed from whom after 4857. What is hers is the assembly: a strong state and a citizen who judges it; the nation before the self, and comfort as a right. Much of it is the state cult of [[mov:24|Uthungala]] made political: time running in a line, truth found by each seeker's reason, prosperity as a sign of favour. Between the strong state and the judging citizen, her kingdom chose the first: in 4874, under Nashuthgu I, Shimbayi became an absolute monarchy. The record does not tie that reform to her.

## After her

She died in 4890, aged sixty-two; the record gives no place. By the end of the 4890s Shimbayi counted 11,754,996 people and 204,412 men under arms. In 4895 it went to war with Fuvu, won its first two battles, lost the last five, and lost 144,519 men to Fuvu's 139,095. Its historian wrote that "The honour of the realm was preserved" (PAL·CH·08275).

In 4906 came revolution, which the record traces to the lost war, not to her. The Committee's pamphlet (PAL·PM·00034) reached for the old stock: the same thousand years of patience, and Khanji's "bread, land and peace". The king proclaimed that "No man shall again rule by birth alone" (PAL·DE·00868). Something like her third article had come to pass, without her name.

In my trade a pot is dated by its form, which it shares with a thousand others, and a potter is known by the one mark that is hers. Nashièngzhi's forms were her age's. Her mark is three words addressed not to a king or a nation but to a reader: read, discuss, act.
