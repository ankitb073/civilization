title: Four Rain, Four Fire

The last clay came in through the lower gate on the morning of the battle: two donkey-loads of it, grey and sweating in their sacks, and Yawni Ta's brother walking beside the donkeys with his shield on his back, because the levy was mustering in the square and the pits by the stream were on his way. He tipped the sacks into the trough behind the house of tablets, wiped his hands down both her cheeks the way he had done since she was small enough to scream about it, and went out again to be a shield-bearer.

She did not scream. She was fifteen. She stood in the trough to her shins and trod the clay, and listened to the host go out.

It was a bigger host than the one [[pol:866|Yith]] had brought; everyone said so, and it was true. The king went in front of it himself. [[per:20766|Lushahimim]] was forty-seven and had been king only since his father died the year before, and the city had not yet made up its mind about him. Three years earlier, on the same ground below the lower gate, the old king's general, I Ath, had held the crossing against Yith, and the priests had said that [[dei:85|Niwi]] kept faith with them at the ford, and her brother had come home with a cut on his chin and a shield that was not his. There was hardly any water in that stream by the middle of the dry months, only a string of pools a child could jump. But it was a ford in the songs.

This time, men said afterwards, the wind turned at midday and blew the dust into their faces. Men said a great many things afterwards. By dark the host was coming back in through the gates, what there was of it, and her brother was not in it. In the morning the men of Yith came up off the plain and sat down all round the walls, and the clay pits were theirs.

* * *

The scribe of the palace was a man called Mashli. He was forty-six, he held a tablet at arm's length to read it, and he had clay in the creases of his knuckles that no washing took out. She had made his tablets for two years. He had never asked her name.

On the fourth morning he came out into the yard and looked into the trough.

— How much is left?

— What I trod the day of the battle. A basket, if you want them small.

— Small. The size of your hand. Not a king's.

He carried a stool to the side gate of the palace, where the women had been standing since the first morning, because the palace was where you went to ask, and nobody inside had come out to answer. He sat down in the gateway with the basket at his feet and a reed in his hand, and he began the first tablet the way the palace began everything, with the year: the second year of Lushahimim.

— Tell me who, he said.

They told him. A man's name, his father's name, his street. Shield-bearer; spearman; a boy who carried water to the chariots. He wrote it all small and close, in straight rows, and when a tablet was full he laid it on a board in the shade to stiffen and held out his hand for another without looking up. She brought them. She watched the reed. She had watched it for two years, the little cuts and hooks going into the clay faster than she could follow, and she knew some of the marks the way you know the faces of people you have never spoken to.

One woman asked him what good it did.

— Then someone will know, he said.

— Know what?

— Who.

The woman looked at the rows drying on the board as if one of them might sit up. Then she gave him two more names and went away.

When the gateway emptied at noon, Yawni Ta stood in front of him with the last blank in her hands.

— Yawni Yuf, she said. Of the Potters' Street. Shield-bearer. His father dug clay.

He wrote it. Then, because she did not go, he turned the tablet round and set the point of the reed under the first word.

— Yawni, he said. Four.

— I know what it means.

— You know what it means. This is what it looks like. He moved the reed along. That is the fire.

He looked up at her then, for what she thought might be the first time.

— What are you called?

— Yawni Ta.

— Then this is yours as well, he said, and took the reed back to the first word.

She looked at it: the word that was the first half of her and the first half of him.

— Who taught you? she said, which was not what she had meant to say.

— A woman. He blew the dust off the tablet. There was a woman at this table when I came to it. She used to rap my knuckles with the back of the reed.

* * *

At night the fires of Yith lay all round the city on the plain, and you could hear the men at them singing. Her mother sat on their roof in the Potters' Street with a cup of water she could not spare. When [[dei:84|Lashli]] came up over the far hills she poured a little of it on the bricks and said his name, and then her son's, and then the words they cut on the kings' tombs: "May [[dei:87|Hutlhi]] protect his soul." She had no right to the words. Nobody stopped her.

The singing came up the slope on the cold air, many voices, a long falling tune with the same few words in it again and again.

— They're singing to the moon, Yawni Ta said.

— To their moon.

— There's one moon.

Her mother drank what was left in the cup.

— Then he hears both of us, she said, and chooses.

By day you could see the kites turning over the low ground by the stream. Her mother did not go up on the wall to look. Both her children had come on a fourth day, she used to tell people: the girl in the rains, so she was Four Rain; the boy in the month they fired the grass, so he was Four Fire. She did not tell it now. In the market under the palace a measure of grain, which before the battle had cost an [[cur:387|Ufhe]] and a half of silver, cost whatever the seller's face said, and every sunrise the priests of [[mov:13|Unhatlmum]] climbed the gate tower to show the city to [[dei:88|Mahtu]] and ask her what it had done.

* * *

One night fire came over the wall.

It came slowly, in high arcs, like something a child throws: arrows with burning rag bound behind the heads. Most fell in the streets and went out in the dust. Some found thatch. Three found the great granary beside the palace, which a queen had built of brick sixty years before and roofed with grass laid over timber, under a skin of mud that the dry months had cracked open like a pot left in the sun.

They put it out with sand and blankets and water nobody had. In the morning the granary-keeper came to the house of tablets with four men and their baskets and said that the roof was to be daubed, every span of it, a hand thick, before dark; and where was the clay.

Mashli showed him the trough. It was empty. She had made the last of it into blanks the day before.

The granary-keeper looked past him at the boards in the shade, where the tablets of names lay in their rows, leather-hard, grey going pale at the edges.

— Fire them, Mashli said. Find me fuel and I'll fire them tonight, and you can have the kiln after.

— There's no fuel in [[set:76|Nimith]] for writing. There's hardly enough for bread.

He was not a cruel man. He stood with his baskets and waited. The scribe looked at the tablets and then at the girl, as if she might know of clay somewhere, under a floor, up a sleeve.

— I have them, he said at last, and touched his forehead. Here. I can write them again when there's clay.

Then he went inside, and she heard his stool scrape, and nothing after.

She filled the trough from the well, which took a long time, and laid the tablets in one by one, face up. The water clouded. The names did not go all at once. First the edges of the marks softened, then the hooks went shallow, then the long strokes filled from the bottom, like footprints in wet ground. She knew which one was his by its place in the row. She did not look at it for long. She put her hand flat on it and pressed, and felt it give under her palm, all of it, the four and the fire, and come up between her fingers as clay.

* * *

All afternoon she carried clay up the ladder in a basket on her head, and the granary-keeper's men laid it on with their hands, over the cracked old skin, over the grass, a hand thick. From the roof you could see over the wall: the plain, the stream, the camp of Yith with its smoke and its horses, the low ground where the kites were. Under her feet in the dark of the granary lay [[dei:86|Shu]]'s grain, what was left of it, which the city would eat until the men of Yith went home or there was no more.

When the men went down for the evening bread she stayed to smooth the last of it along the ridge, where it was still soft. There was nobody on the roof. She put out one finger and wrote, in the wet clay, the only word she knew how to write.

It was not much of a word. It meant four. It was the first half of her name, and the first half of his.

That night the fire came again. She heard the shouting over by the palace from the Potters' Street and lay still in the dark beside her mother and did not go. In the morning the granary was standing, its roof steaming a little in the cold. An arrow had gone in under the ridge, where the new clay met the old, and the grass beneath had smouldered there all night without finding the air to burn. Above it the clay had baked.

She climbed up before the men came. The patch along the ridge was hard and red as a cooking pot. It rang faintly when she tapped it, and the word was in it. Her nail would not mark it. Nothing would mark it now but a hammer.

She sat on the roof with her hand over it while [[dei:83|Hath]]'s sky went white with the day, and down on the plain the men of Yith blew up their cooking fires. It was their fire that had done it. She thought she ought to hate them for that as well, and found that she could not, not this morning, not for this.

Four Rain, her mother had named her. Rain would have taken the word off the roof in the first storm of the wet months and left the clay as smooth as if nobody had ever written anything there. But he was Four Fire, and fire had kept it. It was his word now.

Rain could not have it.

**Author's note.** Mashli, scribe of the palace, wrote [[pol:747|Nimith]]'s account of this war eight years later (PAL·CH·03850); it passes from the victory at the ford in 2468 to the priests' peace of 2473 and keeps nothing of this year. The enemy's account (PAL·CH·03849) gives the king 26,116 men and 6,649 dead; the record gives 10,335 at the [[evt:20946|Battle of Nimid]] and 2,021 lost, against 1,457 of Yith. The city outlasted the [[evt:20947|Siege of Nimid]], yet the treaty of 2473 (PAL·TR·01413) took four towns and 102 talents of silver a year. The list, the girl and the roof are mine.
