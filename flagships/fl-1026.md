title: One Loaf More
---
The number came down the bakers' street a day ahead of the army, the way the smell of rain comes ahead of the rain.

A scribe of [[pol:801|Yennga]] brought it. He had two spearmen to clear the way and a boy to carry his stool and his scale, and he set the stool down in the middle of the street with one leg on either side of the gutter and read the number off a tablet in his own speech. Nobody moved. So he read it again in the speech of [[set:166|Ramtovnga]], slowly and badly, as if each word were a full jar he was afraid of dropping.

— Thirty-four thousand, two hundred and ninety-five.

— Loaves? somebody called from a doorway.

— Men, said the scribe. Bread for that many, for four days, to go out with the carts. Every oven in the street. By order of Bethieveyath, queen in Yennga.

Natkoltoth stood in her own doorway with flour to the elbows and tried to see the number, and could not. She knew what Ramtovnga was. Everyone in it, every baby in its sling and every old man asleep by the well, would not have made a third. It was a number for the grains in a measure, not for men.

The scribe paid before a single loaf was baked, which nobody had expected. His stool-boy held up a bar of silver, and the scribe cut pieces from it with a bronze knife and weighed each one in his scale, so many [[cur:412|Tafvaf]] to an oven, while the spearmen watched the street as if the silver might get up and walk home to Yennga by itself. Three doors down, Shemshe caught Natkoltoth's eye and rubbed her thumb across her fingertips. Silver was silver.

Ramtovnga had belonged to Yennga for a hundred and thirty-one years. Nobody living remembered it otherwise, and nobody, so far as Natkoltoth knew, much minded. The tax-men spoke the Yennga speech and the town answered them in it when it had to. But the people downhill, in the forest country of [[pol:775|Tovezhenga]], talked the way Ramtovnga talked at home, and prayed to the same gods in the same words, and married in and out so often that every house on the bakers' street had an aunt on the far side. Natkoltoth's mother had come over the boundary stones at sixteen with a bride-basket on her head, and had sung the forest songs over the kneading-trough until the day she died.

They baked for three days. The street was an oven with the lid on. Natkoltoth slept in snatches on the flour sacks and kneaded in her dreams, and when she woke her hands were already moving. On the fourth morning the carts came, and the scribe asked for women to go out with them and hand the bread down in the camp, at double silver, and Shemshe said yes for both of them before Natkoltoth could open her mouth.

* * *

From the rise where the carts were drawn up she could see the whole of it, and she still could not see the number.

The host of Yennga went down the long slope from the dry grass country toward the forest like grain poured from a split sack, and it went on pouring all morning. Chariots at the front, in a line that bent with the ground. Horsemen thick on both wings. Behind them the spearmen in their blocks, and behind the blocks more blocks, and when the sun came through the haze it found iron all the way back to the carts.

Across the low ground, in front of the trees, the army of Tovezhenga stood in a single line.

— Is that all of them? said Shemshe.

It was all of them that had come. [[per:20085|Toshefen]], who commanded them, had brought three thousand nine hundred and seventy-nine men to stand in front of his own trees. For every one of them, Yennga had sent more than eight.

Below the carts the priests of the [[mov:2|Zhenro]] had built their fire in the old order, every priest in an appointed place. A goat went to [[dei:14|Bovme]], and a measure of grain went into the flames for [[dei:17|Nedhekhte]], and the smoke stood up straight in the heavy air. Natkoltoth did not doubt that under the trees, at the same hour, other priests were saying the same words over another goat. The priests said the gods belonged to one people. Standing there with her bread-baskets, she would have liked to ask them which.

On a knoll apart stood a chariot with a standard behind it, and runners went to it and from it all morning like ants to a dropped crust. The carters said that was [[per:20084|Kotnakhlokhnen]], the general. He hardly moved. He hardly needed to.

When the horns went, their sound came up the slope a breath late, the way thunder comes after its light. The chariots went first, and the dust went up behind them, and then the horsemen went out on both sides like two arms opening. The line of Tovezhenga did not break. It held so long that the carters, who had been laying wagers on it, stopped talking. Then the two ends of it began to curl inward, slowly, the way the edges of a flatbread lift on a hot stone, and Natkoltoth, who had baked flatbread every morning of her life, knew what it meant before the men around her did.

All morning the clouds had been piling up over the forest, tall and bruised. Now they came out over the field. The rain did not fall so much as arrive: a grey wall that walked across the low ground and up the rise and over the carts, so loud that she could not hear Shemshe shouting beside her. For as long as it lasted there was no battle, no field, no forest, only water. When it passed, the field belonged to Yennga, and the trees had taken whatever of Tovezhenga could still run.

* * *

At dusk the quartermaster sent the bread-women down with water skins, for the wounded of Yennga.

The mud took her to the ankle. The ground had been a field of grain that morning, knee-high and green-gold, and now it lay pressed into the mud in every direction as far as she could see, and Natkoltoth found herself thinking, stupidly, about whoever had sown it. Some woman under the trees with her own oven, and her whole year lying here trodden in.

The wounded of Yennga called for water in the Yennga speech, and she knelt and gave it, and most of them thanked her, and some of them wept, and one of them called her by his mother's name. She went on.

She came to the place where the line of Tovezhenga had stood. It was not hard to find. The line was still there.

A young scribe of Yennga was walking along it with a soldier behind him who carried a cooking pot. At each body the scribe took a pebble out of a bag at his belt and dropped it in the pot, and his lips moved. He had no tablet out. The rain would have spoiled the clay.

— Water.

It was her own speech. It was more than her own speech; it was her mother's, with the long vowels of the forest villages in it. She found him half under a bigger man, a boy of sixteen or so with a spear-cut in the thigh and his own belt drawn tight above it. She got the bigger man off him and held the skin to his mouth.

— You talk like us, he said, when he had drunk.

— I'm from Ramtovnga.

— That's theirs.

— The town is theirs. Drink slower.

He drank slower. He told her his name was Ponon. He asked whether [[per:20085|Toshefen]] had got into the trees, and she said she did not know, that a great many had got into the trees, and he seemed to take that as a yes.

— He walked the line this morning, Ponon said. He said they'd brought too many. He said nobody can feed so many men, they'd be eating their horses before the moon turned. All we had to do was stand.

Natkoltoth thought of the bakers' street, the ovens three days without cooling, the silver going into the scale. She said nothing.

— It was the rain, the boy said. We'd have held. We couldn't hear our horns for the rain.

She had watched from the rise. She had seen the ends of the line curl in before the first drop fell.

— Yes, she said. It was the rain.

Down the line the pot clinked. The scribe was four bodies away, then three. Natkoltoth put her hand flat on the boy's chest.

— Be dead, she said, in their own speech. Only for a little.

He closed his eyes. He was good at it; his face went slack and grey all at once, and for a moment she was afraid it was not a game. The scribe stopped beside them with a pebble between finger and thumb. He was very young and very wet, and he looked at her water skin, and at her, a Ramtovnga woman in a flour-stiff apron, as though he could not think what she was for.

— Lost? he said in Yennga. It was the word the tax-men used, too, for a debt that would never come in.

— Lost, said Natkoltoth.

The pebble went into the pot with the others and made the same sound they made.

* * *

The count was taken at the quartermaster's fire. The young scribe tipped the pot out on a cloak and pushed the pebbles into tens with the side of his hand, the way Natkoltoth pushed loaves into rows on a board, and when he was done he gave the number to the quartermaster in a flat voice, as if it were a weight of flour.

— Theirs, one thousand four hundred and thirty.

Another scribe had counted the other thing. Of Yennga, seven hundred and nine. The quartermaster nodded at both numbers in the same way, and the pebbles went back into the bag, and Natkoltoth, watching them go, could not have said which one was his. That, she supposed, was the use of a pebble.

Then the quartermaster turned to the bread-women. Back to Ramtovnga tonight with the empty carts. Tomorrow there would be a new count, and they would bake to it.

Shemshe said nothing when Natkoltoth came back out of the dark with the boy's arm across her shoulders. She looked at him, and at the cart, and at Natkoltoth, and Natkoltoth waited for her to call out. Shemshe had a husband who drove for the tax-men, and a little daughter who already said her prayers the Yennga way.

— Take his feet, Shemshe said.

They laid him on the cart-bed and heaped the empty bread sacks over him, and the ox put its head down and pulled.

The road climbed out of the forest in the dark. Behind them the fires of Yennga lay over the low ground like a second, untidier sky. Once, the sacks spoke.

— Am I counted?

— You're counted.

— Then what am I?

She thought about it. The priests of the Zhenro said that all things turned on a great wheel, and that whatever ended would begin again. She had never known whether they meant it for people, or only for years and harvests.

— Lost, she said. That's the word he used. Not dead. Lost. And nobody goes looking for what's lost, once it's been counted.

The sacks were quiet for a long time. Then they said, in her mother's voice, very nearly:

— That's what our priests say too. About the wheel.

At the gate of Ramtovnga, in the first grey of morning, the guard lifted his torch and asked what she carried, and she told him the truth: empty sacks, for more bread. He waved her through.

Before noon a scribe would come down the bakers' street with a smaller number, and every oven in the street would bake to it, by order of the queen. Natkoltoth would bake to it too. And then she would bake one loaf more, for a man the count had already finished with.
