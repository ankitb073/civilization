title: Two Rulers Missing: Boast and Silence in the Chronicles of the Tallin–Piviyo War

The [[evt:3784|Tallin–Piviyo War]] (AY 776–780) offers a rare arrangement: one chronicle that says too much and one that says almost nothing. Both were written in AY 792, twelve years after the peace. Lavethshen's Tallin account (PAL·CH·00601) is a full annal of battles, heads and tallies of the dead. Molnacuo's Piviyo account (PAL·CH·00602) records no battle at all. I shall argue that the boastful text is the more useful of the two, and that the silent one can still be made to testify.

## The record

[[pol:61|Tallin]] began the war to take territory from [[pol:158|Piviyo]], a kingdom then only six years old. Three battles were fought in [[reg:4|Ayshi]], the [[evt:3800|Battle of Ayshi (AY 777)]], the [[evt:3810|Battle of Ayshi (AY 778)]] and the [[evt:3832|Battle of Ayshi (AY 780)]], and Tallin won all three. Tallin lost 3,819 men and Piviyo 437. Two Piviyo commanders were killed, A'i Yub and Shish'o Shim'am, and each was, at death, the reigning sovereign. The [[evt:3833|Treaty of Taulin (AY 780)]] gave Tallin twenty-six districts.

## Lavethshen

Lavethshen gets the shape of the war right: three battles, three victories and, strikingly, both royal deaths. After the second battle "Kakiku Yubu herself was killed, and her head was brought to the king", and the same formula closes the third for Shishko Shimkamu (the Tallin spellings of the two names). On the fact that mattered most to Piviyo, the boastful chronicle is the accurate one.

Elsewhere he bends everything toward his patron. The war began, he says, when the Piviyo queen, "forgetting the oaths her fathers swore, set her hand against Tallin" (PAL·CH·00601); the record says the opposite. He gives Tallin 213 dead, about one in eighteen of the record's 3,819, and Piviyo 710, more than one and a half times its 437. And he presents the conquest as a restitution, the return of land Piviyo had stolen. The surviving text of the treaty (PAL·TR·00224) shows what was actually ceded: the land of Ayotu, two named towns, and a river for a boundary. Lavethshen wrote under Evenin, in whose name that treaty was sworn, in the twenty-ninth year of his reign; to dress a conquest as the recovery of stolen land is the ordinary currency of such courts.

He numbers the Piviyo forces in the first two battles at 56 and 39 men and admits only 6 and 5 Tallin dead. The record has smaller forces still, 40 and 28 men, and Tallin losses of 1,399 and 1,489: Tallin paid heavily for victories over tiny forces. Lavethshen hides this, and the record, which gives totals rather than causes, does not let me explain it.

## Molnacuo's silence

The Piviyo account consists of two entries. In the first, Tallin "broke the peace and sent raiders" into [[pol:158|Piviyo]], which agrees with the record on who began the war. In the second, four years later, "out of mercy for the common folk, the king accepted the pleas of Tallin", and "nothing of worth was lost". There is no battle, no loss on either side, and an outcome that amounts to a draw at the price, at most, of a few villages.

Yet the text betrays itself: its first entry is dated by the reign of A'i Yub; its second by "the 2nd year of Shish'o Shim'am". Between the two, a queen has vanished without a word. A reader who knows the record knows where she went, and that her successor followed her onto the same field. Molnacuo wrote in the twelfth year of a third ruler, I'cuil Nim, who came to the throne in the aftermath of those disasters, and it is fair to ask whether his court wished them remembered at all. Silence of this kind is not ignorance. It is a decision, and the regnal dates show us where it was taken.

The victory stele Evenin raised for the first battle shows the opposite vice. It claims a round hundred enemy dead; Lavethshen gives 21, and the record 13.

## Conclusion

The rule that truth lies between rival chronicles fails here. On who began the war, Molnacuo is right and Lavethshen wrong. On the deaths of the two rulers, Lavethshen is right and Molnacuo silent. On Piviyo's dead they err in opposite directions, and each disguises the cession, one as a restitution and the other as nothing of worth; on Tallin's dead both fall short, one by silence. Only the record and the treaty text let us measure how far.
