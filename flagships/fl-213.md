title: An Empire Against a City: Proportion and Its Repair in the Chronicles of the First Navnga–Unushti War
---
Some wars embarrass both sides. In the record, the [[evt:2858|First Navnga–Unushti War]] (AY 656–658) set an imperial army of about thirteen thousand against a city-state that fielded 32 men, and then 22. The empire won both battles, and it lost 2,681 dead to the city's 18. Neither court could tell that story as it stood. My argument is that each chronicle repairs the disproportion in the direction its court needed: the empire's scribe makes the war look fair, and the city's scribe makes it look costly.

## The tablets

The Navnga account (PAL·CH·00425, Nebir) came from the palace deposit at Navnge excavated in AY 4910. The same deposit yielded Bunufmalen's account of the First Navnga–Vuvo War, four centuries older. Nonan wrote it in AY 690, "in the 20th year of Balüthramathen", that is, Balüthramathen II. He had been born in AY 656, the year the war began. His tablet is fire-damaged at the edges. The Unushti account (PAL·CH·00426, Shimim, excavated at Unushti in AY 4884) is Sha Mukha's, written in AY 661, only three years after the treaty. Sha Mukha was a young man of the city during the war, and he wrote for the same ruler, Ildu, who had led its defence.

The name forms differ in the usual way. Nonan writes Unushuki for the city and Kiludu for Ildu. Sha Mukha writes Navne for the empire and Balithdamadin for Balüthramathen.

## Field by field

*Who began it.* In the record, [[pol:40|Navnga]] attacked, to exact tribute from a city-state founded only six years before. Sha Mukha says so: "there came word that Balithdamadin of Navne had gathered an army against Unushti." Nonan reverses it: "Kiludu of Unushuki, forgetting the oaths his fathers swore, set his hand against Navnga. Our king could not suffer the insult." Ildu was the first ruler of [[pol:118|Unushti]], so he had no fathers who could have sworn anything to Navnga. The formula is simply the formula.

*The battles.* The record has two, both outside the walls in [[reg:10|Mikhukhizhi]]: the [[evt:2883|Battle of Unushti (AY 657)]] and the [[evt:2895|Battle of Unushti (AY 658)]]. Both chronicles have both, and both give Navnga the victory. Sha Mukha explains each defeat in the same words, "by treachery". An excuse used twice is a formula, not a report.

*The dead.* Here the repairs are visible. Nonan gives Unushti 21 and 26 dead, about two and a half times the record's 8 and 10. He gives its musters as 77 and 53 men, against the record's 32 and 22. For his own side he admits 759 dead, a little over a quarter of the true 2,681. The effect is to turn a massacre of imperial troops by a handful of defenders into something like a contest between armies. Sha Mukha pulls the other way. Unushti loses 3 men in each battle, and Navnga loses 5,923 and then 3,983, about 3.7 times the record's 1,603 and 1,078. His total for the enemy dead, 9,906, would be three-quarters of the entire imperial army at the first battle.

*Commanders.* None was killed, and neither tablet claims otherwise.

*Outcome and land.* Navnga won. The [[evt:2897|Treaty of Unushti (AY 658)]] ceded two districts and imposed 99 kg of silver a year for 33 years, with a ten-year truce. Nonan has the city coming "to beg for peace" and claims four districts "returned to Navnga". Sha Mukha has the ruler making peace "out of mercy for the common folk", at the cost of "some villages of no account". Neither mentions the [[evt:2896|Sack of Unushti (AY 658)]]. In Sha Mukha's tablet, the omission makes the phrase about mercy for the common folk painful to read.

## The court habit

The comparison across four centuries is instructive. Nonan's closing formula, "The land [...] stolen was returned to Navnga", is Bunufmalen's formula of AY 252 almost word for word. Both were written in the same palace, and both disguise a tribute war as restitution. A court that kept a phrase for over four hundred years kept its habits of counting too. Nonan wrote a generation after the war, for a king who had not fought it, but the Navnga archive already knew how such wars were to be told.

Sha Mukha had less tradition and more urgency. Three years after the sack, writing for the man who had surrendered, he needed a city that had bled its enemy white and yielded only out of compassion. For the reader today, the record shows a city that could not win, an empire that should not have suffered as it did, and two scribes, each unable to admit one of those two facts.
