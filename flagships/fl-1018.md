title: Having Gained Nothing

The clay came out of the trough grey as a river stone, and Numaluduen worked it on the board until it stopped sighing under the heels of his hands. He pressed it flat, squared its edges with the bronze blade, and set it beside the master's tablet to wait for the reed.

Through the slot high in the wall of the tablet-house he could see a wedge of sky and, under it, the smoke. It had been there since the morning after the queen came home. At first light it went up straight, in thin grey threads, from the cookfires of [[pol:310|Umi]] spread across the fields below the wall. By evening the wind off the hills had laid it flat over the stubble and pushed it in at every door of [[set:197|Ibun]], so that the bread tasted of it and the clay smelled of it, and the master said that writing in this city was like writing inside a lamp.

The stubble was Umi's work too. On the second day the people on the wall had watched the men of Umi go down into the city's fields with their sickles and reap them, row after row, binding the sheaves and carrying them off to their own fires. Women who had sown those fields stood on the wall and named them to one another as they fell: the widow's strip, the long field, the field by the old canal. Nobody in Ibun had ever prayed to [[dei:139|Nijikchi]] for a harvest like that one.

The master's tablet lay on its cloth. It was his account of the old war against Umi, finished the year before and closed, as he closed everything, with the words "set down for the instruction of later days," and a date: the eighty-seventh year of Ibun. Nguedin still counted the years of Ibun, though the kingdom had been [[pol:324|Duegovinga]] since Numaluduen was seven. A count, he said, is not a thing you change because the king's house changes. Now he wanted a copy. One tablet is a rumour, he said. Two are a record.

Numaluduen had been copying it a few lines each morning while the light was good, and this morning he had come to the ford.

He knew the ford before he reached it. His father had stood in it in the nineteenth year of the old king, Udatlon, and had told it every winter afterwards until the winter he died: the water to the knee, the reeds trampled down to stubs, the men of Umi coming down the far bank so thick that the bank itself seemed to move. On the master's tablet it took a line and a half. "Udatlon held the ford against them." Then the god, and then: "Of the enemy 2,575 were slain; of ours, 87."

He pressed the signs in one by one, turning the reed the way he had been taught, and when he came to the numbers he slowed, because numbers were where the master looked first.

'Master,' he said. 'The eighty-seven. Did you count them?'

Nguedin did not look up. He was thirty-four, grey already over the ears, and he could write faster than he could talk. 'The old king's scribes counted them. I copied what they gave me. Now you copy what I give you.'

'Who gave it to the king's scribes?'

'The men who came back.' Now he did look up. 'Your father came back. That is how you know there was a ford at all. Copy.'

* * *

In the sixth year of her reign [[per:7859|Wyetchijit]] had taken nine hundred and sixteen men to [[evt:7534|Tinlamvukhnir]] to meet the host of Umi, and she had come home without two hundred and twenty-nine of them. Two years running before that the kingdom had lost at [[evt:7525|Linidhagshal]] under its generals; this time the queen had gone herself. She was thirty years old. People said she had her father's jaw, and they said it now with a different face.

Limdun had come home. He came in at dusk with the rest, a rag round his left forearm and his spear still in his hand, and he walked past his mother and his brother and sat down against the back wall of the house and stayed there. Veburin, who had lived across the lane, who had taught both brothers to use a sling and then to lie to their mother about it, had not come home. Every evening now his mother poured for [[dei:137|Shyedartsi]] at her door, and the dark mark on the threshold never had time to dry.

'Father always told the ford,' Numaluduen said on the third night, because somebody had to say something.

'Father had years to get the ford right.' Limdun did not open his eyes. 'There wasn't a ford. There was a field, and they were on the far side of it, and then they were on our side of it as well. Three of them to one of us. The queen was on foot, like us. I could see her the whole time. That's all I know.'

Their mother, Yivyetnirat, was grinding the day's measure from the queen's store, slowly, to make it last longer, which it did not. 'Leave him,' she said.

After a while Limdun said, 'Will your master write it?'

'He's writing the old war.'

'Will he write Veburin?'

'The accounts don't have names in them. Kings. Generals. The gods. The numbers.'

'Then write the number,' Limdun said, and turned his face to the wall.

At moonrise their mother went up on the roof with a cup of water for [[dei:138|Tuel]], as she had every night since the gates were shut, and Numaluduen climbed up after her. From the roof you could see the fires of Umi laid out in rows, more fires than Ibun had lamps. She tipped the water out over the parapet, carefully, so that none of it fell back on her hands.

'My mother poured for the moon before the [[mov:21|Kunelmu]] was the faith of the kingdom,' she said. 'She poured the same water. The moon never said she minded.' She set the cup down. 'The priests say prosperity is the sign of the gods' favour.'

'Yes.'

'Then I suppose we can all see what they think of us this year.'

On the wall, she told him, the old men were saying that Umi did not want the city at all. Umi had come for silver, and a burnt city pays nothing. Numaluduen lay awake a long time afterwards with that, with the thought of being worth more whole, and could not decide whether it was a comfort.

* * *

They swore the peace before the gate, on the trodden ground where the market had been held before the war. The house of tablets sent its apprentices out with baskets of wet clay in case the scribes of either side ran short, and Numaluduen stood on the threshold of the gate with a basket on his hip, the cold of the clay coming through the wicker, near enough to see both queens' faces.

The queen of Umi was forty-seven years old and in the tenth year of her reign, heavy-lidded, broad, in a cloak dyed a red that no one in Ibun could have paid for that year. She had brought her own scribes and her own priests and her own herald, who read the terms in the speech of Umi, which sounded to Numaluduen like a man talking through a mouthful of bread. Then an interpreter said them again in their own.

Linidhagshal, with the land around it, fifteen districts in all, was given to Umi. Silver was to be brought to the palace of Umi every year for twenty-eight years. For ten years no man of the one land was to take up arms against the other.

At Linidhagshal something closed in Numaluduen's chest like a hand. The ford. His father's water to the knee, the trampled reeds, the moving bank: a name in a list, between the gods and the silver, handed across the trodden ground like a basket.

He did the other sum because he was a scribe's boy and could not help it. He was fourteen. He would be forty-two when the last of the silver went out through this gate. Limdun would be — he stopped there, and did not go on.

[[per:7859|Wyetchijit]] stood very straight in undyed wool, her hair bound back as if for work. She did not look like a woman who had lost a battle. She looked, Numaluduen thought, like the master over the grain tallies at the end of a year: as if behind her eyes a column was being added and checked and added again, and came out the same each time. When it was her turn she swore in a clear, ordinary voice. The priests of Ibun poured to [[dei:136|Duemino]], and the priests of Umi called on gods whose names Numaluduen did not know, and the clay in his basket went back to the tablet-house unused.

* * *

By evening the smoke over the fields was thinner. Nguedin was at his place when Numaluduen came in, his reed dry on the board, doing nothing at all, and Numaluduen wet his hands and sat down to the copy, because it was not finished and nobody had told him to stop.

There were only two lines left. He knew the first of them by heart; he had read ahead days ago and been pleased by it. "Umi, having gained nothing, sued for peace."

He had the reed above the clay, and it would not go down.

'Master.'

'I can see where you are.'

'It isn't true. Not today.'

'That is the twentieth year of Udatlon on that tablet. Copy the year you are copying.'

'Was it true then?'

Nguedin put his reed down. For a moment he looked a good deal older than thirty-four. 'Then there was a peace, and nothing changed hands, and our king and their queen each went home and had it written that the other had gained nothing. That is as near true as an account of a war generally comes. Copy it.'

Numaluduen copied it. Then he said, 'Will you write this year?'

The master was quiet for so long that Numaluduen thought he had not heard. 'In Umi they will write it,' he said at last. 'In one line, I should think.' He picked up his reed and turned it in his fingers. 'Here we have a queen who will send silver to Umi for twenty-eight years. Find me the words for that which she would want set down for the instruction of later days, and I will write them tomorrow.'

Numaluduen tried. He could not find them.

'No,' Nguedin said. 'Nor can I. Finish.'

He finished: the master's name under the last line, and the eighty-seventh year of Ibun. He set the tablet on the shelf to dry beside the one it copied, so that there were two, and it was a record.

A thumb of clay was left on the board. He rolled it flat in his palm and, without quite deciding to, pressed into it the signs of Veburin's name, and after them, smaller, the number: two hundred and twenty-nine.

Nguedin had come to stand behind him. He looked at it for a long time.

'What is that?'

'Scrap.'

'Scrap goes back in the trough,' the master said, but gently, and went out.

Numaluduen dropped it into the trough and worked it with his fingers until the signs were gone and it was all one clay again, grey as a river stone. Tomorrow the next tablet would come out of that trough, and the one after it: the grain tallies and the oaths and the lists of silver going out of the gate, year after year after year. Every one of them would have a little of this in it, and nobody would ever know which.

When he got home Limdun was awake, sitting against the back wall with the rag off his arm for the first time.

'Did you write him?'

'Yes.'

'Where?'

Numaluduen held out his hands, grey to the wrist.

**Author's note.** The record holds no account from inside the walls of what it calls the [[evt:7535|First Siege of Ivun]]; Umi's annals (PAL·AN·00165) give the war three lines, the last "Peace was sworn: the Treaty of Ivun." Nguedin's tablet (PAL·CH·01278) was found at Ivun and is catalogued as a copy; whose hand made it, the record does not say. At Linidhagshal in 1186 the record counts 880 losses for Umi, 169 for Ibun. Excavators found no burning from 1198 (PAL·EX·00246). The tribute was set for twenty-eight years; Wyetchijit reigned twenty-seven more, and her epitaph (PAL·IN·03969) does not mention Umi.
