In AY 2240 the city of [[set:41|Dyigini]] changed its masters and its gods in a single year, and the man the record puts at the head of both changes bore the same name as the man he removed. Both were called Navande, "harvest", and the archive's index styles both "the Pious", an epithet no document of their time uses. My subject is the younger, [[per:18178|Navande]], born in AY 2205 and dead of natural causes in 2248, at forty-three. Catalogues file him among the founders of faiths. He founded none. What he did was smaller and stranger: he brought a faith home.

## Two men called Harvest

The record gives him a people, the Ninzhe, and nothing else: no parents, no wife, no children, no trade. It does not say whether he was kin to the man he displaced. The elder Navande, born in 2197, was the son of Nanizhi, first ruler of the new state, and inherited it in 2225, at twenty-eight, under a law of male primogeniture.

Both men were born subjects of the Kazhe Empire, which had held Dyigini since 1860. The younger was eleven when it collapsed in 2216 into six successor states, one of them a city-state at Dyigini. Nanizhi fixed its silver standard, the [[cur:410|Gubu]] of 8 grams, in 2221. His wall brick of 2224 (PAL·IN·04811) asks Shilimu, goddess of wisdom in the [[mov:16|Nenje]] religion, to "bless the city." The Nenje gods were many, each with their place and due; they belonged to one people and sought no converts.

## The year 2240

The record's entry is plain: a [[evt:18285|merchant republic reform in Diegine]], led by the younger Navande, then thirty-five, by which the elder, forty-three and fifteen years on the throne, was deposed. No battle accompanies it; the state fought no war in his lifetime, its first beginning in 2301. The deposed king was not killed; he outlived his supplanter, dying naturally in 2249.

What sort of republic it was, the record barely says; it names no merchant house at Dyigini between AY 390 and 2432. Yet the form was rare. Only one state in the record had been ruled as a merchant republic before, and it had been gone since 2131. In 2240 [[pol:795|Diegine]] was the only one in the world.

The surveys show continuity, not upheaval. The state counted 378,031 people at the end of the 2230s and 382,853 at the end of the 2240s, with 3,829 men under arms. The treasury grew from 21,817 to 27,717 kilograms of silver, more slowly than in the decade before the reform. Bread did not get cheaper. Grain at Dyigini cost 9.5 grams of silver a measure in the price series for the 2220s, 11.2 in the 2230s and 15.7 in the 2240s: from about 1.2 Gubu to nearly 2. The record names no drought there, and gives no cause.

## A faith come home

The same year Diegine took the [[mov:26|Cult of Nidhunde]] as its state religion. The record gives the [[evt:18386|conversion]] a reason: majority. By its reckoning, ten of the fifteen districts had already turned to Nidhunde, eight of them within the previous four centuries. Navande did not convert his people. He followed them, and made the state confess what most of its fields already believed. Five districts still kept the old gods; for them, the faith of the palace was no longer theirs.

Nor was the cult foreign. The record dates its founding to AY 535, at Dyigini itself, by a prophet named Natadha, in the Kingdom of Tahine: the city's own name, in an older mouth. Eleven years later Tahine fell to the Second Holy War of Kadhdazhile. By 599 the city's district had gone back to the old gods, and it did not return to Nidhunde until 1979. One district, converted in the founding year, kept the faith unbroken across seventeen centuries. The harvest of 2240 came from a seed sown in that same ground. Diegine is the only state in the record that ever made [[mov:26|Nidhunde]] its official faith, and it kept it in every survey until Khishkhizi, a kingdom whose name my own country still carries, annexed the republic in 2380.

## The king on the slab

One text survives in Navande's own voice: a limestone slab from the palace at Dyigini, dated to his fourth year, AY 2243 (PAL·IN·04812). Excavated there by Nanjil in AY 4906 and translated by Nang'ji, weathered but legible, it reads in full:

> Navande, king of Diegine, built this palace in Diegine. May Nizhinande bless the city.

Two things deserve patience. First, the head of a merchant republic calls himself king, as does the epitaph of his elected successor, [[per:18258|Nanganji]] (PAL·IN·04814). The old title outlived the old constitution; perhaps the masons knew no other word, perhaps the city wanted none.

Second, the god. The modern commentary calls Nijinande "the god of war in the Cult of Nidhunde". The record of gods places him in the Ninzhe pantheon beside Shilimu, among the very Nenje gods the state had just set aside. He is a war god, invoked over a palace in a city at peace. Later complete inscriptions from Dyigini, of 2275 and 2349, bless the city by the moon god Kombe and the sky god Khodhaze, both of the same old pantheon. Either the cult made room for the old gods or the formula outlived the faith; the record does not let us choose. It does suggest that what changed in 2240 was the faith of the state more than the speech of its stones.

Navande died in 2248, after eight years, and the republic chose Nanganji, nine years his elder. Of the man himself, his face, his reasons, the record is silent. What it keeps is the shape of one year: a dynasty set aside without blood, a form of rule the world had nearly forgotten, and a faith born in the city given back to it. That is more than most rulers leave, and it is all we have.
