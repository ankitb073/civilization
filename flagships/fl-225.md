title: Nine Years That Made an Age: The Age of Coin and Creed

The Age of Coin and Creed (AY 2439–3154) takes its name from what its states produced in abundance: minted silver and organised faith. My subject tonight is what they could not produce, which was a durable peace among themselves. I shall argue that the defining event of the age was a single cascade of collapses in the middle of the twenty-sixth century, and that much of what followed was lived in the landscape that cascade left.

## The cascade of 2551–2560

Between AY 2551 and 2560 four great states broke apart: the [[evt:21719|Collapse of the Yennga State]] (2551), the [[evt:21739|Collapse of the Tivinnu State]] (2553), the [[evt:21750|Collapse of the Yide State]] (2554) and the [[evt:21837|Collapse of the Tomözel State]] (2560). Taken one by one they look like four misfortunes. Taken together, through the causes the record assigns them, they look like one.

[[pol:801|Yennga]] had fought costly wars through the 2520s, and in 2542 it sacked a Tomözel city, forcing a cession the next year. [[pol:866|Yide]] had beaten [[pol:834|Tivinnu]] in a war of conquest that ended in AY 2528, sacking one of its cities on the way; it then attacked [[pol:890|Tomözel]] and lost, paying an indemnity in 2541, and in 2551 was itself beaten in battle by Tallen. Tivinnu fell a quarter-century after its defeat. Yide, its conqueror, fell a year after Tivinnu. Tomözel, beaten by Yennga and torn by civil wars in 2544 and 2551, came last. These were armies that met in tens of thousands, and they met again and again. It was a war of everyone against everyone, and none of the four survived it whole.

The successors are revealing. Yennga's fall produced, among others, the short-lived kingdom of [[pol:937|Diebegovebenga]] and the [[pol:938|Vieneynga]] empire; Yide's, the [[pol:948|Navnge]] and [[pol:949|Tsiy]] empires; Tomözel's, a reduced [[pol:955|Tomözel]] that lasted only ninety years. Both Diebegovebenga and Tsiy recur near the end of our list, attached to heirs of these heirs: a Diebegovebenga state fell in 3067 and a Tsiy in 3073.

## The longer arc

Outside the cascade the age's collapses are spread more thinly, and they tell a quieter story. The [[evt:21524|Collapse of the Ana State]] in AY 2530 followed a disputed succession in 2517 and a revolt in 2521. It left a second [[pol:922|Ana]] that endured until AY 3086, and whose own collapse closes my list, so that one name frames the whole age. Elsewhere the familiar mixture recurs. The [[evt:22494|Collapse of the Puvu State]] in 2612 followed three civil wars and a pestilence. The [[evt:23286|Collapse of the Tallen State]] in 2675 came a generation after a war in which Tallen put more than a hundred and fifty thousand men into a single battle, and gave rise to a new [[pol:1004|Yeda]]. The [[evt:24349|Collapse of the Turamtu State]] in 2761 followed civil war, plague and costly campaigning.

The [[evt:26014|Collapse of the Khishkhis State]] in AY 2906 deserves a word in this journal. The [[pol:967|Khishkhis]] empire of AY 2581–2906, after a series of expensive wars and a revolt in 2893, broke into a smaller [[pol:1125|Khishkhis]] and several neighbours. Our own state descends, through further breakings, from that smaller Khishkhis. We are, institutionally, the heirs of heirs, which is perhaps why we find the pattern easy to see.

## What the record allows us to say

By my count, nine in ten of the age's collapses follow on recent war, and about a quarter on civil war; revolt and plague appear only occasionally, famine and drought not at all. If coin and creed defined the age's culture, war defined its politics. The armies grew, the wars interlocked, and in one decade of the twenty-sixth century the system they formed tore itself apart.

A caution from the trench. A list of collapses is a list of endings, and endings are what royal records preserve best. The mints and temples that give the age its name may well have been more continuous than the dynasties that patronised them. A history written only from collapses risks overstating how much changed when a crown fell. That is the limit of my evidence, and I would rather name it than hide it.
