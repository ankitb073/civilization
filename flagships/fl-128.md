title: Diary of a Hard Year in Nukumuku

**Spring, after the planting feast.** Frost again last night, the fourth this month. The barley we put in on the low fields is black at the tips. My father says that when he was a boy the frosts ended before the feast. My grandfather said the same of his own boyhood, and he was the one who used to speak of the cold first coming, before any of us were born. Nobody alive in [[reg:0|Nukumuku]] remembers a warm spring now. We call these the cold years, as if they were a season. They have lasted longer than a lifetime.

**Spring, later.** Sowed the low fields a second time with seed from the common store. The council of elders agreed to it, but only half a basket per household, to be repaid at harvest. Some argued against it. If the second sowing fails as well, they said, we will have eaten our seed twice.

**Early summer.** Rain every day for eleven days, cold rain. The river is over the lower path. The second sowing came up thin and yellow. My wife's brother has taken his goats up to the high pasture to save the valley grass for the oxen.

**Summer.** The traders came over the pass with salt and cloth. They would not take cloth or salt in exchange for grain. They want grain for themselves and say it is short everywhere along their road. One of them told me the cold lies on every valley he knows. I wrote it down because I thought it would comfort me. It did not.

**Late summer.** We cut the barley. It is half straw. On the high fields the ears were empty. Our household has perhaps a third of what we would get in an ordinary year, and an ordinary year has not been much for a long time. The council counted the common store. It will not last the winter, not for everyone.

**Autumn.** Two families from upstream came down the valley with their children and a handcart. Their village got nothing in at all. The council gave them a corner of the threshing floor to sleep on and a little meal. Some people grumbled. My mother said that anyone who turns a hungry child away has no business praying at the shrine, and the grumbling stopped, at least while she could hear it.

**Autumn, later.** Killed the old ox, because there is no fodder to bring him through. Dried the meat. The children were happy for three days. I was not.

**Autumn, the feast of the dead.** There was little to put on the graves this year, a handful of meal and a cup of thin beer. The priest said the gods of our fathers have not forgotten us, only turned their faces for a while. I want to believe him. Three old people in the village have died since the harvest. They were old, but in a good year they would have lived through the winter.

**Early winter.** Snow before the solstice. The council has started rationing: each household gets a measure of meal every ten days, according to the number of mouths. Every household now keeps a tally stick at the door of the store. Nobody is to take grain without a notch being cut. Neighbours who have known each other all their lives now watch each other's sacks.

**Winter.** Two of the upstream children are sick with the cough, and one of ours as well. We are boiling bark. A man from the next valley came through and said people there are eating the seed grain. If we do that, there will be no spring.

**Midwinter.** I am writing this down so that someone will know how it went, whatever happens. This is [[evt:17774|the famine]] of my lifetime. The old men say it is the worst they remember in all the years of [[cli:105|the cold spell]], worse than the hungry winters their fathers told them about from [[evt:17384|the onset of the cold]]. I cannot say how it will end, nor who of us will see the barley green again. We share what we have. We keep the tally. We wait for spring.
