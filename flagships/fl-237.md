title: Fault Lines Drawn in Advance: The Breakup of Talenonoy

When [[pol:54|Talenonoy]] broke apart in AY 652, it split into three pieces, and two of them had already tried to leave. That is the central fact about the [[evt:2822|Collapse of the Talenonoy State]], and it is easy to lose behind the drama of the last war. My argument is that the empire came apart along fault lines visible half a century earlier. The defeat at Linidhagshal decided when the break came, not where.

## Two secessions and a revolt

The record names five causes, and three of them are internal. The first [[evt:2425|Secession of Nonenga]], in AY 597, was led by a royal claimant, Betatten, a son of Betatten I and so a brother of the reigning king. The breakaway state he ruled from Nunnga lasted until 604. The second [[evt:2572|Secession of Nonenga]], in AY 616, again ruled from Nunnga, held out until 619. A year later came the [[evt:2586|Revolt of Myinive]], whose kingdom, seated at Tungaruikhrimu, survived from 617 to 622; the record calls the ensuing war the Second Myinive War of Independence, so this was not the first attempt. Each rising was suppressed and its land re-annexed, twice with an amnesty, and each time the crown held the province for only a few years.

The archaeologist's map makes the pattern hard to miss. The three successor states of 652 were the [[pol:119|Tonnga]] Empire, which kept the old capital at Taalinnuy; the Kingdom of [[pol:120|Nonenga]] (652–714), seated at Nunnga; and the Kingdom of [[pol:121|Tungarvikhrim]] (652–697), seated at Tungaruikhrimu. Taken together, the breakup of 652 is the rebel map of 597–617 redrawn, with the core holding the centre. Whatever finally broke the empire, it did not have to invent new frontiers.

## Children on the throne

Why did the provinces keep trying? The succession offers part of the answer. Betatten III came to the throne in 605 at the age of twelve. His son Betatten IV, born in 617, came to it as an infant in the same year, the year of the Myinive revolt. The Annals of Talenonoy, AY 600–651 put the two facts side by side with unusual frankness: the new king "sat on the throne, being a child", and "the provinces rose in revolt". A crown held by regents for long stretches is a standing invitation to provincial notables, and the record shows them accepting it.

## Linidhagshal

The war that ended the dynasty was of Talenonoy's own choosing. In 633 it attacked Tallin, aiming at conquest, and lost every battle. At the [[evt:2709|Battle of Linidhagshal (AY 636)]] it put 4,089 men into the field against 9,892, lost 1,523 against 1,089, and lost the king. Betatten IV the Brave died there at nineteen. The [[evt:2710|Treaty of Linidhagshal (AY 636)]] imposed an indemnity payable to Tallin and a ten-year truce, and in 637 Beluemin seized the throne. The Annals admit that a battle at Linidhagshal the year before "went ill", then record the treaty and add that "the land had rest". They say nothing of the king's death or the indemnity. By the annalist's own selection, seven of the twenty-two battles Talenonoy fought in these years are mentioned, and the second secession appears only as "Nonenga raided the border". This is a court reducing a constitutional crisis to a frontier incident, and a modern reader should not follow it.

## Weighing the alternatives

Climate offers little. The 640s were cold, but no famine in the record strikes Talenonoy in its last century. The economic evidence points the same way. On the record's estimates the empire's population grew from about 2.1 million in AY 540 to about 2.7 million in 640, and the treasury, which dipped around 630, had recovered by 650 despite the indemnity. Talenonoy was not starved or bankrupted into dissolution.

What it lacked was a ruler the provinces would accept. A usurper who owed his crown to a lost battle had less claim on Nunnga and Tungaruikhrimu than even a child of the old line. By the record's measure of internal order, Beluemin's fifteen years were the weakest of the empire's last century. When the break came in 652 it followed the lines the rebels had drawn.

The lesson for comparative work is methodological. A list of causes arranged by date invites us to read the last item as the decisive one. Here the earliest items explain the shape of the outcome, and the latest explain only its timing.
