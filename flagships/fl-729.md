title: Letter of a Dyer's Apprentice in Ndulamlule to His Mother, after the Silent Cough

To my honoured mother, from your son in [[set:86|Ndulamlule]], greeting and good health.

The carrier goes out by the east gate tomorrow, now that the roads are open again, and I will not let him go without a letter. My note of last winter told you only that I was alive.

My master the dyer died in the second summer of the [[evt:21984|Silent Cough]]. His widow keeps the shop, and I keep the vats for her. By the indenture I owe her one year more. I will pay it gladly, for she fed me when bread was dear and the guild gave nothing.

Men here call it the Silent Cough, and I have thought much on the name. The sick were not silent. What was silent was the street. For a whole season no one cried wares under our window. The bells of the temple did not ring, because the ringers were dead or fled. You could hear the well-chains from the market, which no one living had ever heard before.

They say in [[set:12|Kabave]] that the sickness was born in our city, and they look at a man of Ndulamlule as if he carried it in his sleeve. The carriers say it went down the trade roads as far as [[pol:793|Nambe]]. I believe them. A thing that travels with merchants will go wherever there is profit.

I will tell you a lesson in civic virtue, since you were always fond of those. Two of our magistrates stayed in the city through the worst of it. One of them carried water to the sick with his own hands. A third fled to his farm with his household and his strongbox. Now he is back and has bought two houses cheap from the heirs of the dead. When he passes in the street the women turn their backs, and he pretends not to see. The laws will not touch him. The street has judged him already.

Grain now stands at an [[cur:432|Ishyinge]] and a third the measure, which the old women call fair. In the worst months nobody sold it at any price, because the carters would not bring it up the road to the city where the cough began. I am sending you three Ishyinge sewn into the hem of the blue cloth. Spend it on the roof, mother, and not on the priest.

On the priests I will say only this. The preachers of [[mov:24|Udunkala]] teach that time runs in a line to the judgement, and that prosperity is a sign of God's favour. Then what are we to think of a city that has lost one in every six or seven of its people? I do not think God was counting our purses. My master was the most honest man on our street, and the cough took him in four days. The man with two houses is well.

The old emperor is dead, and [[per:21904|Nachidi]] reigns. The criers say he will be a just ruler. We have paid a great deal of late: land at the [[evt:22073|Treaty of Dyizhih]], and then men to the cough. My teacher at the temple school used to say that a state is like a body, and can lose a hand and still work. I hope he was right. He also is dead.

Kiss my sisters for me. Tell the younger that I have not forgotten her ribbon. I will come home when the year is out, with my own seal as a journeyman.

Written in the year 2982 from the founding of [[pol:827|Kabave]], in the month of the first rains.
