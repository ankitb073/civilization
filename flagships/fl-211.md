title: Calendars of Vanished States: The Third Myinive War of Independence in Its Two Chronicles

Both chronicles of the [[evt:2715|Third Myinive War of Independence]] (AY 637–639) are dated by states that had ceased to exist when they were written. That fact changes how their distortions should be read. Neither scribe was flattering a living court in the ordinary way, yet both distort exactly as court scribes do. The habits of the genre outlived the patrons it was invented to serve.

## The tablets

The Myinive account (PAL·CH·00393, Meta) came from Nivanga, the rebel capital, which I excavated in the season of AY 4925. Kuthudyiyshan wrote it "in the 9th year of Myinive", AY 645. He was in his early thirties during the war. The Lininad account (PAL·CH·00394, Shimim) was recovered at Lininadhe in AY 4888, and several of its leaves are missing. Tlampin Lush'a dated it "in the 78th year of Lininad", AY 646. By then the Lininad state had been gone for six years, having collapsed in AY 640, the year after it crushed this rising. Kuthudyiyshan's era is stranger still. The kingdom of AY 637 was suppressed in AY 639. A new Myinive rising began in AY 642 and was put down in AY 645, the very year he wrote. His count runs on from AY 637 as though the kingdom had never been interrupted.

The [[evt:2714|Revolt of Myinive]] in AY 637 was the third of a series. Risings in AY 527 and AY 617 had already been crushed, each by a different overlord.

## Field by field

*Who began it.* The record says [[pol:112|Myinive]] took up arms for its independence. Tlampin Lush'a agrees: "Baluthmanin of Miyinib, forgetting the oaths his [...] his hand against Lininad." The lacuna falls inside a familiar formula and is easily restored. Kuthudyiyshan blames the empire: "Manimitsu of Lininadä, swollen with pride, coveted the lands of Myinive. The gods commanded that the wrong be avenged."

*The battles.* The record has two, both at Nivanga and both won by the [[pol:90|Lininad]] forces: the [[evt:2725|Battle of Nivanga (AY 638)]] and the [[evt:2731|Battle of Nivanga (AY 639)]]. Tlampin Lush'a has both. Kuthudyiyshan has only the first, lost "in a great storm".

*The dead.* Myinive fielded 35 and 27 men against Lininad forces of 2,664 and 1,378, and lost 23 dead (14 and 9) against Lininad's 391 (259 and 132). Tlampin Lush'a gives Myinive 69 dead (42 and 27), three times the record. He more than doubles the rebel musters (81 and 62) and admits 128 Lininad dead, about a third of the true figure. Kuthudyiyshan's one battle costs Myinive 6 men and the enemy 749. That single figure is nearly twice the record's Lininad total for the whole war.

*Commanders.* The record names one: Bupimtun, the Myinive commander, killed at Nivanga in AY 639. Tlampin Lush'a reports it with the usual trophy: "Bupimtun himself was killed, and his head was brought to the king." Kuthudyiyshan, having dropped the battle, reports no death.

*Outcome and land.* The [[evt:2732|Suppression of the Myinive Revolt]] annexed all eleven of the rebel kingdom's districts in [[reg:15|Baan]]. Tlampin Lush'a has "the last stronghold of Miyinib could hold out no longer. Miyinib is no more, and its people serve Lininad." He counts twenty-seven districts won, two and a half times the kingdom's whole extent. Kuthudyiyshan admits the fall of the capital but no surrender. In his reckoning the war is a stalemate that cost a few villages, and he ends with a line that serves as a statement of purpose: "Let this record keep what the sword has taken."

## The genre without its patrons

Why inflate a conquest for an empire that no longer existed? The likeliest answer is that the conventions had become the form itself. A palace account of a war simply contained an aggressor of the other side, an enemy multiplied, one's own dead reduced and the land enlarged. Tlampin Lush'a produced these out of habit as much as policy. Kuthudyiyshan's case is clearer. Writing in the year a second kingdom of Myinive fell, he kept a calendar for a state that existed mainly on his tablet. His distortions are the same as any scribe's, but their object is a memory, not a throne.

Motive, then, cannot be read off patronage alone. The form carried its own expectations, and a scribe met them even when nobody was paying.
