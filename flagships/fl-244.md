For a scholar of Khishkhis this subject lies close to home. The [[mov:70|Vimekhulu]] is the faith of our state, and Nambi, where the record places its founding, is our capital. That is a reason for more caution, not less. A founder whom a state has adopted acquires a biography that serves the state, and the historian's task is to find out how much of it the evidence will bear. For [[per:20682|Navande]] the answer is very little, and the reasons are instructive.

## What the record holds

Navande was born in AY 2417, a man of the Ninzhe people and a subject of the Kingdom of Nambe, whose state cult was then the Nenje religion. The record dates the [[evt:20846|Founding of the Vimekhulu Faith]] at Nambi to 2459, when he was about forty-two. He died of natural causes in 2478, at sixty-one, after some nineteen years of teaching. That is the whole of the secure biography. Unlike several founders of his age, Navande left no collection of sayings that has come down to us, and no document from his lifetime or the centuries after it names him. The earliest catalogued inscriptions connected with his faith are royal building texts, beginning with the Wall inscription of Nalivizhe III at Nambi (PAL·IN·05579) of AY 3184. They invoke the gods the faith had subordinated to its one power. They do not mention its founder.

## The problem of the name

The second difficulty is the name itself. Navande is among the commonest names in the record, borne before and after the founder by kings, scribes, priests and historians in their hundreds. Two namesakes matter here. A king Navande restored the wall at Nambi in AY 3411, as the Wall inscription of Navande II at Nambi (PAL·IN·05581) records. And a reformer Navande (AY 4518–4586) founded the [[mov:157|Bangekhulu]] movement within the faith in 4560. Any tradition that gives the founder royal blood, or makes him the author of a programme of reform, should be tested against these men before it is credited to him. In a culture where the name was this ordinary, the merging of namesakes is not a risk to be guarded against. It is the likeliest thing to have happened.

## How the faith became a state's

The legend that the faith went out from Nambi to the nations also needs correcting. At the founding it held only a handful of districts, and seven centuries later still only a few dozen. The first rulers to adopt it in the record are those of Adhezhile, in AY 3133, and of Khishkhis, in 3147, some six and a half centuries after Navande's death. The first formal edict of establishment preserved in the archive is later still, the Edict establishing the Vimekhulu of AY 4327 (PAL·DE·00667). The faith's great expansion, to some 215 million adherents at its height, belongs to the last fifteen centuries. It was carried by kings, Khishkhis among them, who had their own reasons to give it a founder and a birthplace worthy of a state religion.

There is an irony here that the faith's own teaching sharpens. The Vimekhulu holds that teachers are honoured but that no one owns the truth. A tradition which makes Navande the owner of its truth, and Nambi the seat of his authority, sits uneasily with that principle. The reform movements of the last four centuries can be read partly as a return to it. The [[mov:157|Bangekhulu]] of 4560, the [[mov:162|Duthladul]] of 4643 and the [[mov:164|Vamtsikulu]] of 4686 all arose as reforms rather than sects, a kind of dissent that appeals to the teaching over its custodians.

## Conclusion

The historical [[per:20682|Navande]] is a Ninzhe man of Nambi who began teaching at about forty-two and taught for nineteen years in a kingdom that followed another cult. Everything else, his ancestry, his reforms and his journeys, is either borrowed from his namesakes or supplied by the needs of the states that later adopted his faith. Our own state is one of them. That does not make the [[mov:70|Vimekhulu]] less worth studying. It makes its founder a figure we should describe with the restraint his own teaching asked for.
