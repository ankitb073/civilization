To [[per:21443|Yi Shimen]], king of [[pol:927|Toyinmiwa]], guardian of the scales and of the roads, from a dealer in wool and oil of the lower market of [[set:120|Toyinmiwa]], who has paid his dues at the gate for thirty years: greeting, and long life.

I do not write, O king, as one who loves complaint. The priests of [[mov:25|Mie'to'ieshio]] teach us that the wise man withdraws from appetite, and that he who shouts in the market over a few grains of metal has already lost something worth more than the metal. I know this teaching, and I honour it. But the same priests teach that justice in dealings is the floor on which every house stands; and when the floor is cut away, even the wise man falls. It is of the floor that I write.

In the last year, by the order of the treasury, the [[cur:499|O'a]] was struck anew. Where before the coin held four parts and seven tenths of its weight in good metal, it now holds not four. Let the king's own scales be set beside mine: a hundred of the old coins weigh as much as nearly a hundred and nineteen of the new. That is the [[evt:21700|Debasement of the O'a]], and though the heralds did not proclaim it, every money-changer in the city knew it within a month, for the money-changer's thumb is quicker than the herald's tongue.

Consider what has followed, and judge whether I complain without cause.

First, the old coins have gone into the ground. No man who holds a heavy coin will pay it out when a light one will serve; and so the good money hides in jars beneath the floor, and only the light money walks the streets. A coin, O king, is like a citizen: when the bad are honoured, the good withdraw from public life.

Second, prices have climbed to meet the coin. A measure of barley that I bought for less than three O'a when your reign began now costs more than three. The baker raises his loaf, the carter raises his fare, and the labourer who is paid in the same number of coins as before goes home with less bread. The coin has grown lighter; the poor man's belly has grown lighter with it.

Third, and worst for men of my trade, the foreign merchant now weighs every coin we offer. He does not trust our stamp. He asks to be paid in wool, or in oil, or in the old coin, which no one will part with. So trade slows at the river landing, and the dues that the king collects at the gate are fewer, and the treasury, which hoped to gain by the lighter coin, gains less than it hoped.

I am not a fool, O king, and I know why this was done. Since the misfortunes at the [[evt:21576|Battle of Iazhash]] and the [[evt:21577|Battle of Ashmath]], since the [[evt:21597|Treaty of Listlitl]] bound us to send tribute year upon year, and since the [[evt:21630|Battle of Listlitl]] and the second treaty cost us fields and towns besides, the treasury has carried a heavy load. Now the [[evt:21768|Toyinmiwa–Tivinnu War]] eats silver like a fire eats straw, and the news of the [[evt:21848|Battle of Zhaalakhazhash]], which reached the market this month, has made every man close his purse. A king must pay his soldiers. I do not dispute it.

But I say this: a state that pays its soldiers with a lie will soon have to pay them twice. When the [[evt:21611|First Coinage of Toyinmiwa]] was struck, it was struck full, and men said, "Here is a coin a man may trust." That trust was the treasury's true treasure, and it has been spent for a sixth part of the metal.

Therefore I ask, with the other dealers of the lower market who have set their marks below mine: let the next striking of the [[cur:499|O'a]] be made at the old weight, and let it be proclaimed. Let the light coins be taken back at the gate for dues at their face, so that the loss falls on all alike and not on the poor alone. And let the scales of the money-changers be tested by the king's officers, as in the days of your fathers.

Moderation in all things, the priests say. Moderation in the coin also, O king. May the gods keep [[per:21443|Yi Shimen]], and may his scales be true.

Written in the 4th year of Yi Shimen.
