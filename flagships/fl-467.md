*[Tablet I]*

In the 13th year of Ielo, king of [[pol:1072|I'a'ie'i]], may the gods keep him, I set down what I saw in the years of the rising, while my eyes still see and my hand still holds the stylus. Let the young who did not see it read it. Let them know what their freedom cost.

Twice before, in the days of the old men, our people rose for their freedom. Twice before, the yoke came back upon our necks. In my childhood we paid the tribute of [[pol:1039|Sa'se]]: the tenth sheaf, the tenth lamb, and silver counted in the [[cur:586|Titha]] at the gate of the governor. My father paid it, and bowed while he paid it. The collectors of Sa'se rode into the villages of [[reg:13|I'a'ie'i]] on horses, and we walked.

Then [[per:24570|Ielo]] stood up in [[set:21|Nie'khonuesnon]]. He was not yet king; he was a man of the city, with a loud voice and a quiet face. He went up to the high place and poured out wine, and a hawk came down from the hills and sat on the altar and did not fly. The priests of [[mov:25|Mie'to'ieshio]] said: "The gods have their place and their due; this one has chosen his." That was the beginning of the [[evt:24827|Revolt of I'a'ie'i]]. I was young then. I took my father's spear down from the wall. My mother wept. I went.

In the second year of the rising we went down to [[set:110|Onetie]]. Our host was led by [[per:24606|Sho'e Mov]], who knew every hill road between the two cities. Against us came [[per:24607|Ipil Ash]], the general of Sa'se, a hard man in a red cloak. We were many; they were few, not eight hundred. But they fought like men defending their own hearths, which they were not. When the sun was high their line broke. Forty-eight of ours lay dead; I counted my own tent and found one missing. That was the [[evt:24876|Battle of Onithie]], and the first time I saw the backs of the men of Sa'se.

*[Tablet II]*

In the third year Ielo himself led us to the walls of [[set:102|Sa'se]]. Ipil Ash came out a second time to meet us, with six hundred. Before the battle a black ewe dropped twin lambs in our camp, and the priests said it was good. It was good. We broke Ipil Ash at the gate. That was the [[evt:24893|Battle of Sase]]. Then the gate was opened to us, and we went in, and we took back what had been taken from us year upon year: the silver of the governor, the grain of the governor, the horses of the governor. That was the [[evt:24894|First Sack of Sase]]. I took a horse. I rode it home. My father wept to see a child of his on a horse.

In the fifth year we came to Sa'se a third time. Sa'se had found a new general, [[per:24629|Milayiweve]], but it had not found new men; he had four hundred and twenty-seven. Sho'e Mov led us again, and we were more than eleven thousand. It was a hard day all the same. The slingers were on the walls, and the ground was stony, and twenty-eight of ours did not come home. But that was the [[evt:24916|Battle of Sase]] that ended it. We sat down around the city, in the [[evt:24917|Third Siege of Sase]], and at last the elders of Sa'se came out with their heads uncovered.

At the [[evt:24918|Treaty of Sase]] Ielo set his seal. Sa'se let us go free. Sa'se swore ten years of peace. No collector has ridden into our villages since.

So ended the [[evt:24828|Third I'a'ie'i War of Independence]]. Of ours, more than a hundred fell in all. Of theirs, near three times as many; I will not boast of it. But they fought to keep what was not theirs, and we fought to take back what was ours, and the gods weighed it so.

The priests teach justice in dealings and moderation in all things. I have tried to be moderate. I have not been able to be moderate about this.
