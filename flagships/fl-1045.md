title: Ten Rain

You could tell how much water was left by the sound. To'amamshe had known it since she was small enough to be sent down with the little jar. A jar set under the lip of the spring sang while it filled, deep and hollow at first, then higher as the water climbed inside it, until near the neck there was hardly a note at all, only a kind of hurry, and you lifted it away before it spilled. In a wet year it took as long as the short prayer to Etlie, lord of the sky. This summer she had said the prayer through twice and begun it a third time, and the jar was still singing low.

Behind her, in the grey before sunrise, the other women waited with their jars on their hips. Nobody pushed. Above them the terraces went up the hill in their walls of piled stone, and on the terraces the barley stood in stooks no higher than a child's waist, cut early, because there was no sense in waiting for ears that were not going to fill.

"We have a king called Ten Rain," said O'e To'a from the back, "and not one drop."

Somebody hissed at her, as if the king could hear it in [[set:3|Eshue'ethie]].

"What? It's his name. Ma'she To'a. Ten Rain." O'e To'a shifted her jar to the other hip. "My mother was Eight Rain and I'm Eight Rain, and the two of us have had more rain on our heads in one afternoon than this hill has had since the barley went in."

To'amamshe lifted her jar away and stepped aside. The water went on falling onto the flat stone where the jar had stood, a thread no thicker than the yarn on a spindle, and the next woman set her jar under it.

* * *

The hamlet had belonged to [[pol:492|Tomözel]] for nine years. Before that, for six, it had belonged to Yisamashtsi, whose queen still kept her court at Yinishme on the far side of the ridge. On a still evening you could stand by the boundary stone at the top of the goat path and watch the cook-smoke of Yinishme go up behind the shoulder of the hill.

The stone had come the year the war ended. The men of Tomözel's queen brought it up the valley on an ox-cart, stood it in a pit and packed the pit with rubble, and cut signs into its face that nobody in the hamlet could read. That same year every man who meant to keep his fields walked to Ashash to swear to the queen, because a man who did not swear within the year lost them. To'amamshe had heard of one who went to law over it afterwards and asked the court at Ashash for his father's fields back. The answer had gone round every threshing floor in the hills:

"Let him seek his fields in the land of his former lord."

Her husband Inelie'an had gone in good time. He was days on the road each way, and he came home sunburnt and pleased with himself and full of the town: the market, the beer, the scribe who stood on the steps and read the treaty out from a tablet of clay as though the signs were speaking to him. Inelie'an could not read, but he had a good memory for a curse, and he brought one home. For years afterwards, when the children squabbled over whose turn it was at anything, he would lift one finger and boom like the scribe:

"Whoever changes the words of this tablet, may the river refuse him water and the field refuse him grain."

It always made them laugh. He had been dead four winters. To'amamshe had not thought of the curse in all that time, until this summer, when the whole hamlet seemed to remember it at once.

The queen he had sworn to was gone now, and the throne in Eshue'ethie had been taken by a man called Ten Rain. The hills had not noticed much difference. In spring his soldiers had come up the valley road on their way to make war on Le'im, and the hamlet had fed them; before midsummer they came down again the same way, without having fought anybody, and the hamlet fed them again. People said Pibetie had swallowed Le'im first. Le'im was not here. Here was the spring.

* * *

They met on the threshing floor at dusk, where there was very little to thresh.

Yueppaluesh'a spoke first, because he always did. He was a broad, slow man who had sworn at Ashash the same week as Inelie'an, and the two of them had walked home together arguing about the beer.

"They're coming over the ridge at night," he said. "From Yinishme's side. Their stream's gone to mud. Every morning there are more jar-rings in the dust by the spring than we've got hearths."

"Then the spring's ours," someone said.

"The spring is the king's, and so are we. One jar to a hearth, and the king's hearths only." He looked round to be sure they were listening. "It's not as if they don't owe us. Thirty-six talents of silver a year, Yinishme owes the palace, for twenty-four years. And does anybody here believe that girl of theirs has thirty-six talents of anything?"

The girl was their queen, Innish, who had been queen since she was eight years old and was eighteen now. The hamlet had seen her once, a small stiff figure in a cart on the Yinishme road, before the stone.

"There was a line about that. I heard it read." Yueppaluesh'a lifted one finger, the way Inelie'an used to, and gave it in the scribe's voice:

"If he withholds it, he has broken the oath."

He let it sit. "And you all know what comes after."

Nobody needed telling. Half the floor said it for him under their breath, the way you say the end of a prayer you have said too many times: the river, the field.

To'amamshe heard herself speak before she had decided to.

"The curse is for whoever changes the words."

They turned to look at her. In the dusk she could not see their faces, only the pale of their eyes.

"That was what Inelie'an always said. Whoever changes the words of the tablet. Not whoever is poor." She found she was angry, and it surprised her. "And it isn't only their river that's refusing, is it? Go and look at your own barley, Yueppaluesh'a. Did you change the words?"

"Nobody's saying who changed what."

"The priests of [[mov:25|Mie'tse'ieshio]] come up here every new moon to tell us the gods want justice in dealings and moderation in all things. One jar to a hearth, that's moderation. I'll carry one jar and be glad of it. But a stone isn't justice. A stone is a stone."

"A stone is the king's," Yueppaluesh'a said, not unkindly. "And so is the spring."

They decided it his way, as she had known they would. One jar to a hearth, and the men would take turns sitting up at the spring. Yueppaluesh'a would take the first night himself.

Walking home, O'e To'a caught her arm. "A salt-man came through from the lowlands this morning," she said, low. "He says in Iith they're already eating the seed barley." She was quiet a moment. "Your girl's over there, isn't she. Over the stone."

"She married over there the summer before it came," To'amamshe said. "It was only a ridge, then."

* * *

Yi Sim'a came over the ridge a little after moonset, with two jars slung from a pole across her shoulders and the boy, who was four summers old, walking at her side holding a fold of her skirt.

To'amamshe had not lit the lamp. She had been sitting in the doorway in the dark, and she was not surprised.

"Their stream's a ditch of cracked mud," Yi Sim'a said, before she had even set down the pole. "In Yinishme they queue at the wells from midnight. He's gone with the queen's levy, against Yennga, and the boy cries for water in his sleep." She held out her fist and opened it. In the starlight there was a twist of cloth, and in it the small grey weight of silver. "It's weighed by the antli. I know it's lighter than your [[cur:253|Iebi]] over here. It's still silver."

"Put it away."

"Mother—"

"I said put it away. Are you a buyer now? Am I a seller?" She stood up and took one end of the pole. "They've set a man at the spring tonight. We'll go round by the lower terraces and come at it from below, and if he says anything, you let me answer him."

They went down through the terraces in the dark, through the stubble, the boy between them stumbling and silent. The night was warm and smelled of dust and sheep. There was no sound of water anywhere: no frogs, no trickle in the channel that fed the lower terraces, nothing but the dry rasp of their feet. Then, near the rock, the faint high thread of it, so faint she was not sure for a moment that she had not imagined it.

There were people at the spring.

She stopped, and Yi Sim'a stopped behind her. In the dim she made out the broad shape of Yueppaluesh'a sitting on the rock above the spring with his knees drawn up, and below him a woman crouched with a jar under the thread. A second woman stood behind her, waiting, a jar on her hip. Neither of them was from the hamlet. To'amamshe knew every jar in the hamlet by its shape.

Yueppaluesh'a turned his head. For a long moment nobody spoke. The jar went on singing its low note.

"Who's that?" he said.

To'amamshe drew breath to answer him.

"I can't see," he said, before she could. "It's too dark. I can't tell who anybody is." He looked away again, down at the crouched woman, and after a moment To'amamshe understood that it was his wife's sister, who had married over the ridge the same year as Yi Sim'a. Half the hamlet had kin on the other side. That was what the ridge had been for, before it was a border: walking over to marry.

They waited their turn. It was a long wait. The thread fell, and the jars sang low, and nobody said anything at all, and once the boy fell asleep standing up against his mother's legs and had to be caught. When it was their turn To'amamshe crouched and held the jar under the water herself, and said the prayer to Etlie three times through, and a fourth.

* * *

At the top of the goat path the stone was the first thing to come out of the dark, grey on grey.

To'amamshe had carried one of the jars up the hill for her daughter, and now she set it down in the dust beside the stone and straightened her back. Over the ridge the cook-smoke of Yinishme was beginning to rise into a sky that was already, at this hour, too clear.

The boy put his palm flat on the stone and looked up at her.

"Is it true your king is called Ten Rain?"

"It's true."

"I'm Four Rain," he said. Yawni To'a was his name; his father had chosen it. "So that's fourteen."

"It doesn't work like that," said his mother.

He thought about this, and then he looked at the foot of the stone, where the dust lay deep and fine as ground meal. "It's thirsty," he said.

"It's a stone."

"Everything's thirsty."

To'amamshe should have said no. A cupped palmful was a palmful less for the boy himself by evening. But she tipped the jar, and he caught what came in his two hands, and carried it the length of his arm to the stone without spilling more than half, and let it go. The dust drank it with a small soft sound, and a dark patch the size of his two hands spread at the stone's foot.

"One," he said, as if he were starting to count something.

Yi Sim'a lifted the pole onto her shoulders. "Next year the truce is finished," she said. "That's what they're saying in Yinishme."

"Next year it will rain," To'amamshe said, and they both nodded, as though they had said the same thing.

It did not rain properly the next year, or the year after. The kingdom on the far side of the stone lasted three more summers. In the seventh, [[pol:492|Tomözel]] reached over the ridge for Yinishme's fields, and after that the stone stood in the middle of one king's country with the same dust on both sides of it. The famine the salt-man had talked about ran on in [[evt:11773|Iith]] until one person in seven there was dead. The next year another began in [[evt:11774|Yimla]], and Ya'cuam families from there came walking into these same dry hills with their old people on donkeys, as if there were nowhere wetter left to go. The boy would be a man of twenty-six, in the fourteenth year of the king's son, the second [[per:12102|Ma'she To'a]], the year of the [[evt:12153|Treaty of Tliyiya]], when the record closes [[evt:11772|the Great Drought in Yizamashti]]: twenty-two years, across four hundred and seventeen districts.

But that morning she only watched them go over the ridge, the woman and the boy and the pole with its two jars swinging, until the path took them. Then she went back down the hill, took her own jar from beside the door, and went to wait her turn at the spring. Behind her, at the foot of the stone, the dark patch the size of two small hands had already dried to the colour of the ground on either side.

**Author's note.** Inelie'an's curse closes the Treaty of Ashash (PAL·TR·00775), which gave these hills to Tomözel in AY 1599, with thirty-six talents of yearly tribute and a ten-year truce. The man told to seek his fields in the land of his former lord sued at Ashash in 1603 (PAL·LS·00203). The record puts six of the drought's districts in Tomözel and no famine there. It does not say whether Yinishme paid its silver, or why the king's war on Le'im that year ended without a battle. The spring, and everyone at it, is mine.
