*From "Recollections of an Old Citizen", printed at Nambi, 2 Vandi 2328 of the Vimikhulu. Chapter the Fourth.*

**IV. THE NIGHT THE LAMPS WERE LIT**

The young people who come to my door ask me what it was like. They expect me to say it was glorious. It was cold, and it smelled of coal smoke and wet wool, and I was frightened for most of it. Let me begin, as all of us who lived it must begin, with Nzhivi.

I was a printer's boy in the year 2294 when the wire brought the news of [[evt:45934|Nzhivi]]. I remember the foreman reading it aloud in the composing room, in the flat voice men use for figures they cannot believe. Three hundred and eighty thousand of our men, out of an army of [[pol:1638|Khishkhih]] that outnumbered the men of Ane. Outnumbered them, and lost. We set the casualty lists in the smallest type in the shop, six columns to the page, and they ran for eleven days. My uncle was in the fourth day's list. My mother kept that sheet folded in her prayer-book until she died.

Then came [[evt:45949|the rising in Khumbivi]], and the Crown sent soldiers it did not have to put down a province it could not hold. Then the price of bread. Then, five years after the first, the wire spoke again: [[evt:45980|Nzhivi]] a second time, and nearly a hundred and fifty thousand more. That was when the people of [[set:85|Nambi]] stopped believing. Not in the One, you understand; the preachers of [[mov:70|Vimikhulu]] never lost their congregations. But in the palace.

You will read in the schoolbooks that the Empress [[per:45163|Navaavun]] was a tyrant. I did not think so then and I do not think so now. She was a woman who had been taught that duty and order were the same thing, and who could not see that the order had already broken. She sent out proclamations; we printed them; nobody read them. She raised the tax on bread, so many [[cur:901|shikh]] more on every measure; we printed that too, and that was read.

I first heard [[per:45324|Najimi]] speak in the yard of the tram depot, in the autumn of 2305, from the top of a coal wagon. He was not a young man even then. He did not shout. He said that the wise withdraw from wealth and appetite, and that the palace had withdrawn from neither; that a nation which sends three hundred thousand sons to die in a bad war owes the living a voice; that teachers are honoured but no one owns the truth, not the priest, and not the crown. He said: *we do not need to hate her; we need only to stop obeying her.* I went home and could not sleep.

The night itself, in the year 2306, I remember in pieces. The order came by runner, not by wire, because the wire belonged to the Crown. Every house that was with us was to light a lamp in its front window at the tenth hour. I lit ours with my own hands. When I went out into the street I saw them going on, window after window, street after street, down the hill to the river, until the whole of Nambi was lit as for a feast. The guards at the palace looked out and saw it, and understood that they were alone.

There was very little shooting. The young never believe that. The Empress was not harmed; she was conducted from the palace at dawn by a deputation of the city wards, and she lived out her life in quiet. By noon the [[evt:46037|Revolution]] had a name and Najimi had an office, and by evening we had printed the first decree of the Republic, with the ink still wet when the carriers took it.

He did not live long to enjoy it. Two years, and he was gone, worn out. But the Republic outlived him, as he said it must. Those who doubted it should remember [[evt:46171|Kamvamvil]] and [[evt:46179|Mbilu]], where the armies of the Republic did what the armies of the Crown never could, and [[evt:46181|brought Mbilu under our flag]].

Today Citizen [[per:45530|Nazhigi]] sits where the Empress sat, by the votes of the people. I was in the hall when he was chosen. I lit a lamp that night too, out of habit.
