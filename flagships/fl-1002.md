title: A Name That Means Honey
---
The gates went at first light, and by the time the sun stood a hand above the plain the smoke had found its way into the house of [[dei:104|Shilimu]] and lay along the roof beams like a second ceiling.

Nashili swept. There was nothing else to do with her hands. She swept the ash that drifted in at the door and the white dust that the shouting in the streets seemed to shake from the walls, and she swept around the dark patch on the east wall without looking at it, the way a woman steps around a sleeping dog.

Behind the oil jars in the storeroom her grandson lay on a reed mat with his mouth open. Nabayu had come back from the hill country four nights ago with his lips split like the skin of a windfall fruit and a cut along his forearm that he would not let her wash. Two hundred and thirty-four men of [[set:51|Luyulule]] had gone out with Nakhushumbu to meet more than nine thousand of [[pol:18|Kadhazhile]]. Seventy-one had not come back. Her grandson had come back, and she had not yet decided whether to thank the goddess for it, because now the army of Kadhazhile was in the streets, and young men who had carried spears were what it was looking for.

* * *

She had been seven the first time.

Her mother had pushed her into this same storeroom, behind these same jars, or their grandmothers, and gone out to the court and not come back until the next night. Through the gap between two jars Nashili had watched a soldier come in alone. He was not big. He held a bronze knife in one hand and a cracked honey pot in the other, pressed to his chest the way a child carries a puppy, and he sat down against the east wall and ate the honey with his fingers and wept while he ate.

She sneezed. The dust of the jars got into her nose and she could not stop it. He looked up, and for a long moment they looked at each other, the soldier with honey on his chin and the girl with her knees in the spilled grain. Then he held the pot out to her.

She came. What else does a child do? They sat against the wall and ate, and he told her his name was Nambave, and laughed when he said it, because the name means honey and he had honey all down his front. Then he stood up and set the point of his knife to the plaster and cut signs into it, slowly, with his tongue between his teeth, like a man who has learned his signs late and is afraid of getting one wrong. When he had finished he looked at what he had made, nodded to her as if she were a grown woman, and went out into the noise. She never saw him again.

It was the old scribe of the temple, a man with clay under his nails, who read it to her when the soldiers had gone home. He put his finger under each sign in turn.

"I, Nambave, of the army of Kadhazhile, wish I were home."

Her mother wanted it plastered over. Every few years the house of Shilimu was freshened with new white plaster, and every time Nashili stood beside the plasterers, a girl and then a wife and then a widow, and made them carry their trowels around it. The patch had gone dark with sixty years of lamp smoke, a square of old wall the size of two hands in the clean white. She could not have said why she kept it. At first, she thought, it was so that she would remember to hate him. Later it was for other reasons, and later still she stopped asking.

She had seen kings come and go from the palace on the mound. The old king Nanzhizhim. His daughter the queen, who built the new wall and set a bronze plaque in it that said "May Shilimu bless the city," and who died in battle twenty years ago. The boy king after her, who reigned less than a year before they put him to death. Then the strangers who ruled the city from somewhere else for fourteen years, and then Nadazum, five years king, who was this morning somewhere on the mound with his hands empty. The priests of the [[mov:16|Ninzhe]] said that all things turn on a great wheel, and that what ends will begin again. She had heard it all her life as a comfort.

* * *

The soldier came a little after midday.

He stood in the doorway with the light behind him, a spear with a bronze head taller than himself, a leather cap, and a neck burnt raw by the sun of the hill country. He could not have been more than seventeen. An officer had posted him there, he said, speaking past her into the cool of the room, so that no one should carry anything out of the goddess's house. Shilimu had given them the city. It would be bad luck to rob her.

— Then you'll want to sit, Nashili said. It's a long afternoon, and she's your goddess too.

— I know whose house it is.

But after a while he sat, with his back to the doorpost and the spear across his knees, and drank from a fat waterskin, and wiped his mouth, and drank again. Nashili watched the water go into him and thought about the split lips behind the jars.

He was from [[set:65|Kadhazhile]] itself, from the city on the sea. He told her this without being asked, the way the young tell old women things. He had never been so far from the sea in his life. In the hill country there had been no smell of home at all, he said, not salt, not fish, not weed, nothing; the air was like an empty jar. His mother made offerings to [[dei:108|Bumbe]] every new moon so the goddess of the sea would send him back. He said this to the floor.

— What's that? he said then, and pointed with his chin at the dark patch on the wall. Someone's spoiled your plaster.

— Somebody from your city.

He got up and looked at it closely, the way people look at signs they cannot read, as if the meaning might be in the cut marks themselves.

— You read?

— No. I was told it. I've been told it so long I could cut it myself, if I knew how. And she said it for him, the way the old scribe had said it to her, word by word. "I, Nambave, of the army of Kadhazhile, wish I were home."

The boy said nothing for a moment.

— One of ours? Here? When?

— Sixty years ago. The first time you came in. I was seven.

He turned and stared at her.

— There was a first time?

Nashili laughed before she could stop herself, a dry sound that hurt her chest. Of course there was. In Kadhazhile they would tell it as though the wheel had only just been made. Nobody in the conquering city ever remembered the last time. It was only the conquered who kept count.

— There was a first time, she said. There's always a first time. Sit down, you'll have the officer on you.

* * *

In the long heat of the afternoon, when the noise in the streets had settled into the steady grinding of carts, he talked about the hill country. The general Navande had not wanted a fight at all, he said. He had put the army between them and the water and waited. At night you could hear the men of Luyulule out in the dark arguing about who should drink, and in the end they had come out because there was nothing else to do, and their tongues were so swollen they could not even shout.

— I didn't kill anyone, the boy said. I don't think so. It was very fast and I don't think so.

Behind the curtain of the storeroom a jar grated on the floor.

The boy's head came round. Nashili kept her broom moving.

— Rats, she said. They go for the grain.

He looked at the curtain for a long time. She could see him weigh it, the way she had once seen a man with honey on his chin weigh a girl among the jars. The smoke hung in the roof beams. Somewhere outside a woman was calling a name over and over, and nobody answered her.

— Big rats, the boy said at last, and settled his back against the doorpost again.

Toward evening horns went on the mound, and a runner went past the door shouting for the soldiers of his company. The boy listened and stood. The great king had come up himself, he told her; Nangashu, in the seventh year of his reign, had come to take the oath of Luyulule with his own hand. The scribes were already wetting clay at the gate. After the oath, they said, the city would be the king's, and taking from it would be taking from him, and there would be no more of that. The king would put up a stone to [[dei:105|Khudhaze]], he said, with how many he had killed written on it.

— How many did he kill?

— As many as the stone says, the boy said, and for a moment he looked much older.

At the door he stopped.

— I can't cut signs, he said. Nobody in my house can.

— No.

— Then you say it. The way you said his. Nakhizhi.

— Lion, she said. It's a big name.

— It was my mother's idea.

He unslung the waterskin, still more than half full, and set it on the floor beside the storeroom curtain, carefully, without looking at the curtain at all.

— For the rats, he said, and went out with his spear into the red light.

* * *

When it was dark she drew back the curtain and put the waterskin into her grandson's hands, and he drank with his whole body, choking, not stopping, the way she thought the men in the hills must have wanted to drink at the end. She took it from him before he was sick and gave it back a little at a time. Then he slept.

She lit the lamp under the dark patch and sat against the east wall with her knees drawn up, as a girl once sat there with honey on her fingers, and said the names over to the plaster the way a woman counts measures of grain into a jar, so as not to lose one. Nambave. Nakhizhi. Nabayu. The wheel turned, the priests said, and what ended would begin again. She had always understood that to mean the soldiers. She understood now that it meant the honey too, and the water, and the jar that scrapes in the dark, and the boy who chooses not to hear it.

Sixty-seven years she had lived in Luyulule and never once wished she were home, because she had never been anywhere else. Tonight the city belonged to the great king, and she sat in it, in the same house, against the same wall. She had not moved a step. She wished it anyway, with her whole body, the way Nambave had.

**Author's note.** Nambave's line is real: cut into temple plaster during the First Sack of AY 567 (PAL·GR·00012), and found by Nanjil in AY 4885 with the queen's plaque (PAL·IN·03458) and the great king's stele, which claims 200 dead at the ford where the record counts 39 (PAL·IN·00161; PAL·EX·00083). The water at the [[evt:2655|Battle of Yilish]] and the fallen gates come from the court's account of the [[evt:2643|Holy War of Kadhdazhile against Luyulule]] (PAL·CH·00375). After the [[evt:2656|Second Sack of Luyululi]] Nadazum swore the oath; he died in AY 631. The record does not say whether Nambave got home.
