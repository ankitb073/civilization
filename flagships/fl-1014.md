title: The Fifth Saying
---
A weigher's hands are her whole trade, and Nazhige put both of hers into the fire.

The cedar box had stood on the shelf above the cistern since before she was born. When the roof beam came down across the courtyard it took the shelf with it, and the box split on the paving, and by the time she reached it the roll inside had begun to curl at one end, slowly, the way a vine leaf curls on a brazier. There was water a step away. She did not think of it. She took the roll in both hands, held the burning edge against her own lap, and pressed until it stopped.

Then she sat on the stones with it in her skirt and listened to the lower town.

She had lived fifty-five years in [[set:84|Keezhe]] and never heard it make this sound. Roofs went in with a long sigh, one after another, down the hill. A mule was screaming somewhere and would not stop. Men were calling to each other in her own tongue, the one the palace spoke and the coins were lettered in, so that when they first came up the street she had taken them for the queen's own soldiers, home from the battle under the walls. Then she had seen what they were carrying, and how they carried it.

Above the lower town the palace quarter stood dark on its height. Down here every street she knew was lit.

Her mother had named her for rain. She thought of that, sitting there while her hands began to tell her what she had done to them, and it seemed to her the first joke she had ever properly understood.

She did not unroll the parchment. She knew what was on it: seven sayings of the Teacher, set down after his death by a priest, in the old letters, in a hand so even it might have been weighed. For thirty years she had read them aloud in this courtyard on reading nights, to the dyers and porters and widows of the street, because she was the one among them who could read the old letters; she read them all day long, around the rims of coins. The first saying was the one people liked best. "What rises will fall and what falls will rise; do not grieve at the turning of the wheel."

She tried to say it now, under her breath, and could not get past the second word.

* * *

On the third day the soldiers of [[pol:1290|Diegine]] began bringing her silver.

Her stall was gone and the awning with it, but bronze does not burn. She had dug the scale out of the ash of her front room, the beam and both pans and the leather bag of weights, and hung it from a nail in the one doorpost still standing. She sat beside it on an upturned basket with her hands wrapped in strips of her skirt and greased with the last of the oil. She could not hold the beam. She could still read it.

Someone had told them there was an honest weigher in the street of the dyers. It was the kind of thing a street says about you when there is nothing else left to say.

The first of them was very young. He had a crossbow slung across his back and a beard that had not yet made up its mind, and he tipped onto her cloth a dozen of the queen's coins, a cup, and a pair of ear-rings Nazhige knew, because she had weighed them for the woman who owned them two summers ago, when the woman's daughter married.

'The men say your queen puts copper in her silver,' the boy said. 'I don't want to carry copper home.'

She looked at the ear-rings for a while. Then she showed him with her chin where to put the coins.

There is a trick every weigher's daughter learns before she learns her letters: the thumb laid light on the beam, on the far side, where the buyer cannot see. Her mother had taught it to her so that she would know it when others did it. In forty years at the scale Nazhige had never once used it. She had been proud of that, in a way the Teacher would not have liked, and she had known he would not like it, and had gone on being proud.

She could cheat him now without a thumb. She had only to say that the queen's coin was bad and the cup was tin under a skin, and he would leave them with her rather than carry rubbish home, and the ear-rings would go back to the woman who owned them, if the woman was alive.

"Test it as you test silver." That was the third saying. It did not say: unless the man holding it has burned your street.

She had him set the coins in the pan himself, and she watched the beam. The queen's coins came down level against her bronze, as they always had. She had him draw one across the black stone and turn the streak to the light, and it was the colour it always was. She had him weigh the cup. Silver. The ear-rings: silver.

'Your queen's coin is honest,' the boy said, surprised.

'Our queen's,' said Nazhige.

He turned one over in his fingers. On its face was the queen's head, veiled, looking right; on the back a bull walking, and the mark of the city that struck it. Every child in the lower town could draw that bull in the dust. Before the war a [[cur:727|Nidha]] had bought about a measure of grain. She did not know what it bought this morning. She did not think anyone did.

'They say she led the army herself at Anjith,' the boy said.

'She did.'

'My father was at Anjith.' He seemed to want to say more and not to know what it was. In the end he took a coin of his own from his belt, a Diegine coin, and laid it in the pan against one of hers, the way children do, to see. The Diegine coin went down. It was heavier. It had always been heavier; she could have told him that without the scale.

He paid her for her trouble with one of the queen's coins, which was the custom, and which meant, she supposed, that the city had paid her for telling the truth about it. At the corner he looked back. Diegine kept the cult of [[mov:38|Zhibinjizu]], as the palace on the hill did; the men who had come through the gate had prayed before the battle to the same gods as the men they killed under the walls.

'It turns,' he said, the way everyone says it, soldiers and priests and women at the well. 'It'll turn for you.'

When he had gone she sat with the coin in the burnt fold of her skirt, where the roll had lain.

* * *

The peace was sworn in the palace quarter, and the lower town climbed the hill to watch, because there was nothing in the lower town to stay for.

The record-room had not burned. People said the clerks had stood on its roof all night with wet hides, so that whatever the kingdom owed would at least be written down correctly.

Nazhige stood at the back, where the street narrows. She had held [[per:30640|Nangazhu]]'s face ten thousand times. She had held it so often that she had stopped seeing it, as you stop seeing your own hand: the veil drawn forward, the face turned to the right. Now the queen came out and stood with her face turned to the right, toward the queen of Diegine, and she was older than the coin. Coins do not grow older. Queens do; and this one had led her army at the [[evt:31253|Battle of Anjith]] and come home beaten, and stood now in her own city to set her seal to what it would cost.

Beside her stood Navande, queen of Diegine, a woman in her forties, who had beaten them at the [[evt:31260|Battle of Agni]] with her own army the year before. When the crier called her name, an old man next to Nazhige said under his breath that it had been the name of our queen's father, who was king for a single year, as though that might count for something.

The crier read the terms. [[pol:1321|Keezhe]] gave to Diegine seven districts. Keezhe would pay silver to Diegine for the costs of the war. There would be a truce of fifteen years. He read out the weight of the silver twice, because the first time the crowd made a sound as if it had been struck, and the second time it made no sound at all. Nazhige, who had turned weights into coins in her head every day of her working life, turned this one, and found she could not stop: so many coins to the pan, so many pans to the day, so many days. They will need weighers, she thought. She would be seventy when the truce ran out.

Then the two queens swore before the gods, the same gods, there being no others to swear by. Nazhige thought of the second saying, "There is one above all the gods, and the gods are its servants," and wondered whether the servants had been told.

* * *

That evening was a reading night, and since no one could think of a reason it should not be, they came.

They sat along the fallen beam as if it had been put there for the purpose: old Nambave, who had carried loads up the hill for forty years and now had nothing to carry; Nashugu, whose ear-rings were somewhere on the road to Diegine; two children nobody had yet come to claim; and the young woman from the upper town.

She had begun coming down to the readings that year. She was twenty-seven; she had said so the first night, as though it were a qualification. She had learned the old letters at home and did not need anyone to read them to her, and came anyway, and asked the questions nobody else asked, and Nazhige had not yet decided whether she liked her.

It was the young woman who held the roll, since Nazhige could not, and unwound it across her knees by the lamp. The fire had taken the right-hand edge of the column in a brown scalloped lace, fine as a moth's wing. The first saying ended a finger's width from the burn. The second kept its last word by a hair. The third and fourth were whole, and the young woman read them out as they were written, and Nazhige said them again after her in plainer words, as she always had, so the porters would understand: "Test it as you test silver." "Whoever feeds the hungry stranger has fed the god."

Then the fifth.

"Wealth is a" and then the brown edge, and nothing, the end of the line gone up over the lower town three nights before with everything else. Below it, at the start of the next line, as though nothing had happened: "the wise go without."

'Burden,' said Nambave at once. 'Wealth is a burden the wise go without.'

'It was never burden,' said Nashugu. 'It was snare.'

Someone said fever. One of the children said dog, and for a moment they all laughed, there on the beam in the ruin, and it was terrible, and it was good.

The young woman did not guess. She looked from the burnt edge to the street beyond the doorpost, where nothing was left standing to the height of a man.

'Look at us,' she said. 'If going without made people wise, this would be the wisest street in the world.' And then, more quietly: 'He tells us to feed the hungry stranger. With what?'

Nazhige held out her bandaged hand with the coin lying in it. The young woman took it and turned it over, the veiled head, the walking bull, and then, half in play and half in something else, laid it on the parchment in the gap the fire had made, where it fitted almost exactly. Wealth is a, and then the queen, looking right. Nobody laughed at that.

'You've read it to us every reading night for thirty years,' the young woman said. 'You know the word.'

Nazhige knew the word. She had known it since she was a girl, the way she knew the weight of a coin in her palm before the scale told her. It was short and plain, and it would have filled the gap exactly. She looked at the people on the beam with their burnt sleeves and their empty hands, and at the young woman's face, which was not asking to be comforted. It was asking what the boy had asked. Is it good. Is it honest. Can I carry it home.

'I don't remember,' she said.

It was the first time in forty years that she had laid her thumb on the scale. She felt it go down, lightly, on the far side, where no one could see; and what she could never afterwards tell was which way she had made it fall.
