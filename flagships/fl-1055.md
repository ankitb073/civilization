title: A Palm's Breadth for the Growing
---
Sinew has to be chewed. There is no other way to soften it, and so for the whole of that dry season the bowyer's house in [[set:10|Navnge]] went about with its mouth full and said very little.

The tendons came from the backs of oxen, two long grey straps from either side of the spine, and they had hung in the rafters since the rains until they were as hard as horn and rang when Lubun knocked them on the bench. He beat them with a wooden mallet until they gave up and came apart into cords, and split the cords with his thumbnail into threads, and the threads went into mouths. His daughter Byugeth chewed. The potter's boy from across the lane chewed, for a bowl of whatever was in the pot at noon. Lubun chewed too, though his back teeth had been bad for years, because nobody else could tell by the taste when a thread was ready. First the salt went, then the animal. When it tasted of nothing at all you took it out of your mouth and drew it through the glue and laid it along the back of the bow, and pressed it flat with the bone, and reached for the next.

"My jaw is going to fall off," Byugeth said, around a mouthful.

"Then you'll be quiet," said her father, around his.

The glue was boiled from the bladders of river fish and smelled of them, and the smell had got into the walls and the bedding and the girl's hair. The bow lay on its form on the bench under the window, bound down with cord in seven places, bent backwards into a shape that made people who knew nothing about bows think it had been broken: a curve like a new moon lying on its back. Its belly was horn, two strips from the same grey ox, boiled soft and fitted and glued in the last dry season. Its core was wood from the hills. Its back, this dry season, was sinew, layer on layer, each left to dry for half a month before the next went on.

It was the king's bow. The king was eleven years old.

* * *

He had come up the bowyers' lane the year before on a dun pony, with two spearmen walking behind and his mother's steward hurrying after in the dust. He had gone along the racks with his hands clasped behind his back like an old man at a horse fair, and then he had stopped at Lubun's door, and taken his hands out from behind his back, and touched everything.

"Not a boy's bow," he said. "I have a boy's bow."

"What's wrong with it?"

"It's a boy's bow."

Lubun asked him what he wanted a man's bow for, which was not a thing you asked a king. But this one had been king of [[pol:866|Yide]] since he was five, and that year the heralds had begun calling his kingdom an empire, and neither seemed to have made him any better at it.

"There's a tablet at Khungumnu," the boy said, "that says the war was mine. That I stirred it up." He said it the way children repeat what they have been told about themselves, like a nickname they have not yet decided whether to mind. "I was five. They sat me on a stool and a man read it out, and it went on so long I fell asleep, and when I woke up it was still mine."

Lubun knew the war. Everyone in Navnge knew it. It had begun three years before this boy was born. His grandmother the old queen had begun it, and died at the [[evt:20125|Battle of Binge]] in the year of his birth; his father had gone on with it and died at the [[evt:20181|Battle of Binge]] when the boy was four. After that it had been his: the dead, the shame, the silver, seven hundred and seventy-six talents of it carted over the border in the month of the harvest. A treaty needs a king's name on it, and his was the only one left.

"The [[evt:20190|Treaty of Khungumnu]] has ten years of truce in it," the boy said. "Five are nearly gone. When it ends I'll be fifteen. I want my bow by then."

"A good bow takes the time it takes."

"How much time?"

Lubun told him. Horn this dry season, sinew the next. Then the bow would hang in the smoke under the roof for two rains before he would trust a string on it. The boy counted on his fingers, lost his place, and began again.

"That's before fifteen."

"Just."

"Then do it."

Lubun stood him in the doorway, in the light, and had him hold out his left arm and close the fist, and ran a cord from the corner of his mouth to his knuckles. Then he laid his own hand flat against the end of the cord, so that it reached a palm's breadth past the boy's fist, and pinched the cord there.

"What's that for?"

"For the growing."

The boy looked at the bowyer's hand, which was wider than his own was long, and then at his own hand.

"Is that how much I'll grow?"

"About that."

"My wife says I won't grow at all." He thought about this. "She's eight. She doesn't know anything."

"If you grow past it," Lubun said, "come back, and I'll make you another."

"You'll be too old."

"I'm too old now."

"Yes," said the king of Yide, with great satisfaction. The spearmen looked at the sky. The steward weighed out half the price in silver by the [[cur:406|Ngevu]], and said the rest would come when the bow was strung.

* * *

The news that the king was sick came down from the palace the way all news did, through the kitchens. A fever; the heat; nothing yet. Then: the healers were sleeping in his room. Then: smoke going up over the palace roofs from morning to dark, where the priests were burning offerings to [[dei:16|Dumamnul]] of the sky, and the priests did not burn offerings like that for nothing.

The women of the lane talked about his mother. She had borne four children. A daughter, long ago, who had not lived to four. Two small sons who had not lived to six. Now this one. Nobody said aloud what the lane was thinking, which was that the priests of [[mov:2|Tsinru]] taught that the gods showed their favour in good fortune, and that by that reckoning the old house had mislaid it somewhere.

Byugeth stopped chewing. "Should we stop?"

"Stop what?"

"It's bad luck. Making a bow for a sick king."

They were halfway along a layer. Under Lubun's hands the glue was cooling; in a little while it would skin over, and anything laid on it afterwards would never truly join, and one day, at full draw, in somebody's hands, the back would lift away like bark from a dead tree.

"It's worse luck making half a bow," he said, and held out the next thread. After a moment she took it.

On the fourth morning the wailing began behind the palace wall, high and thin, and was taken up street by street until it reached the bowyers' lane. The potter's boy took the thread out of his mouth and ran to see. Lubun went to the bench and lifted the bow on its form, still bound in its seven places, and looked at it for a while, and put it down again. Then he laid a cloth over it, the way you would over a face.

* * *

That night there were horses in the lanes, a great many of them, and torches, and the long groan of the gates being shut. By the next noon everyone in Navnge knew the name [[per:20124|Bethukathrin]] and almost nothing else. Nobody could say who his father had been. He was thirty-two. He had a wife, and three small daughters, and a son not two months old, and men with spears, and the palace. That was the whole of what the lane could find out about him, and the lane was good at finding things out.

"A son," said the potter, who had come over to stand in Lubun's doorway and look at nothing. "The old house has nobody left, and this one's got a son in the cradle. You don't need a priest to read that."

"No," said Lubun.

"Well, then."

"You don't need a priest," Lubun agreed, and went back inside.

On the third day after the king died a cart came up the bowyers' lane with soldiers walking beside it, and an officer went from door to door. They were taking bows for the new king's men. They were not rude about it. The officer said the new king would pay for everything, which is a thing every new king says, and some of them do.

They emptied Lubun's racks. Then one of the soldiers, a boy not much older than Byugeth, lifted the cloth from the bench.

"That one isn't a bow yet," Lubun said.

"What is it, then?"

"Glue," said Lubun. "And a promise."

The soldier looked at his officer. The officer looked at the strange backward curve bound down on its form, plainly did not know what to make of it, and might have taken it anyway, to be on the safe side, if a horseman had not stopped in the lane outside, and the officer had not straightened as though someone had pulled a cord in his back.

Bethukathrin did not get down. He had dust in his beard and a cloak that looked as if it had been somebody else's until very recently. He had been riding through the city since first light, lane by lane, so that people could see there was a king and what he looked like, and the bowyers' lane was one more lane. He looked at the cart, and at the bare racks, and through the door at the thing on the bench, and said the name of [[dei:14|Vvumi]] under his breath, the way horsemen do.

"Is that broken?"

"It's made backwards," Lubun said. "When it's finished you string it against its own bend. That's where the strength comes from."

"Whose is it?"

The officer began to answer. Lubun answered first. "The king's."

There was a short silence in the lane. The new king of Yide considered the bowyer from the height of his horse.

"Which one?"

"The one who's dead."

Bethukathrin looked at the bow again. "When will it be done?"

"Two rains before it will take a string."

"It's small."

"It's his arm at fifteen. When the truce runs out."

"And the price?"

"Half paid."

"Who owes the rest?"

Lubun opened his mouth, found that the true answer was a dead child, and closed it again.

"I do, then," said Bethukathrin. He said it flatly, the way a man names the price of a horse, and Lubun understood that it was not kindness, or not only kindness. A man who has taken a house has taken its debts, or people begin to wonder what else he means not to pay. "Leave it," he told the officer. "Take the rest." And to Lubun: "Finish it the way you told him."

"It won't fit you."

"I'm not asking it to," said the king, and gathered up his reins, and rode on down the lane to show himself to the potters.

* * *

When the half month was up they laid the last layer.

The city had settled into its new name the way a house settles after a death, with the furniture in the same places and everything different. Heralds had cried the king's name at the gates. The smoke over the palace roofs was for him now. In the lane the talk had moved on to the price of grain, which had been too high for years and showed no sign of having noticed the new king.

Lubun took the cloth off the bow and set the glue pot on the coals. The measuring cord hung on its peg by the door, where it had hung for a year, with a smear of red ochre at the place where his palm had ended. He took it down and laid it along the bench beside the bow, and it was the right length, as it had been the right length every time he had looked at it.

Byugeth pulled a thread from the bundle and looked at it without enthusiasm.

"Who's it for now?"

Lubun ran his thumb down the cord to the ochre. The boy's arm, and a palm's breadth for the growing.

"A boy of fifteen."

"Which boy?"

He thought of the king asleep on his stool at Khungumnu while the war was handed to him. He thought of the new king's son in his cradle behind the palace wall, who was not three months old and would be fifteen soon enough. He thought of all the boys of fifteen there had been in Yide, and would be, for as long as there were truces and the truces ran out.

"There's always one," he said, and put the next thread in his mouth, and waited for the salt to go.

**Author's note.** The tablet the boy complains of survives. The Treaty of Khungumnu (PAL·TR·01364, AY 2401) names [[per:20061|Myithdiyin]], in the enemy's spelling, as the one who "stirred up" a war that, by the record, began in AY 2393, three years before his birth; the threatening letter to his grandmother (PAL·LT·01193, AY 2393) shows the quarrel already sharp. The record calls his death natural and says no more. It names no bowyer and no bow. It does record that [[per:20124|Bethukathrin]]'s son was born in the year of the [[evt:20244|usurpation]], and reigned after his father for forty-three years.
