title: When the Goats Are Done
---
On the morning he made himself king of [[pol:204|Khie'a]], [[per:5729|Po'ayueve]] came out into the yard and asked To'a'obi for the loan of her milking stool, and she told him he could have it when the goats were done.

He did not argue. He had lodged with them since the autumn, and he knew the order of things in that yard. He squatted against the wall of the pen with his forearms on his knees and watched her work down the line: the brown nanny with the torn ear, who kicked; the two white sisters, who leaned on each other; the old black one, who gave least and complained most. The milk rang in the jar and then hushed as the jar filled. Over the wall the wind came off the dry hills the way it always came, smelling of thorn and dust, and lifted the ash from last night's fire and set it down somewhere else.

"There's men coming in from the districts," he said. "By noon. And the priest."

"You said last night."

"They'll want me sitting down."

"Most men sit down." She shifted to the black goat, and the stool came with her, three legs in the muck. Her husband's father had cut it from a juniper root before she was born. One leg had split along the grain two winters ago and been bound, and the binding had worked loose. She knocked the leg with her heel. "Keep off that side. Lean on it and it folds. Lean the other way."

He studied the leg as gravely as if she had told him where the queen's army slept. "The other way," he said.

E'alada came out of the house with sleep in her hair and went straight to him, as she had all winter, and climbed onto his back without asking. She was nine. In the year the soldiers of Khie'a came up the road and wrote down everyone in [[set:184|Lona]], To'a'obi's mother had given her new daughter a plains name, Rain Heart, in a country where it hardly rained, because a girl with a plains name, she said, would at least be written down right; and To'a'obi had done the same for hers. Her husband had a name of the old kind. In thirty-four years no scribe had spelled it the same way twice.

"Are you king yet?" E'alada said into his ear.

"At noon."

"King of here?"

"King of Khie'a."

She thought about it, hanging from his neck. "Khie'a isn't here."

"Lona's in Khie'a. Has been since the year your mother was born. So if I'm king of Khie'a, I'm king of here." He reached back and swung her round into his lap. "That's the way of it. You take the whole thing, or you're only a man with a stick."

To'a'obi stripped the last of the black goat's milk and said nothing. There was a king of Khie'a already, and she was a queen, and twenty-three years old, and by the talk at the well she had been making holy war these three years on Onoshti, whose people spoke as the people of Lona spoke and had never taken the plains gods.

Then she stood, and wiped her hands down her skirt, and gave him the stool.

* * *

Her husband's mother had been up since before the birds, and had opinions.

"King Fire," she said, when To'a'obi came in with the milk. That was what his name meant in the plains tongue, and the old woman had found it funny since the autumn. "His mother called him King Fire. What did she think would happen?"

"Is there barley?"

"There's what there is."

There was not enough. To'a'obi went down the lane with a basket and came back with a measure from one house and half a measure from another and a promise from a third, and a twist of salt the potter's wife had been keeping for something better. The king's men had brought up a ram for [[dei:0|Atlie]] from somebody's flock; she went out and looked at its ear-notch to make sure it was not theirs. They had also brought a skin of plains beer, which the men had been told not to touch, and had touched.

She set the old woman to the curds and herself to the bread. The griddle stone heated, and the smoke went up straight for once, which the old woman said was a sign, and did not say of what.

"What do kings eat?" To'a'obi asked him at the door. "In Khie'a. You'll have seen."

He was rubbing fat into his sandals. He thought about it honestly; that was the thing about him, he always answered the question you had asked. "I saw a king eat once," he said. "At [[set:83|Khie'a]], the year of the peace. He ate bread."

"Only bread?"

"I was a long way back."

"Then they'll have bread," she said, "and curds, and what's left of the ram when Atlie's had his share, and if the men of the districts want figs they should have brought figs."

* * *

They came up the hill road all through the middle of the day: herders with their staffs and their dogs, men from the plains villages with spears and baked faces, a few old men in their best cloaks who had walked two days and wanted everyone to know it. Lona was a town you could walk round before a jar of milk turned, and by the afternoon there was nowhere in it to stand. The scribe sat on a mat by the gate and wrote the districts down as their men came in. When he had thirty-four he stopped, and carried the tablet to Po'ayueve in both hands, the way a boy carries a bird's nest.

Then the scribe began a second tablet, for the men who would carry spears.

To'a'obi was passing with bread when her husband gave his name. She saw the stylus stop above the clay. She saw the scribe ask again, and her husband say it again, slower, the way he had been saying it to scribes since he was a boy, and the scribe make a mark and scrape it out with his thumb.

Po'ayueve leaned over. "Write: the husband of To'a'obi, who lent the seat."

The scribe wrote it. The men near the gate laughed, and her husband laughed with them and looked across the yard at her. She found that her face had gone hot, and could not have said whether it was the fire. She took the bread on to the next man.

The priest came at the turn of the afternoon. He was old and narrow and had been ordained at Khie'a in the time of the queen's mother, and he had been dreading this day, To'a'obi thought, since the spring. He would not look at the king. He cut the ram's throat with the bronze knife and caught the blood in the bowl, and burned Atlie's portion on the stones until the fat spat, and all the while his lips moved and no one heard him.

They set the stool on a reed mat in the middle of the yard, where the goats slept at night. Po'ayueve sat down on it, and leaned, very slightly, the other way.

To'a'obi, at the fire with the old woman, watched him hold it. The priest poured the oil on his head, and it ran into his eyebrows, and he did not blink. The priest said the words of the making. She heard the god's name, and Khie'a, and Po'ayueve's own name, which the old man said quietly, the way you speak in a house where someone is asleep. Then the men of the districts took the name from him and shouted it, and the name went over the wall and down the hill, past the black stone that E'a Nieme had set up for [[dei:4|Lashla'e]] when he took Lona from Onoshti, and on down the road toward the plains, where there was nobody to hear it but the wind.

All through it the king sat crooked. Afterward men from the far districts would say that the new king sat like a man listening for something on his right.

When it was over he stood up too fast, and the stool went over on its side in the dust.

The yard went quiet. Men looked at the stool, and then at the priest, and the priest looked at the stool and opened his mouth.

E'alada was out from under her mother's arm before anyone could stop her. She set the stool on its legs and brushed the seat with her hand and said, loudly, in her high carrying voice, "It does that." And the men laughed, the whole yard at once, the way men laugh when they have been let off something; and Po'ayueve threw back his head and laughed louder than any of them and swung her up onto his shoulders; and the priest closed his mouth and looked at the sky, where there was nothing to see.

To'a'obi looked at the leg. It had folded, the way it always did.

* * *

By the middle of the night the beer was gone and the men of the districts were asleep in the lanes in their cloaks, with the dogs among them. The old woman had stopped muttering. To'a'obi went out to bank the fire and found the king sitting by it on the stool, still leaning the other way, from habit already.

"You want sleep," she said. "Kings need sleep."

"I've never been one. I don't know what they need." He turned a stick in the embers. "I was born the year [[evt:5020|E'a Nieme took the seat]]. Did you know that? The boy king died, eight years old, and that old man sat down in his place, and nobody asked him to. My mother said I came out shouting about it."

She sat on her heels across the fire from him.

"At [[evt:5192|Tliyiya]] I was eighteen. There were fewer than three thousand of us and more than thirty-five thousand of them, and I didn't run. Everyone says that was brave." He shook his head. "I hadn't understood yet that we'd lost. A man had to take me by the belt and pull. Then [[evt:5194|the peace]]: six districts to the queen of Mii, and twenty-four talents of silver every year for eleven years. The old man's son put his name to that. I carried some of the silver. On my back, up the road, to a foreign queen."

"And now there's another queen."

"The old man's granddaughter. [[per:5661|Niememovi]]. She was thirteen when they sat her down. I was thirty-seven. I've never seen her face." He was quiet for a while. "She gives to [[dei:5|Nieshashta]] before she fights, they say. I asked Atlie. We'll see who's listening."

"Why?" To'a'obi said.

He looked at her as if he did not understand the question, and then as if he did. "I've no wife," he said. "No son. No field. I'm forty-seven, and I've slept under other people's roofs all my life. I thought a man ought to have one thing that was his." He put his hand flat on the seat of the stool between his knees. "Even if it's borrowed."

"Can you win?"

"I don't know how to win. Nobody ever showed me." He smiled at the fire. "I know how not to run."

He lay down after that, where he was, on the ground by the hearth, with her husband's old cloak over him, and was asleep at once, the way soldiers and children are.

* * *

She took a strip of rawhide from the peg by the door and put it to soak in the water jar while the fire settled. When it was soft she sat on the ground with the stool across her knees and bound the split leg, round and round, hard, pulling each turn tight against the last and tucking the end under, the way her husband's father had shown her when she was a bride. It would shrink as it dried. By morning it would grip like a hand.

It would hold a season or two. Rawhide always did. Then it would work loose, and want doing again, by whoever was there to do it.

The plains priests said that everything turned on a great wheel, and that what ended would begin again. She had never needed a priest to tell her that. She had goats. She set the stool down beside the king's hand, good legs nearest, so that he would find it there when he woke; and then she went in to sleep, because the goats would want her before he did.
