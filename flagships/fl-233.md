title: Why Ror Keeps Its R: Relative Chronology in the Brikimo Family

The [[lan:3|Brikimo]] family shares an awkward distinction with Telnu: it has a single recorded daughter. [[lan:34|Tsekhu]] separated from the parent language about AY -368 and is still spoken. With one daughter the comparative method cannot triangulate, and every asterisked Brikimo form is a reconstruction of the weaker, internal kind. Yet in Tsekhu the parent's dated laws can actually be seen at work, and in an order. My argument is that a family of one, though it cannot give us a confident ancestor, can still give us a confident chronology.

## The evidence

The standard list gives five words:

- 'water': *lur, Tsekhu lue
- 'king': *skanoma, Tsekhu eskanoma
- 'god': *hotli, Tsekhu othli
- 'mother': *tal, Tsekhu thal
- 'silver': *raru, Tsekhu ror

The record dates fourteen changes in the parent before Tsekhu separated, and our tables give the first eight: palatalization of t and d before i and e, c. AY -2952; the merger of o into u, c. -2886; assimilation of n to a following labial or velar, c. -2716; the loss of h, c. -2650; lowering of e before a final consonant, c. -2630; open-syllable lengthening of e and a, c. -2135; the loss of final r after a vowel, c. -1938; and final devoicing of voiced stops, c. -1768. All precede the separation of Tsekhu and belong to its inheritance.

## Three laws at work

The loss of h is the plainest: *hotli gives othli, with no trace of the initial consonant. The loss of final r is equally plain in 'water': *lur gives lue, and whatever the final e may be, the r is gone.

But then 'silver', *raru, gives ror, which ends in r. This is no exception, and the reason is instructive. In AY -1938 the r of *raru was not final; a vowel still followed it. The law removed r only at the end of a word, so it passed this one by. The final vowel was lost later, leaving an r that the law, by then no longer operating, could not touch. Ror keeps its r because of the order in which things happened. One form thus yields a relative chronology: the loss of final r came before the loss of final vowels in words like this one.

The same word shows a third law. Its vowel is o, where the reconstruction has a. Compare *tal, which gives thal with its a intact. The difference lies in the syllable. In *raru the first syllable was open, and open-syllable lengthening (c. AY -2135) made its vowel long; in *tal the syllable was closed by the l, and the vowel stayed short. The long vowel was later rounded to o, the short one was not. Thal and ror make as clean a demonstration of a conditioned change as five words can offer.

## An apparent exception, and what is not explained

The merger of o into u, dated c. AY -2886, should have removed every o from the parent's vocabulary. Yet Tsekhu has o in eskanoma and othli (the o of ror, as we have seen, comes from a). Either the reconstructions are wrong, or Tsekhu later lowered u back to o. The second explanation is the more economical, and it gains support from lue: if the u of *lur was lowered to o and that o was later broken into a diphthong after the initial consonant, lue is exactly what we would expect. I offer this as a hypothesis; the law that would prove it is not among those listed here.

Some features belong to Tsekhu's own later history and lie outside the parent's laws altogether. The th of thal and othli is a Tsekhu spirantization of t. The initial e of eskanoma, from *skanoma, is a prothetic vowel of a kind languages often add before s and a consonant. Four of the eight dated laws (palatalization, nasal assimilation, the lowering of e, and final devoicing) find no witness in these words at all. Their dates rest on fuller evidence than our list reproduces; five words do not confirm eight laws.

## Conclusion

[[lan:34|Tsekhu]] cannot tell us with certainty what the ancestor sounded like, since there is no second daughter to check it against. But it can tell us in what order the ancestor changed, and that is worth having. The lesson of ror is that apparent exceptions are often the best evidence of chronology we have. In a family as thin as [[lan:3|Brikimo]], we should prize them rather than explain them away.
