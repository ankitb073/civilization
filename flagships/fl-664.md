No document in the archive names [[per:29391|Nandithim]]. No letter, seal, epitaph or page of accounts of his survives. When Nanjil excavated [[set:269|Dambulu]] in AY 4919, its eleven inscribed finds jumped from 3057 to 3326; the merchant's whole life fell into that gap. What remains is the record's bare entry: a man of the Kubis, born in 3215, whose name means "dawn"; founder in 3241 of a house at Dambulu dealing in copper, horses, grain and spices; father of one daughter; dead of natural causes in 3261. And one fact more: the [[hou:1112|House of Dundivu]] lasted 267 years. Of the some 2,500 merchant houses, companies and banks the record knows, only one lasted longer, and none passed through as many hands.

## A town after a peace

He was born a subject of [[pol:1206|Kambambül]], an empire of about 10.6 million people in the survey of the 3210s, in the year the regency for the young emperor [[per:29004|Nambuvethi]] ended. Of his childhood the record says nothing. He was twenty-six at the [[evt:29836|Founding of the House of Dundivu]] in 3241. The year before, the [[evt:29783|Second Kambambül–Kaazhe War]] had ended in the Treaty of Mungu, which gave the empire twenty-eight districts and four towns and promised fifteen years of truce. The record gives no reasons; it shows only that he began when that frontier had fallen quiet.

Dambulu was an inland town of about 27,000 people in the record's count for AY 3200, under half the size of the capital, [[set:246|Kamvamvil]]. It already had a house, Kumbivi, founded in 3220 and dealing in the same four goods and in cloth. In his working life the record knows only six merchant houses seated anywhere in the empire, two of them in this one town.

The prices he met were rising. In the 3230s a measure of grain at Dambulu cost 6.4 grams of silver; in the 3240s it cost 9.5, about 0.64 [[cur:665|Kyivu]]. A horse went from 216 grams to 262, a mina of spices from 76 to 106. Grain rose almost as steeply at the capital, though elsewhere in the world it barely moved. Whether his house profited by the rise, no book survives to say.

## Trading with the enemy

The record's route survey for the 3200s gives Dambulu four partners: Khambizhinu, a port; Hunjingivji; Ajü; and Hil, whose road carried more than twice the traffic of any other. Hil and Ajü lay outside the empire, in the Kingdom of Pièvi, and in 3246 Kambambül went to war with Pièvi for territory. That year Nashigindu of the House of Mithlu, a merchant at Hunjingivji, kept a day-book that survives on papyrus. In its margin he noted: "The roads are unsafe because of the [[evt:29887|Kambambül–Pièvi War]]." It is the nearest the archive comes to Nandithim's voice, and it is someone else's.

The court remembered that war differently. A rock relief at Lamligul, its last lines lost, says that Nambuvethi "defeated Pyivi in Lamlikulu. He killed 7,300 soldiers." The record gives 1,755 Pièvi dead, and the army there was led not by the emperor but by his general Natange. By 3250 Kambambül had lost 3,244 men and Pièvi 8,339, and the Treaty of Aguga took eleven districts and made Pièvi a vassal.

Seven years later came the [[evt:29996|Holy War of Kambambül against Khishkhis]]. Here I must declare an interest: that kingdom was ruled from Nambi, as my own country is. The Kambambül chronicle, written in 3305, claims 22,475 enemy dead at Agni in 3258 against 656 of its own; the record gives 5,948 and 1,662. The annals of Khishkhis say only: "There was a battle at Agne; the army withdrew." One side multiplied the enemy's dead nearly fourfold and cut its own by three-fifths; the other could not bring itself to write the word defeat. Nandithim died in 3261, in the middle of this war, aged forty-six. The bronze Treaty of Luyululi, sworn two years later, carries an article he never saw: "The merchants of Kambambül shall trade in the markets of Khishkhizi free of all tolls."

## A daughter's house

The one who could use that article was [[per:29605|Natithmi]], his only recorded child, who became head of the house in 3261 at thirty-one. If the dates are right, her father was fifteen when she was born; the record names no mother. Her name means "honey". Eleven years earlier the empire itself had passed to a daughter, Nakagum, under a law that favoured sons.

The House of Kumbivi ended in 3262 with the death of its founder. It had never had a second head. Dundivu had. [[per:29605|Natithmi]] led it until her death in 3272. After her came Naluvukh, whom the record ties to no one before him, then five generations of his descendants, and later two more women among the heads: twelve in all.

The house outlived the empire. Kambambül broke into six successor states in 3300, and Dambulu became the capital of one of them, the Kingdom of Ntsinakhizi. Kabav armies besieged it in 3409 and 3410, but in the house's lifetime the town was never sacked. When Ntsinakhizi collapsed in 3496, Dambulu became the capital of the new Kingdom of Tambulu. The house was dissolved in 3508, the year its twelfth head died.

## What the record cannot weigh

The state cult of [[mov:24|Uthungala]] taught that prosperity was a sign of divine favour, and asked for justice in dealings and moderation in all things. What Nandithim believed, the record does not say. It lists no loan from his house to any crown, though his empire fought two wars in his twenty years as head; whether from caution, principle or a small purse, the evidence cannot tell.

I cannot give him a face, a voice or a motive, only the shape of what he made. The emperor of his day set up a relief claiming 7,300 dead where 1,755 fell. The merchant of Dambulu claimed nothing, and left an arrangement sturdy enough to pass through eleven successions and outlive the empire that saw it founded by 208 years.
