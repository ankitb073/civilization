title: The Priests Said
---
It came out of a clean sky in the eighth year of the queen, a month after the vines had flowered, and everyone in [[set:148|Dadavli]] took it for the god.

Nalüving was on the temple roof, spreading barley for the doves. He felt it before he heard it, a push against the breastbone like a door slammed in a far room, and then the sound arrived, low and long, rolling in over the roofs from somewhere beyond the western wall and going on eastward as if it had business there. The doves went up all together. Down in the court somebody dropped a jar. It came a second time, and a third, and after that only the clatter of wings and the dogs of the lower town barking at nothing, under the hard blue of a summer morning without a thread of cloud in it.

When he came down the ladder the priests were out on the steps in whatever they had been wearing, and the eldest, who had served [[dei:73|Dith]] in that house for fifty years, stood with his face lifted and his eyes closed, the way he stood when rain came after a long dry.

'He has spoken,' the eldest said.

They were all looking east. The whole city looked east that year, the way a man with a bad tooth keeps going back to it with his tongue. East was Dalüdhim, where a man named Naguf had made himself a king, and since the [[evt:15389|Secession of Dalüdhim]] the east road had been full of the queen's men going out and of talk coming back.

'It came from the west,' Nalüving said.

Nobody answered him. He was thirty-eight and had been a priest for eighteen years, which in that house made him young.

West of the wall lay the vineyards, and past the vineyards the brown slopes where the Nïl ran their sheep, and past those the hills, and past the hills higher hills, where people lived who did not talk as the city talked, and beyond them nothing that anyone in Dadavli had ever needed to know. Toward noon a thing stood up out of that country such as none of them had seen: a grey stalk, taller than any cloud, that spread at the top the way a pine spreads and then did not move. People climbed the walls to look at it. Children were lifted onto shoulders. It stood there all afternoon, the colour of a wasps' nest, and no rain fell out of it, and in the evening the sun went down behind it and the western sky turned colours there were no names for.

* * *

After that [[dei:77|Ugash]] rose red.

He came up over the eastern hills the colour of a coal that has been blown on, and he stayed that colour far into the morning, and a man could look straight into him and not blink. It was the middle of summer and the women in the market wore their winter shawls. The doves sat fluffed along the ridge of the roof and would not come down for barley. The flagstones of the temple court, which by noon in any other summer could not be stood on barefoot, stayed cool all day, and in the afternoons the old priests laid their palms on them and said nothing.

The city came to the god. It brought kids and lambs, oil in jars, honey cakes, a bronze bowl somebody's grandmother had kept wrapped in cloth for forty years. The eldest made the sacrifices with his own hands. In the evenings he stood on the steps and told the people what the priests had agreed among themselves: that the gods were angry.

He did not say with whom, and so the city supplied it. In the market the gods were angry with the rebels of the east, who had set themselves against the queen. In the lower town they were angry with the queen, who had taken the town's sons east in the middle of the year to fight them. On the palace hill nobody said anything, but a steward came down for a queen of [[pol:601|Yif]] who was sixty-eight and had a war in the east and now this, to ask with great courtesy where exactly the god's displeasure lay. The eldest told him that the god would make it known in the god's own time. The steward bowed and carried that back up the hill, which was what stewards were for.

* * *

The herdsman came on the fortieth day.

Nalüving found him at the sheep-pens inside the western gate with a dozen ewes: a hill man in a felt cloak, standing a little apart from the other sellers. The ewes were grey. Not the grey of a road: a fine, heavy grey, like flour from a mill that ground stones, that came off on the hand and would not brush away. The buyers pushed their fingers into the fleeces and wiped them on their clothes and walked on.

'A month and more,' the man said, when Nalüving asked him. He spoke Nïl the way a man walks on ice. 'The hill opened. Then for three days the sky came down. Grey, like this.' He took a pinch of it from the nearest ewe and let it fall. 'Roofs break under it. It lies on the grass. The sheep eat the grass with it, and the belly —' he made a swelling with his hands. 'These are the ones that did not eat.'

'Which hill?'

The herdsman said a word. Nalüving asked him to say it again, and he said it again, slowly, as to a child. Lamliladol.

'What does it mean?'

The man looked for the Nïl word and did not find it. He laid his hand flat on the air and then humped it. 'Old,' he said. 'Old hill. Only this. Nobody gave it a better name. It never did anything.'

'And now?'

'Now it does.' He looked past Nalüving at the wall and the roofs, and at the temple standing up over the roofs. 'At night it is red. All night. Like a hand you hold over a lamp.'

A dealer came and weighed out silver for the ewes on his little scale, against his stone [[cur:313|Agü]] weights, and the herdsman took what he was given without counting it, which told Nalüving more than the ash had. When the dealer had gone Nalüving said, because it was what a priest said, that the temple of the storm god stood just above them, if the man wished to make an offering. That thunder, after all.

The herdsman shook his head, not unkindly. 'It was not thunder,' he said. 'And he is not ours.'

Nalüving did not press him. The gods of [[mov:11|Kyif]] belonged to their own people and had never gone out looking for strangers. He gave the man the bread he had brought for his own noon meal, and the man ate it standing up, all of it, and went away.

* * *

'A mountain,' the eldest said that evening.

'He called it the old hill.'

'And who sets fire to a mountain? Who throws fire down out of the sky?' The eldest spread his hands, as if the matter lay open in them. 'He has spoken. You heard him yourself.'

'I heard something,' Nalüving said. 'A man has walked for a month to tell me what it was, and not one of us has walked as far as the vineyards to look.'

The eldest was quiet for a while. Then he said, 'The teachers say that no one owns the truth.'

'They do.'

'Then I don't own it. Neither do you, and neither does your herdsman.' He got up slowly. 'What the priests can do is say what the priests say. The gods are angry. Go and look at the vines.'

* * *

The vines did not ripen.

Najikh, who had dressed the temple's vines under the western wall for forty years, as his father had before him, brought the first basket up to the steps in the autumn all the same, because the first basket was the god's in a fat year or a thin one, and a due is a due. The grapes were hard and green and no bigger than peas. The skins had never taken the bloom. In the basket they rattled.

'It's not a vintage,' Najikh said. 'It's a basket of gravel.'

'He'll take it.'

'He'd better. There's nothing else.' The old man put one in his mouth, bit it, and spat it into his palm. 'Angry,' he said. 'If they were angry they'd have sent hail. Hail I understand. This is only cold.'

The barley came in thin as well; the women made offerings to [[dei:74|Bingi]] at the shrine by the granary, and bread went up and kept going.

They sat on the temple steps, the old man and the priest, with the basket between them.

'My father had a year like this,' Najikh said. 'When he was a boy. The sun went sick and it was cold in summer, and the vines were no good, and it went on, he said, year after year. Long enough that the children thought that was how the world was.' He rolled a green grape between his finger and thumb. 'And then one spring it wasn't.'

'What did the priests say then?'

'Ask your eldest, he'd know. Probably that the gods were angry.' The old man laughed. 'Everything comes round. That's the one thing I was ever taught that I believe.'

The sun went down while they sat there, small and red in the haze over the western hills, and they went on watching after it had gone, because the west did not go dark. Long after it should have been night the sky there kept a red glow low along the hills, deep and steady, not like any sunset. Najikh stopped rolling the grape.

'Look at that,' he said.

Nalüving thought of the herdsman's hand held over a lamp, and the light coming through the fingers. He did not say it. They sat on, and the moon came up behind them over the eastern hills, over the road and the war, and it came up red as well, so that for an hour there was a red thing on either side of the city and the two of them sat between, turning their heads from one to the other like men at a market who cannot decide. Then the cold drove them in.

* * *

In the spring of the ninth year of [[per:14673|Nangumbich]] the eldest told him to make the record.

The temple kept a tablet for every year out of the ordinary, in reed baskets in a room behind the shrine, and there were words for such a year, as there were for a flood or a fever. The eldest had learned them as a boy, copying old tablets in that room, and Nalüving had learned them after him. He said them over now with his eyes shut, like a prayer worn smooth.

'The sun rose red and gave no heat. A haze lay over the sky for many months. Snow fell in the month of harvest. The vines did not ripen. The old men had never seen its like. The priests said the gods were angry. Travellers spoke of a mountain that burned in the far country. The birds did not sing in the spring. At night the moon was the colour of blood.' He opened his eyes. 'Take what is true of this year. Leave the rest. That is all a record is.'

Nalüving took clay from the tub, kneaded it, flattened it on the board and cut the edges square. He wrote the opening the way openings were always written, the temple, the city, the year of the queen. Then he went through the old words one at a time, as the buyers had gone through the herdsman's ewes, with his fingers.

"The sun rose red and gave no heat."

"The vines did not ripen."

He stopped. The haze he let go; the red sun said it already. There had been no snow at harvest, only cold, and the old men had seen its like, or one of them had, and told his son. He passed over those and came to the gods and wrote it, and the eldest got up and stood behind him and read it over his shoulder, moving his lips.

"The priests said the gods were angry."

The eldest was silent so long that Nalüving turned round.

'Said,' the eldest said.

He had spoken those words since he was ten years old, and stood on the steps all summer telling the city the gods were angry, and he had never once, Nalüving saw, heard what the old line said. Some scribe dead longer than anyone could count had pressed that word into the clay, and it had waited in the baskets for him. Nalüving watched him hear it now. It was not a comfortable thing to watch.

'[[dei:76|Jilum]] keep whoever wrote that first,' the eldest said at last, and went back to his bench and shut his eyes.

The mountain came next. Nalüving held the stylus over the clay and said the line to himself, travellers, a mountain, the far country, and it would not go in. The herdsman had not been a traveller. He had been a man walking his last ewes to market. And the country was not the far country. It lay inside the queen's own borders, and it had come down on the roofs of people whose word for it was the only name it had. There were no other words the tablet knew how to take. He left the line out.

What came next was the night. He wrote *At night the*, and stopped again, because he had seen two red things that autumn and the old words had room for only one. The moon was in the old words, and true. The west was not in any words at all. He sat a long time with the doves walking on the roof above him, and then he wrote one of them, and then *colour of blood*, and then his own name at the foot, because that was how it was done.

He laid the tablet out in the court to dry. In any other year the sun would have had it hard by evening. It lay there eleven days.

Long afterwards, at a time no one wrote down, fire got into the room behind the shrine. It baked the tablets harder than any sun had, and it ate at their edges. From the last line of his it took what he had seen at night, the moon or the west, and the rest it kept: the sun, the vines, and what the priests said.

**Author's note.** Nalüving's tablet, PAL·RE·00062, written at Dadavli in AY 1941, is damaged by fire at the edges; the lines quoted from it are as they survive, and what stood between "At night the" and "colour of blood" is lost. The eldest's old words come from earlier accounts of dark years (PAL·RE·00010, PAL·RE·00020, PAL·RE·00023, PAL·RE·00055); the oldest of them speaks of a burning mountain, and Nalüving's does not.
