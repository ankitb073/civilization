title: What Listeners Could Check: The First Talenonoy–Nonenga War in Two Chronicles
---
The two accounts of the [[evt:1888|First Talenonoy–Nonenga War]] (AY 525–528) agree on more than rival chronicles usually do. Both have three battles, all won by [[pol:54|Talenonoy]]. Both record the death of Donupmalin, and both end with the fall of the Nonenga capital. They disagree on who started the war, on the dead and on the land. The pattern is not random. Both scribes wrote within a decade of the war, for audiences that included veterans. What a listener could check, they kept. What no listener could check, they bent.

## The tablets

The Nonenga account (PAL·CH·00244, Radape, excavated at Nunnga in AY 4922) was set down by Shayanin "in the 13th year of Nonenga", AY 532, four years after the conquest. It is fragmentary. Shayanin was about twenty during the war. The Talenonoy account (PAL·CH·00243, Nunmula, excavated at Taalinnuy in AY 4908) is Buluemin's, dated "in the 32nd year of Anat", AY 537, under the queen who had reigned throughout the war. Both scribes use local forms for the battlefields: Noltennga for Nuwkennga and Deratno for Jerathnu.

## Field by field

*Who began it.* In the record, Talenonoy attacked to seize territory. Shayanin says so: "Anat of Talenonoy, forgetting the oaths his fathers swore, set his hand against Nonenga." Buluemin reverses it: "there came word that Midaltat of Nonenga had gathered an army against Talenonoy. Our king could not suffer the insult."

*The battles.* The record has three engagements in [[reg:7|Tunnge]]: the [[evt:1904|Battle of Nuwkennga (AY 526)]], the [[evt:1916|Battle of Nuwkennga (AY 527)]] and the [[evt:1923|Battle of Jerathnu (AY 528)]]. Talenonoy's commander Nguylumonin won all three. Both scribes have all three, correctly placed and dated. Shayanin explains each defeat differently: "in a great storm", "when the allies failed to come", "because the omens had been neglected". Three defeats with three different excuses suggest a scribe choosing from a repertoire, not remembering.

*The dead.* Here both scribes were free. The record gives Talenonoy 638 dead (156, 223 and 259) and [[pol:73|Nonenga]] 24 (6, 10 and 8). Buluemin reports 87 enemy dead (22, 36 and 29), more than three and a half times the record, against enemy musters of 55, 50 and 41 where the record has 30, 27 and 22. For his own side he admits no more than about a quarter of the true cost. Shayanin inverts the picture. He gives his own side 14 dead (3, 6 and 5) and Talenonoy 2,262 (553, 791 and 918), each about three and a half times the record's figure. In his telling, Nonenga lost three battles while killing some 160 of the enemy for each man it lost. No listener could have counted the dead of three fields, and neither scribe expected to be checked.

(The record's own proportions, with armies of several thousand losing hundreds to forces of a few dozen, are odd in the same direction. Shayanin pushed an odd truth further.)

*Commanders.* On the death of Donupmalin at Jerathnu, a fact any veteran would know, the scribes agree with the record and with each other. Buluemin has "Donubmalin himself was killed, and his head was brought to the king". Shayanin has "There fell Donupmalin, whose name shall be remembered."

*Outcome and land.* The [[evt:1924|Siege of Nunnga (AY 528)]] ended the war, and the [[evt:1925|Conquest of Nonenga]] annexed all thirty of its districts. Buluemin's gates of Nonenga "could hold out no longer", and "Nonenga is no more, and its people serve Talenonoy". But he counts sixty-eight districts won, more than twice the whole kingdom. Shayanin records the fall of the city ("the royal city was taken. The gods had turned their faces from us"), but no surrender and no cession. His war ends in an honourable peace, with nothing given up.

## Two audiences

Buluemin wrote for a victorious court that needed the war to be defensive, cheap and rich in land, and every one of those claims lay beyond a listener's reach. Shayanin wrote for the survivors of a kingdom that had lasted eight years. He needed its last war to have been unlucky rather than lost, and its enemy to have paid in blood. Neither could deny a battle, a dead commander or a fallen capital. Those things happened in front of witnesses, and the chronicles keep them. Everything else was negotiable.
