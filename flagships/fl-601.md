Later ages called him the Victorious, and the name is earned if victories are only counted. In fifty-four years on the throne of [[pol:370|Yennga]], [[per:12386|Tamon]] lost no battle that he commanded himself. His stelae say so in the flat, repeated formula of the Khave script: great king, king of Yennga, defeated his enemy, killed so many soldiers, erected this stele for his lady. The record agrees that he won. What it adds is the size of what he defeated, and what the winning cost. A biography of Tamon has to hold both.

## A child among the dead

He was born in AY 1636 into the Shadhjevan house, a Jinru dynasty seated at Yinnge that had held the throne since 1318. His father was [[per:12275|Lomejeyshan II]], his mother [[per:12382|Lame]]. The family records are a list of small graves. One brother, born in 1634, died in his first year, before Tamon was born. Another, born in the same year as Tamon and perhaps his twin, died at seven. His father died in 1642, aged thirty-two, and the throne passed to the eldest surviving son, [[per:12384|Lomejeyshan III]], a boy of seven, with Lame as regent. In 1645 that boy died too, at about ten. The [[evt:12339|Accession of Tamon]] made a nine-year-old king, and Lame regent for the second time in three years.

It is Lame, not her son, whom we should picture behind the first great act of the reign. The [[evt:12316|Second Yennga–Ttogannga War]] had begun under her first regency. In 1647 it ended with the [[evt:12385|Treaty of Riniy (AY 1647)]], and its terms were harsh. Ttogannga gave up twelve districts and the town of Yishdhagshal, promised tribute for thirty-eight years, and became Yennga's vassal under a ten-year truce. The record lists the eleven-year-old Tamon as signatory beside the Ttogannga queen Duynit, a woman of forty-six. We may doubt that he understood what he sealed. Lame's regency is recorded until 1652. She lived until 1681 and watched her son become a conqueror.

## The first wars of the grown king

From 1654 Tamon fought the [[evt:12471|First Yennga–Mikhuthitsu War]], a war of conquest against [[pol:494|Mikhuthitsu]] and its king Lomaghonin. His first stele, the Victory stele of Tamon for the Battle of Ashash in 1655, claims 2,000 enemy dead. The record gives about 800. More telling is what no stele records. In 1660 and again in 1661 Yennga's armies were beaten, and the second defeat cost nearly 4,700 men. Tamon was not in command at either. In 1662 he took the field himself at the [[evt:12571|Battle of Lininadhe (AY 1662)]], with about twelve thousand men against fewer than five thousand, and won. Lininadhe was sacked that year, for the fifth time in its history. The [[evt:12600|Treaty of Lininadhe (AY 1665)]] gave Yennga nine districts and a ten-year truce. Its clay text, written by the scribe Toton and scorched at the edges, has the king say that the storm-god went before him and that tribute was sent to his camp. The surviving terms mention land, not tribute.

In the same years his own kingdom split. In 1658 a province of the kingdom seceded as [[pol:556|Nebanga]] under [[per:12549|Ngadhemon]], and the [[evt:12514|Second Yennga Civil War]] began. The figures deserve attention. At the [[evt:12537|Battle of Himi (AY 1659)]] the Nebanga general Mernan attacked with nearly 23,000 men a royal army of fewer than 17,000. He lost nearly eight thousand of them. Yennga lost about fourteen hundred. The Nebanga account of the Second Yennga Civil War, written by the palace scribe Menen, admits that its own host "fell back to the walls" in a great storm with the loss of 4,311 men, yet claims 2,711 royal dead. The record nearly doubles the first number and halves the second. The rebels never again outnumbered the king's forces. At the battle of Linidhagshal the Nebanga commander Vademenen was killed, and that year the [[evt:12579|Suppression of the Nebanga Revolt]] returned 121 districts to the crown. The terms included an amnesty. Menen's account ends without bitterness, only with the question: "Who will now remember the kings of old?"

## Victories over almost no one

The pattern of the later reign is a great army sent against very small ones. In the [[evt:12657|Fifth Baan Trade War]] Tamon marched on the city-state of [[pol:542|Nubi]]. At the [[evt:12681|Battle of Tinlamvukhnir (AY 1673)]] he led 20,294 men against 249 under Khuknakhuanavin. Nubi lost eighty. Tamon's rock relief at Tinlamvukhnir commemorates a second battle there in 1675, fought by his general, and claims two hundred dead where the record has twenty-eight. The Nubi account by the scribe Livan claims forty-two Yennga dead, and the record says eleven. So both sides inflated their figures, in opposite directions, and the one number neither scribe gives is the most revealing. The king brought some eighty men for every one the city put in the field. The [[evt:12704|Treaty of Tinlamvukhnir (AY 1675)]], sworn with Kafbafnathbin, brought two districts, an indemnity and trading rights.

At home these were years of grief that no stele records. Of his three daughters, the second died in 1671 at five and the third in 1673 in her first year. His younger brother died in 1670, his queen in 1680, his mother in 1681. Of his whole family only his eldest daughter, [[per:12945|Refnath]], survived him.

In the [[evt:12808|Second Yennga–Mikhuthitsu War]] he faced a Mikhuthitsu restored by Wunkan, a rebel who had made himself king in 1684. At the [[evt:12843|First Battle of Kadhdanul (AY 1688)]] the defender Lwukafbafin met 26,200 men with about 2,200 and lost 216. Tamon's basalt stele, the best preserved of his victory stelae, commemorates the battle his general fought there in 1687 and claims 1,400 dead. The record has 223. The [[evt:12845|Treaty of Kadhdanul (AY 1688)]] brought three districts and a small tribute. Wunkan was executed the following year.

Then Nebanga rose again, and this enemy was not small. In the [[evt:12868|Third Yennga Civil War]] the rebel king [[per:12895|Olokolvan]] fell in 1691 leading some 25,400 men against a royal army of about the same size. His successor's general Podolen tried with about 12,500 the next year. In 1693 Olokolvan's son [[per:12909|Roro]], twenty-seven years old, came out with some 14,600 men and then signed the surrender at Tinlamvukhnir. The later Nebanga account says only that "the gates were opened to the enemy." Yennga lost some 3,700 men putting down rebels who mustered as many as twenty-five thousand.

## The old king and the kingdom after him

The record gives us a kingdom that grew. By the end of the reign Yennga held over three hundred districts and about eight million people, its army had risen past forty-six thousand, and merchants priced oil in the royal Mava. A letter of 1682, from one Ramagonen to his agent, reports olive oil at 1.19 Mava the jar in Yennga and tells him to be quick. That is the reign as most of its subjects knew it. The same accounts show a realm that was never settled. Its measures of order stayed low across the reign, lowest of all in the years of the Nebanga secession and low again in his last two decades.

Tamon died a natural death in 1699, aged sixty-three. The [[evt:12937|Accession of Refnath]] put his daughter on the throne, and she reigned until 1708. Her successor is remembered as the Last. In 1721 the kingdom collapsed into six successor states, one of them seated at Nivanga, the old rebel capital of Nebanga, which kept its independence for a generation. The name of Yennga survived in a new empire at Yinnge, but the realm Tamon held together for half a century lasted twenty-two years after him.

He should not be judged by the stelae, which were written to be believed and not to be read closely. Nor should he be judged only by the numbers that expose them. A boy who buried a father and two brothers, one perhaps his twin, before he was ten, and who was handed a war and a treaty before he could read them, spent his life making the kingdom hold. It held while he lived, and the people who paid for it were his own soldiers.
