A translator learns a ruler's reputation from how his enemies spell him. The historian of Vami, writing five years after their war, calls him Maxe Yweve and says he was "swollen with pride". The historian of Piesi'a, a generation later, has him "breaking the peace". His own scribes called him great king of [[pol:924|Yiotl]]. His granddaughter, when she came to the throne, bore his name. [[per:23952|Mashe Yueve I]] took a crown that was not his, and his house kept it for a hundred and forty-six years. This chapter tries to explain both facts.

## The usurper

He was born in AY 2715, a man of the La'im; his name means "Ten Fire". The record does not name his parents, and that silence is the first fact of his life. Yiotl was one of the states that had risen from the breakup of the old Ana Empire two centuries earlier. Since 2700 it had been ruled by [[per:23457|Khisha]], who by 2743 was seventy-eight. That year she died, the record marks a crisis, and the [[evt:24132|Usurpation of Mashe Yueve I]] put a man of twenty-eight on her throne. The record does not say that he hastened her end, and I see no reason to suspect it. An old queen died, and he was ready.

He had married a woman three years younger, and by then they had two daughters, born in 2735 and 2736. A third, [[per:24449|Ea Ada]], followed in 2746. There was no son. His wife died in 2763. Yiotl's law favoured the eldest child, and a usurper with only daughters had reason to make his house worth inheriting.

The record makes the usurpation itself a cause of three of his wars. A man without ancestors needed victories, and he looked for them at once.

## Vami: the general-king

The [[evt:24139|Yiotl–Vami War]] began in 2744 against the [[pol:953|Vami]] Empire. Yiotl's own lost account, known from a single quoted sentence, says that the Vami ruler [[per:23609|Fikhia]] "broke the peace and sent raiders across the border". The record names Yiotl as the attacker. Whoever began it, the new king fought it well. At the [[evt:24145|Battle of Bathshi (AY 2745)]] he led 54,793 men against 31,473 under [[per:23975|Ia Lixtla]], and lost 3,937 men while killing 8,454. Few battles of the age show an attacker losing fewer men than the defender. At the [[evt:24155|Battle of Ash (AY 2746)]] he met Fikhia himself, 67,915 against 16,595. That victory cost him 10,279 men to the enemy's 6,559.

The Vami historian passes over Bathshi in silence. For Ash he blames "treachery" and claims his side lost 3,663 men to Yiotl's 27,806. A third battle, in 2747, was won by Yiotl's general at Tanshedi. A basalt stele there credits the king with 13,300 enemy dead, about double the truth. That is restrained by the standards of royal monuments. The [[evt:24167|Treaty of Tanshedi (AY 2747)]], sworn at [[set:152|Tanshedi]], took thirty-six districts and four towns. Its bronze text orders that it be "read aloud in the markets of both lands". The Vami historian describes the same cession as "some villages of no account ... which had always been poor". Over the whole war Yiotl lost 16,195 men and Vami 21,548. The defeat left Vami unstable for a generation: civil wars in 2753 and 2781 are traced in part to these battles.

## Los and Tinim

Then he let a general do the fighting. His commander Tlamfen Luesha, a near contemporary, won all three battles of the [[evt:24250|Yiotl–Los War]] (2754–2757). This did not stop the king's stonemasons: a stele for the first of them credits Mashe Yueve with 57,500 dead, more than six times the record's figure. The [[evt:24295|Conquest of Los]], sealed at [[set:127|Lus]] in 2757, ended the Kingdom of [[pol:739|Los]] after more than six centuries. Its last queen, [[per:23598|Yi Luesh'a]], had reigned for twenty-one years and signed away thirty-two districts. In the same year the king built a temple to the goddess of war. Its foundation tablet asks only that she "protect the king".

The [[evt:24386|First Yiotl–Tinim War]] (2765–2772) was waged against [[pol:1049|Tinim]], a kingdom barely five years old. The general fought the first three battles, two of them against defending forces of twenty-nine and twenty-seven men. In 2772, at fifty-seven, the king took the field once more. At the [[evt:24493|Battle of Tuzhi (AY 2772)]] he led 90,948 men against 7,573 under [[per:24250|Sasxisiüp]], losing 4,756 and killing 2,813, and [[set:27|Tuzhi]] was sacked. His stele claims 19,900 dead. The [[evt:24495|Treaty of Tuzhi (AY 2772)]], sworn with Tinim's young queen [[per:24200|Khizhiwmamxi]], took twenty-four districts and eighteen years of tribute. The record counts the sack of Tuzhi among the causes of Tinim's collapse in 2816.

## The last war and the daughter

His last war was opportunism pure and simple. In 2780 [[pol:999|Piesi'a]] was badly beaten by Vami, and two years later Yiotl attacked it. Generals fought all four battles of the [[evt:24597|Yiotl–Piesi'a War]]. The Piesi'a historian accuses Mashe Yueve of sending raiders across the border, and that, for once, matches the record's roles. He claims Yiotl lost 22,469 men at one battle against 797 of his own, and concludes that "nothing of worth was lost". The [[evt:24644|Treaty of Tanshedi (AY 2786)]] was sworn on the same ground as the first, with Queen [[per:24385|Ish'o Imin]]. It took nineteen districts and two towns, imposed thirty-two years of tribute and vassalage, and left a boundary stone in the new lands. Piesi'a broke apart in 2833. The war had cost Yiotl 45,120 men.

The last years were years of loss. His two elder daughters died in 2776 and 2782, and his old general died in 2782 too. Mashe Yueve died in 2789, aged seventy-four, of natural causes. The [[evt:24678|Accession of Ea Ada]] passed the throne to his only surviving child, then forty-three. She proved a capable heir: she renewed his war against Tinim and commanded Yiotl's army herself against an invasion. In 2804 her daughter succeeded her under her grandfather's name, Mashe Yueve, and reigned forty-one years. His house held the throne until Yiotl itself collapsed in 2889.

Over his forty-six years the empire grew from about 205 districts to 296, and from under nine million people to over fourteen million. Its internal order, middling when he seized power, held steady. The verdict the record allows is unusual for a usurper. He was a genuinely able soldier: he commanded in three battles and won all three, one of them at a cost lower than the enemy's. He was also a king who put his name on his general's victories, and who chose his last war because a neighbour had just been beaten. The Vami historian's charge of pride is partisan. The Piesi'a historian's charge of opportunism, in this case, is simply accurate.
