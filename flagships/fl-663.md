When the peace with Munu was sworn in AY 3248, King Lafmodhalen stood at the enemy's altar and told the story of the war. His clerks took the speech down, and the fair copy of 3249 survives (PAL·PM·00071). Of the first battle at Nidhinge he says only this: "my captains met the host of Mono, and the men of Mono fell before them as the barley falls before the sickle." Those two words, *my captains*, are as close as any document of the age comes to the man who commanded there.

His name was [[per:29360|Tafvavsheyen]]. He fought eleven battles over forty years and won all eleven. The record holds thirty-nine commanders who fought eleven battles or more, and he is the only one of them who never lost. Nobody in the record fought more battles without a defeat. Yet no document in the archive names him. This chapter tries to give him his name back and to ask what his victories were worth.

## A name and nothing more

The record gives him a birth in AY 3207, a people, the Jinru, and a natural death in 3278 at seventy-one. His name means *bull victory*. It names no father, no mother, no wife and no children, and it says nothing of how he rose. We meet him first in 3235, aged twenty-eight, as a general of [[pol:1139|Lienonoshal]]. By the end of the 3220s that empire counted 310 districts, some twenty-six million people and about 210,000 men under arms. Its faith, [[mov:2|Jinru]], taught that prosperity is a sign of divine favour.

His first war was the [[evt:29759|Eighth Tuvizhinge Trade War]], fought against the city-state of [[pol:1267|Tubizhinga]], which at the end of the 3220s had six districts and some 570,000 people. At the [[evt:29775|Battle of Nunuunge (AY 3235)]] he led 131,442 men against 1,277, better than a hundred to one, and lost 110 of them to the defenders' 164. Lienonoshal sacked Nunuunge that year and burned its archives. The record does not say who gave the order. Nambili's excavation of 4952 found the layer, a "burnt destruction layer, ash and fallen brick, dated to AY 3235" (PAL·EX·00143). At the [[evt:29785|Battle of Poynge (AY 3236)]] he beat 1,491 men with 117,735, and the Tubizhinga general, [[per:29361|Kutmanin]], died on the field at forty-five.

The old emperor Natdatathrelen died in 3236 as well, and [[per:29364|Lafmodhalen]] seized the throne in what the record marks as a crisis. The record gives only the year, so we cannot tell whether Poynge was won for the old house or for the usurper. It was the usurper who signed the peace at Nunuunge, and Tafvavsheyen served him and his heirs for the rest of his life.

## Four sovereigns, four steles

The record counts the usurpation among the causes of the [[evt:29866|19th Tunnge Trade War]] against [[pol:1232|Munu]] (3243–3248). Lafmodhalen led at Virnadhu himself in 3244 and left the rest to his general. At the [[evt:29883|Battle of Nidhinge (AY 3245)]] Tafvavsheyen met [[per:29304|Bithbunbunen]] with 69,915 men against 35,281, barely two to one. He never again had so slight an edge. Nidhinge was also his costliest field. He lost 2,710 men there, a third of all the men he lost in forty years, and Munu lost 7,626. If the record holds evidence of skill rather than mere weight, it is this battle.

At the [[evt:29895|Battle of Jerathnu (AY 3247)]] he faced Munu's king, [[per:29276|Batathren]], in person, with 124,730 men against 22,329. The record scores the win as barely decisive: Lienonoshal lost 1,084 men and Munu 1,676. The oration says Batathren's men "lay so thick upon the field that the crows could not eat them all." At the [[evt:29906|Battle of Nidhinge (AY 3248)]] he broke [[per:29430|Ruwnin]], 120,296 men against 32,686, and killed 10,439 for 1,591 of his own. In the peace Munu gave up seventy-one districts. Its survey at the end of the 3240s shows 89 left of the 160 it had held at the end of the 3230s. It also paid an indemnity that the oration calls "silver to the weight of some eight million mava". On that figure, for once, the record agrees with the king.

Lafmodhalen's stele at Nidhinge says: "He killed 76,500 soldiers" (PAL·IN·02241). The record gives 10,439. His son Tuemagonen raised steles for the general's victories at Shivange (PAL·IN·02247, PAL·IN·02248), and Queen Luravievshevath raised one for his last (PAL·IN·02256). Between them the four stones claim 104,800 enemy dead, about six times the record's 17,290. Each says "He killed" or "She killed". Nambili dug three of them out at Shivange and Yirantudhu in 4953 and 4954. None names the man who stood on the field. That was the custom of the age, not a slight aimed at him, but the custom is why he is unknown.

## A hundred to one

The [[evt:29969|Nodhinga–Lienonoshal War]] (3253–3258) was the only one of his wars in which Lienonoshal was attacked, and the record again counts the usurpation among its causes. [[pol:1266|Nodhinga]] came to seize territory in Yidhe. Its general, [[per:29543|Kulununin]], attacked him three times and was beaten three times: at the [[evt:29984|Battle of Vuvu (AY 3255)]], with 10,252 men against 122,962, then at the [[evt:29988|Battle of Shivange (AY 3256)]] and the [[evt:30004|Battle of Shivange (AY 3258)]]. Nodhinga lost 876, 3,866 and 2,500 men. Lienonoshal lost 450, 574 and 431. The peace made Nodhinga a vassal.

What did such wars cost people who never held a spear? Nuwkennga was one of the towns Munu had ceded in 3248, and a merchant house there kept a day-book for 3255, reckoned in [[cur:625|Mava]] (PAL·LE·01576). It has a single note in the margin: "Caravans are held back on account of the Nodhinga–Lienonoshal War." Winning on the field did not keep the roads open.

In the [[evt:30054|Lienonoshal–Rila War]] he twice beat [[per:29661|Zhiytuvan]]. At the [[evt:30061|Battle of Rile (AY 3265)]] he had 150,402 men against 8,052, and at the [[evt:30067|Battle of Virathkhanu (AY 3266)]] 141,812 against 8,347. Rile was sacked in 3266, and [[pol:1265|Rila]], a city-state of eighteen districts and some two million people, was annexed whole. Nambili's excavation at Rile in 4919 found the burnt layer of 3266. It also recovered a silver coin struck around 3250 (PAL·NU·00195). One face shows the veiled head of Queen Inuvath and the other a bull walking. The coin was called the *tafbaf*, the bull. Her son, Vinidhun the Last, signed the city away at Rile.

His last field was the [[evt:30142|Battle of Yirantudhu (AY 3275)]] in the [[evt:30137|Lienonoshal–Viraduthi War]]. He was sixty-eight, his opponent [[per:29751|Ranüvin]] was twenty-seven, and the armies were 147,977 against 2,181. Other generals finished the war against [[pol:1300|Viraduthi]], and he died three years later.

## What the bull left

In eleven battles 8,180 of his men died and 34,371 of the enemy. He never fought outnumbered, and in six battles his odds were better than ten to one. The record cannot tell us whether he was a great captain or only the man given the bigger army. Two things count in his favour: the hard day at Nidhinge, and how cheaply he won the rest. On average he lost fewer than 750 men a battle.

What his care was worth shows in what came after. Eight years after his death, at Taalinnuy in Tunnge, the region his greatest war had been fought over, a Lienonoshal army of 159,220 men was beaten by 11,981 men of Munu and lost 41,822. That is five times what Tafvavsheyen lost in forty years. The record lists that defeat, and a famine in Tunnge from 3290 that killed 699,119 people, among the causes of the empire's collapse in 3320. In his years the empire grew from 310 districts at the end of the 3220s to 443 at the end of the 3270s. His victories did not make it steadier. Over those same years the record's index of its stability fell from 0.48 to 0.26.

Sovereigns put their names on his work. A different instinct survives from Nunuunge, the city where his career began. In 3322 an old porter who had lived through a later sack there gave testimony to the men rebuilding the city's lost rolls. He asked them to "write my master's name at the head of them, for he died beside the old ones" (PAL·CH·08503). That is the historian's task too. Here, then, is the name at the head of eleven battles: Tafvavsheyen. The record does not say whether he would have wanted it written.
