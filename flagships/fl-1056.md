title: As Badly as That
---
On the inside of the robe of state, under the left arm, where nobody but a king would ever feel it, there was an eagle about the size of a thumb, and it was very badly sewn.

Mo'o had watched it made. It was the second year of [[per:23357|Shim'ayueve]], and the robe lay spread on the long table to be let out across the shoulders, because a king of twenty-two is still filling out, and the queen had come down to watch. She was sixteen. Her name meant Mercy Fire, and she showed none to a needle. She asked for one, and for an arm's length of the gold thread, and before Mo'o had found a polite way to refuse she had turned back the linen lining and was stabbing an eagle into it with the tip of her tongue between her teeth. One wing came out longer than the other. The beak came out like a thumb. When the king put the robe on that evening he said something was scratching him, and the girl laughed at him, and he said, "Then it stays."

She died the next year, of a fever, at seventeen. He did not marry again. The priests asked him to, and the great houses asked, and his mother would have asked if she had lived; he told them that every thing in the world had its place and its due, which was what the priests themselves taught, so they could hardly argue. For thirty-three years after that Mo'o let the robe out and took it in around a dead girl's eagle, the way a river goes round a stone.

* * *

He died in the afternoon, of his chest, as he had been dying since the last rains. The law of [[pol:923|Movosh]] gave the throne to a king's eldest son. He had no son, so the law had nothing to say, and by the time the lamps were lit somebody else had said it: the gates were held by men whose cloaks Mo'o did not know, and the palace guard had stood aside, which in [[set:14|Movosh]] counts as an opinion.

She was sitting up with him in the long hall when they came for her. The priests of [[mov:25|Mie'to'ieshio]] had washed him and dressed him in undyed linen, because the wise go out of the world the way they ought to have lived in it, without ornament, and now they were walking the gods around him one by one, each to the place that was theirs: Atlie to the sky, Uelti to the counsels of men, and so on down the order, which was kept exactly and handed down exactly. About where the king himself was going they had very little to say. It was the thing Mo'o liked best in them.

The young man stood in the doorway with a lamp and would not come in, as if death were catching. He was eighteen or so, and he had shaved for the occasion, not well; there was a nick under his jaw.

"The robe-woman," he said. "They told me to ask for the robe-woman."

"They told you right."

"My father is to be shown at the gate at first light. In the robe. It has to fit him." He swallowed. "I'm Satlietlilie'an."

"Everybody knows. They've been shouting it at the gates since the lamps were lit."

"The son," he said, and went red to the ears, and she was sorry for him, a little.

She got up. Her knees made their two sounds. She looked at the king before she went, and found she had nothing to tell him that the priests were not telling him better, so she straightened the linen at his neck, where someone had pulled it crooked, and followed the lamp.

* * *

She had to lead; he did not know the way. Left at the cistern court. Left again. Down three steps, and mind the third, which had cracked in her mother's time and never been mended.

She had been eight that year, and had held the lamp while her mother let the robe out for a man who had come into the palace much as this one had. The palace annals would put the whole night into seven words — "Shim'ayueve I took the palace by force." Her mother had put it into three, through a mouthful of pins: "Tack it loose."

In the robe-room the new king — she made herself think the words, so as to have them ready — was standing by the shutters in a plain brown tunic with the sweat dried white under its arms. He was a hand shorter than the old king and a good deal broader, a man past forty with a heavy, patient face, and he had the stillness of someone who has been on his feet since yesterday and has decided not to sit down until it is over. His wife sat on the linen chest. She was the older of the two, grey at the parting, and the hem of her skirt had been turned and resewn by somebody careful; Mo'o noticed that before anything else, because she always did. A girl of fourteen or so held the second lamp. The smallest of his daughters was asleep on the bolts of red wool with her mouth open.

"Is that it?" said the girl with the lamp.

Mo'o lifted the robe off its frame. It weighed about what the sleeping child weighed. It was red wool with a border of gold, older than anyone in the room, and when she held the shoulders to the lamp, as she did now, they were a lattice of tiny holes. There were holes from before her mother's time, when it had been taken in for a boy who sat on the throne, the annals said, "being a child." There were her mother's holes, from the night of the first Shim'ayueve. And there were her own, more than anyone's, because the second Shim'ayueve had lived in it thirty-five years and changed the most. She had let it out the year he came home from the [[evt:23749|Battle of Zhaalämiviyi]] and ate like a man who has won. She had taken it in after the [[evt:23766|Battle of Iazhash]], and in again the year after that, when he went back to the same field and lost the other [[evt:23773|Battle of Zhaalämiviyi]], and set his name to the [[evt:23774|Treaty of Zhaalämiviyi]] on the ground where he had won, and sent the silver after it. Since then it had mostly been in. This last year she had taken it in until the side seams nearly touched the eagle.

She reached into her apron for her cord.

"Don't trouble," said the wife, and told her his measure without getting up from the chest: round the chest, round the belly, neck to shoulder-point, nape to heel, the length of the arm, and that he carried his right shoulder lower than his left, so the right hem would want watching. She said it the way other women say a prayer they learned as girls.

Mo'o measured him anyway, because she always did. The wife was right to a finger's width everywhere.

For thirty-three years Mo'o had been the only woman alive who knew the king's measure. She had always thought of it as a skill.

* * *

She worked. She opened the side seams and let them out as far as the old cloth would allow, and shortened the hem by a hand, and set the new lines in long running stitches she could have pulled out with one tug.

"Why so long?" said the wife, who knew what stitches were for.

"Tacking."

"You're not going to sew it properly?"

Mo'o had been awake since the day before yesterday, and she was sixty-four years old, and she found she did not have a lie anywhere in her. "My mother's rule. In Movosh you tack a king loose. The next one comes sooner than the moths."

Nobody said anything. The girl with the lamp looked at her father. In the doorway the son looked at the floor. Then [[per:23794|Satlietlilie'an]] laughed, once, through his nose, the way a man laughs at a joke against himself that he has already made in private.

"Your mother had sense," he said. "Sew me loose. Only loose enough to last the morning."

They put it on him. He said it was heavier than it looked, and she said they all said that. She had him lift his arms, one and then the other, to see how the shoulders took it, and he stopped with the left one half raised.

"There's something in here."

"An eagle," Mo'o said. And then, because they were all looking at her, the rest of it: the second year, the long table, the girl with her tongue between her teeth, *then it stays.*

The girl with the lamp came close and turned back the lining with one finger. "That's a terrible eagle."

"She was sixteen."

"I'm fourteen. I'd have done it better." She put her head on one side. "I'm an eagle too. Shim'ashimen. Eagle Storm."

"Half the girls born that year were eagles," her father said. "It was a compliment to the king. We weren't any of us very original."

"Take it out," said his wife. She did not say it unkindly. "You can't stand at the gate with his queen's eagle under your arm."

"Nobody would see it."

"You'd know it was there."

He stood with his arm still half raised, like a man about to be measured for something else. Then he said to Mo'o, "What would he want done with it?"

She thought of saying the old king's own words back to him. She thought of the robe going on, year after year, round the shoulders of this man who had laughed at her mother's joke, with a dead girl's eagle scratching under his arm; and it came to her, as plainly as a dropped stitch, that the eagle had never been sewn for the robe at all.

"He'd want it where she put it," she said. "She put it on him."

Satlietlilie'an lowered his arm. "Then take it to him."

"And let it be seen that you sent it," said his wife.

"Let it be seen however it's seen."

Mo'o understood then that these two had been married a long time and argued in a shorthand, and that each of them had meant both things.

She unpicked it there, with the robe still on him, standing under his arm while the girl held the lamp. The queen had knotted every stitch twice, the way people do who are afraid of losing what they have made, and each knot had to be teased loose with the needle's point. The gold came out crinkled, keeping the bend of every stitch, so that it lay in Mo'o's palm like something that had been alive. When the last of it was out she turned back the linen and held it to the lamp, and the eagle was still there: a scatter of pinholes with the light coming through, one wing longer than the other, the beak like a thumb.

"It's still there," said Shim'ashimen.

"Thread comes out," said Mo'o. "Holes don't."

* * *

In the long hall the priests had come to the end of the order of the gods and started again from the top, as they would all night. Nobody looked up when she came in. She knelt by the king and turned back the undyed linen on his left side, under the arm, and threaded the crinkled gold through her finest needle.

She knew that eagle better than she knew her own hand; she had been sewing around it for thirty-three years. She put the needle in where the girl had put it in, and brought it out where the girl had brought it out. She made one wing longer than the other. She made the beak like a thumb. It is not easy for a good seamstress to sew like a girl of sixteen with her tongue between her teeth, and it took her most of what was left of the night to do it as badly as that.

When it was done she folded the linen down over it and closed the seam with small stitches set very close together, each one pulled tight, the way you sew for someone who will not be needing the room.

**Author's note.** The record says only that [[per:23357|Shim'ayueve]] II died naturally in AY 2728 and that [[per:23794|Satlietlilie'an]] seized the throne that year ([[evt:23966|Usurpation of Satlietlilie'an I]]); it does not say how, and it gives the new king no parents. The queen, Mercy Fire, died at seventeen; the king left no child. Shim'ashimen, Eagle Storm, was born in AY 2714. The annals (PAL·AN·00430) give the seven words and the child king; the treaty (PAL·TR·01582) the silver.
