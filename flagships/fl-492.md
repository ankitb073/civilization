**OF THE JOINT STOCK; OR, HOW A HUNDRED PURSES MAKE ONE SHIP.**

*A discourse addressed to the merchants and citizens of [[pol:1863|Shivange]], by the deviser of the method, [[per:43939|Likhshinkhen]]. Shivange, 11 Vithni 2347 of the Vishithmikh.*

---

Since the publication last year of the articles of the first company, I have been stopped in the street, written to, preached at and once, in the fish-market, threatened with a gaff, by persons wishing to know what exactly I have done. The editor of this sheet has kindly lent me his columns so that I may answer them all at once, and save my coat.

**I. The difficulty.** Every merchant of [[set:66|Shivange]] knows it. A voyage to the far markets wants a ship, a cargo, a crew and two years' patience. Few houses in the city can furnish all four. Those that can will not risk all four upon one hull, for the sea does not read contracts. Hitherto the remedy has been the partnership: three or four friends sharing a venture, each liable for the whole of its debts down to his last spoon. Such partnerships are few, slow to form, and quick to quarrel; and when one partner dies, the venture dies with him.

**II. The method.** I propose, and with certain friends have now practised, the following. The whole capital of a venture is divided into equal parts, which I call *shares*, each of a moderate sum in [[cur:956|Tuthshi]], such as a master craftsman might put by in a year. Any citizen may buy one share or a hundred. The money is joined in one common stock, which is held and laid out by governors whom the shareholders elect each year, one voice for each share. When the venture returns, the profit is divided by shares, as a loaf is divided by slices.

Three things follow, and these are the whole of the invention.

*First*, the shareholder may lose what he has put in, and no more. His house, his wife's dowry and his last spoon are his own.

*Second*, the share may be sold. If a man needs his money before the ship comes home, he may sell his part to another at whatever price they agree, and the company does not notice the difference.

*Third*, the company does not die. Shareholders die, marry, go mad or emigrate; the stock remains, the governors remain, the ship sails on.

**III. The objections.** I am told by some of the ordained of the [[mov:58|Vishithmikh]] that this is a device for multiplying appetite. I answer with respect that the faith bids us show compassion and relieve suffering, and that it is no compassion to the widow to forbid her to put her savings into honest trade, while permitting the rich to do it alone. A share is the poor man's ship.

I am told by others that strangers will buy shares and carry off the profits of the Republic. Very well; let the articles restrict the purchase to citizens, as ours do.

I am told, finally, that such a company is a state within the state. To this I reply that it has no soldiers, no courts and no coin of its own, and that its governors are answerable to the shareholders as the magistrates are to the citizens. If that makes it a little republic, I do not see that the Republic need fear the comparison.

**IV. The occasion.** It is not by chance that the method was wanted now. Since the [[evt:44453|Conquest of Nilinge]] the trade of that city has come into the hands of our merchants, and it is more trade than any three houses of Shivange can carry. The war made the opportunity; it falls to peace to use it.

**V. A word of caution.** I do not say the joint stock cannot be abused. A share is only as good as the governors who keep the books. Let every company publish its accounts at each return, and let any shareholder who suspects fraud have the right to see the ledgers. With these safeguards, I believe the method will do for the commerce of this city what the arch did for its bridges.

I have the honour to remain the servant of the public, and of His Excellency [[per:43772|Maagunbaaluthin]], under whose government the articles were registered without delay.

LIKHSHINKHEN.
