title: The Larger Half

Every sheep carries two of them, one low in each hind leg, and by the second day of the slaughter the kitchen boys of [[set:121|Runiy]] had a basket of knucklebones and a war over who owned them.

Kuvkulupat let the war run while the fat rendered. Boys fighting over bones were boys not under her feet. But the bones had to be boiled before anyone played with them, or the yard dogs would have them back, so when the shouting came too near the hearth she took the basket down from the beam where Patun had hidden it, tipped it into the small bronze pot, and pushed the pot to the back of the fire where the water would only shiver.

"Those are ours," said Patun. He was nine, and the war had been his idea.

"They're the sheep's," she said. "When they're white, they're yours."

Out in the yard the men were hanging the last carcasses from the beams of the cart shed. The kitchen smelled of mutton fat and oak smoke and of the honey she had set by the fire to loosen. Beyond the wall the town was sweeping its thresholds, the way a town does when it is about to become more than a town and is trying not to say so. Tonight in the hall, before the priests of [[dei:36|Nidziktti]] and the men of the district, the princess would be hailed as queen.

Not queen of [[pol:538|Runiy]]. Everyone in the house had learned to be careful about that, the kitchen included. Queen of [[pol:477|Ttogannga]]: of everything her mother had held. And the man in the palace by the sea would be, from tonight, a king only in his own opinion.

The man was [[per:11527|Pimpansen]], and Kuvkulupat had wiped his nose.

* * *

She had gone down from the hills as a young woman, in the last years of old King Sishettmunin, and she had been scouring pots in the palace kitchen at [[set:215|Ttogannga]] the year he died and his daughter Luldzivialat took the throne. The coast taught her things the hills never had. Salt got into the flour. Honey ran thin as water by midmorning. Every pot had to be turned over at night against the ants. By the time the queen's children were old enough to be a nuisance, Kuvkulupat had charge of the sweet ovens, and the children knew it.

There had been four. The boy came first, then a second boy, who died at ten; then, five years after him, two girls close together. When the court wanted something from the eldest it used his name in full: Pimpansen, Eagle-Wise. The youngest girl was Pimttumkuvtut, Eagle-Sweet. Between the two eagles came [[per:11529|Matungat]], whose name meant a green gift and had no bird in it anywhere.

"Why am I not an eagle?" she asked once, at seven, sitting on the lid of the flour bin and drumming it with her heels.

"Eagles eat what they're brought," Kuvkulupat said. "Green things grow."

It was the kind of answer you give a child to get her off a flour bin, and Kuvkulupat forgot it the moment it was out of her mouth. The child did not.

On the hot afternoons, when the palace slept, the children came down to the kitchen, because the flagstones by the oven were worn smooth and nobody upstairs could hear them. They played knucklebones. You toss one bone, and while it hangs in the air you sweep the others up off the stone and catch the falling one in the same hand; then two at a time, then three, then all. Pimpansen at fourteen had long fingers and no patience. Matungat at seven had short fingers and nothing but patience. They played for honey cakes, and the loser fetched the forfeit from the cooling board with his own hands.

He lost to her far more often than a boy twice her size had any business to. Kuvkulupat never settled in her own mind whether he meant to. But he paid at once, every time. He would carry the cake across the kitchen in both hands, like an envoy bearing gifts, and bow from the waist, and say, "From the king of the floor. In full."

And every time, Matungat broke it and gave him back the larger half.

He carried the reckoning in his head. "You owe me half a cake from the day of the storm," he would say, a month afterwards, and he was always right. Kuvkulupat, who fed the whole palace and could not have told you what she had cooked on the day of any storm, used to marvel at it. He was never wrong about what he was owed, and he never let a debt go because it was small. The youngest lost to both of them and ate the crumbs, and was perfectly happy.

Then the queen died, and the boy was king at nineteen, and kings do not come down to kitchens. Some years after that, Kuvkulupat went home to the hills.

* * *

The hall at Runiy was the district's own oak, black with generations of smoke. Kuvkulupat did not belong in it and did not mean to stay. She came in behind the boards of mutton to see the cakes set down properly, and then she stood by the door post with her arms folded, because nobody tells the cook to go.

The priests of Nidziktti sat in their order, the eldest nearest the fire. It was the eldest who spoke, and he spoke the way priests do, as if he were reading the words off a wall. The gods show their favour in plenty, he said; every child of the [[mov:5|Lelmi]] is taught it before he can walk. And where was the plenty? The king had gone out himself last year with his chariots, and at the [[evt:11865|Battle of Nuwkennga]] he had lost nearly a third of his men. He had sent tribute to a foreign queen's camp and sworn the [[evt:11866|Treaty of Nuwkennga]]: ten years of peace, bought on his knees. And the old men of this district, the priest said, needed no reminding how the host of Ttogannga had given way outside their own town at the [[evt:11197|Battle of Riniy]], or how the very next year it had not held even at the capital, at the [[evt:11204|Battle of Togannge]]. The gods had been telling Ttogannga something for more than fifty years. Now the wheel had turned.

Kuvkulupat had been a small girl the year of the first of those battles. She did not remember the fighting. She remembered the dust over the pastures, which hung there for days, and her mother's hand pressed over her mouth in the dark.

Then a man down by the door, a horse-breeder from the steppe side with a red face and a full cup, got to his feet. In a high, sing-song, foreign voice he gave them the words that had come up the trade roads from the foreign queen's tablet: "Tugannga sent tribute to my camp." He let it sit. Then, rolling it round his mouth: "*Bimbanshen*."

They could not even say his name, over there. The hall roared. Somebody beat the board with the flat of his hand. Somebody else called out that the king of Ttogannga was a famous payer, ask anyone, ask the foreigners, and the laughter came round again, louder, the way laughter does in a room that has been frightened all day.

Kuvkulupat looked to the head of the hall. Matungat was not laughing. She sat with her hands flat on the board, one on either side of a honey cake she had not touched, and she was looking at the cake.

When the noise had worn itself out, she stood. She did not make a speech. Kuvkulupat thought afterwards that she had probably made one ready and thrown it away somewhere between the mutton and the cakes. She said, "My mother held this land, and her father before her. I am my mother's daughter." The priests rose in their order and hailed her queen of Ttogannga, and the hall rose after them; and before the shouting was done, the young men who had been sitting nearest the door were out in the yard at the chariots.

Kuvkulupat heard the wheels go over the packed earth and out of the gate and away down the valley road toward the river country. By morning they would be over the border. Everybody in the hall knew it. Nobody had said it.

* * *

The fires were banked. The kitchen boys slept where they had dropped, on the grain sacks, and Patun slept with his fist shut tight around the knucklebones, white now, smelling faintly of the pot.

Matungat came down past midnight, alone, in her feast clothes, carrying a lamp. She did not say anything. She went to the cooling board and took a cake and ate it standing up, the way she used to, with her other hand cupped under her chin for the crumbs.

"You made them the same," she said.

"There's only one way to make them."

She looked at Patun for a while. Then she knelt, the queen the hall had just made, in her good wool on the kitchen floor, and worked the bones out of his fist one at a time without waking him. She threw. One bone up, the others swept, the falling one caught. Then two at a time. She was still good. She was better than good; she missed nothing.

"Did he lose on purpose?" she said, without looking up.

Kuvkulupat had had twenty years and more to think about it. "Until you were nine or so," she said. "After that, I don't believe he could help it."

Matungat laughed once, softly, so as not to wake the boy. She tossed a bone and caught it.

"They laughed at him tonight."

"I heard them."

"Because he pays." She turned the bone over in her fingers. "He always paid."

"He did."

"He has five girls now, did you know? The eldest is fourteen." She threw again, and swept, and caught. "I hope he teaches them. I hope one of them beats him every single time."

She got up, and her knees cracked, and she made a face about it. The priests said the wheel had turned, she said. Did Kuvkulupat think so?

"It's a wheel," said Kuvkulupat. "It doesn't stop because you like where it is."

Matungat looked at her for a moment as though she might be angry, and then she wasn't. She took a second cake from the board and broke it, and held out the larger half to nobody — to the warm dark beside the oven where a tall boy used to stand — and caught herself, and laughed again, and put it down.

"Keep that back," she said. "He'll say I owe it him."

Then she took up her lamp and went upstairs to be queen.

Kuvkulupat wrapped the half cake in a clean cloth and put it in a lidded jar, and put the jar on the high shelf where no boy could reach it. Honey keeps. That was the first thing anyone had ever taught her in a kitchen.

She sat a while after that with the lamp, not ready to sleep. They had laughed in the hall because he paid: at once, in full, with a bow. That was the half of the boy the hall knew. The other half Kuvkulupat had watched for years across a kitchen floor, and nobody tonight had thought to ask her about it. He had never once forgotten what he was owed. And sooner or later, however small the debt, he had always come to collect.
