title: Who Answers for the Absent
---
Lidhden had counted the host of [[pol:1562|Yedha]] every morning since it left the coast road, and every morning the count had come out the same, which gave him a pleasure he had never mentioned to his confessor.

The captains of hundreds came to the clerks' awning at first light, as they always did. Each one said the number of his hundred and the names of any man missing from it, and the twelve clerks wrote them down, and the twelve sheets came to Lidhden, because Lidhden was the one who did not make mistakes. He added them twice, the second time from the bottom up. Then he wrote the total under the heading he had written every morning that week, *the year 4806 from the founding of Tallin*, blew on the ink until it went dull, and carried the sheet to the general's tent.

Fifty-eight thousand one hundred and thirty.

Raamadhulun was waiting outside the tent with his helmet under his arm. He was a captain of a hundred, a broad grey man who had been at every battle Lidhden had been at and several before, and who liked to ask Lidhden questions he could not answer.

— And them?

Beyond the dry streambed the ground rose gently into the spice gardens, low dark bushes in rows, the pods drying on the stems because nobody had been out to pick them. On the rise, among the bushes, the army of [[pol:1603|Kugannga]] stood in the thin early light. Last night its fires had been fewer than a market town's.

— I can't count what I can't walk among, Lidhden said. Three thousand. Some hundreds over.

There were three thousand three hundred and sixty-seven of them. If anyone on that side of the stream wrote the number down, the sheet has not come down to us.

— Seventeen of us for every one of them, Raamadhulun said. It was seventeen to one last year too, when their king led them himself. They came back anyway. I'd like someone to tell me what they're counting on.

He meant the fight the record calls the [[evt:38004|Battle of Togannge]], where [[per:37266|Duvanaven]] had beaten [[per:37445|Pienen]] the year before. Two years now since the [[evt:37990|Revolt of Kugannga]]; two years that four districts had called themselves a kingdom.

Lidhden did not answer. He was thinking of Nini.

Six years ago, at Nini, he had sat at a table while the levies of Kugannga filed past to be written down, four thousand two hundred and thirty-five of them, men of the city and of the spice country round it, marching for the old great king, Bilin, who was dead now a year. He had learned enough of their speech that season to ask a man his name and his village. It was an old speech, the one his own tongue had come out of long ago, and hearing it had been like seeing his own words at the bottom of a well. At the foot of that roll the scribes had written what they always wrote: "The captains of hundreds answer for the absent." By evening a great many had been absent. Yedha lost at Nini. Lidhden had counted ten thousand two hundred and fifty-six of Yedha's dead there, and he did not expect anyone in Tallin to remember it. Defeats were not the kind of thing that got remembered in Tallin.

Some of the men on the rise, he thought, must have walked past his table that day.

* * *

Clerks do not see battles. They sit in the baggage lines with the cooks and the horse-boys and the priests, and they hear them.

The knights of Tallin went out at mid-morning. Lidhden felt it before he heard it, a trembling in the board on his knees, and then the long shout, and then a sound like surf that did not stop. He cut reed pens. He ground ink and wet it and ground it again. The priest beside him prayed with his eyes shut and his lips moving, and once, when the noise rose, stopped praying and simply listened, as they all did.

It did not end when Raamadhulun had said it would end. Through the heat of the day the sound stayed in one place, on the rise among the bushes, and the wind brought down dust and something else with it, a sharp sweet smell that Lidhden did not know until the cook said, crushed spice, they're fighting in the gardens, and laughed, and then did not laugh.

Once [[per:37266|Duvanaven]] rode past the baggage with four men behind him. He was near fifty and had been a general of Yedha for eighteen years, and there was dust in his beard and on his teeth. He looked at the wagons as though counting them. He spoke to no one.

When the shadows of the wagons reached the cook-fires, the sound moved. It came down off the rise and went away along the road, thinning as it went, and when Lidhden stood on a wagon-tongue to look, the rise was empty, and the banner of [[per:37464|Shievshekhuen]] was going back up the road toward [[set:215|Kugannga]] with what was left of his army behind it.

* * *

There were two counts after a battle, and they were not done the same way.

For their own dead the captains of hundreds came to the awning again, one at a time, and each said a number and then the names, and the clerks wrote the names under the number. Raamadhulun said two names. He said them as if he were reading them off something, and then stood a moment longer than he needed to, and went away. Lidhden added the sheets twice. One hundred and sixty-two. The names would go home to the towns they came from, and somebody in each town would be told.

For the other side there was nobody to answer. So the clerks went out.

The burial parties had laid them in rows between the spice bushes, where the ground was soft enough to dig. Lidhden walked the rows with his board in the crook of his arm, making a stroke for each man and a bar across every ten, and he made himself look at each face, because a man counted without being looked at could be counted twice. Most were young. Some were not. One had a heel of bread inside his shirt, and one had his sandals tied to his belt as if he had meant to save them for the road, and many, most, had the faces of Lidhden's own cousins, the same long upper lip, the same heavy brows, as if the whole field were a family he had not visited in a long time.

By the time the light went red he had walked every row twice. Seven hundred and sixty-one.

Raamadhulun found him at the end of the last row.

— Call it eight hundred, he said. Nobody's going to walk it again. And whoever carves it on a stone will make it four thousand.

— It's seven hundred and sixty-one.

— You'd count the flies.

— Somebody should, Lidhden said.

* * *

She came at dusk. The pickets let her through because she was old and alone and carried nothing but a water jar on her hip, and because by then nobody was much interested in stopping anyone.

She went from soldier to soldier until one of them pointed. Then she came and stood in front of Lidhden and looked at the board in his arm and spoke, and it took him a moment to fall back into the old speech, to find the words at the bottom of the well.

— You are the one who counts.

— Yes.

— Count my son.

He told her, as kindly as the language let him, that the count was done. They were all in the rows. If she wished, they would walk the rows together with a lamp.

— I have walked them. He isn't there.

— Then he went back along the road. Many went back.

— They went back past my door, she said. Before dark. They told me where he fell. Down by the old well, where the ground goes into the gully. They couldn't carry him. They were being chased.

She said it plainly, the way one gives directions. The water in her jar moved a little when she breathed.

— You haven't counted him, she said. So your number is wrong.

Raamadhulun, who had understood perhaps one word in five, looked at Lidhden's face and went to fetch a lamp.

The gully was a dry cut in the red earth below the gardens, its sides grown over with thorn. They went down it slowly, the lamp making a small room of light around them that moved as they moved. Twice the light found something and was wrong. The third time she made a sound and went past them both.

He had crawled. They could see where. He had come down out of the gardens and along the bottom of the gully on his elbows, a long way, and then he had pulled himself into the shade of a thorn tree, as a man does in that country at noon, and lain with his face toward the end of the gully where the path went up to the villages. He was perhaps seventeen. His mother knelt and turned him onto his back and straightened his hands. Then she took the jar from her hip and poured a little water into her palm and washed his face with it, his eyes and his mouth and the dust out of his eyebrows, slowly, without speaking, and Lidhden understood why she had carried water all that way.

He put the board on his knee and made the stroke.

Seven hundred and sixty-two.

She looked up at the sound of the reed.

— What did you write?

He held the board down into the light so she could see. Strokes, and bars through the strokes, row on row.

— Which one is him?

— This one. The last.

She looked at it a long time. Then she said her son's name, and Lidhden wrote it beside the last stroke, small, in the letters of Yedha, which she could not read. She put out her finger and touched it, and the ink was still wet, and a little of it came away on her fingertip, and she looked at that too.

Raamadhulun had taken his helmet off. He was not a praying man; Lidhden had never once seen him at the priests' tent. But he stood at the dead boy's feet now and began, as such prayers begin, "In the name of the One, who was before the beginning and will judge at the end," and stopped, because he did not know what came after it.

The woman finished it for him. She said it in the old speech, without looking up, and it took Lidhden a moment to hear that it was the same prayer, word for word, worn into a different shape by a different mouth. They were all of the [[mov:58|Vieshethmekh]], on both sides of the stream; priests ordained in the same unbroken line had taught both their villages the same things about compassion, and about the end of suffering, and about the Judgement. It had not stopped anyone this morning. It did not seem to Lidhden, kneeling in the gully with the lamp, that it was nothing.

* * *

In the morning the sheets went to the general's tent: of theirs, seven hundred and sixty-two; of ours, one hundred and sixty-two. It had been the last battle of the [[evt:37991|Kugannga War of Independence]]. Before the year was out the four districts were joined to [[pol:1562|Yedha]] again, and the peace that did it, which the record calls the [[evt:38012|Suppression of the Kugannga Revolt]], carried a pardon for those who had taken up arms. The men who had gone back past her door went home to the spice gardens and, in time, picked them. [[per:37464|Shievshekhuen]] lived thirteen more years.

Lidhden's sheet has not survived; nothing written on that field has, and the one name on it went with it. The figure survived. The record gives it still, seven hundred and sixty-two, and every one of them is in it.

Lidhden would have wanted it known that the figure is exact. He might also have wanted it known that at sunset it was wrong by one, and that the person who put it right could not read.
