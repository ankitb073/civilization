title: A Mouthful Before Her
---
For eleven years Khenil had put into her mouth whatever the queen of [[pol:1183|Mono]] was about to put into hers, one spoonful ahead, and so she knew before the physicians did.

It began with the fish. In the spring [[per:27108|Fayatlmamxi]] stopped eating the fish that came up each morning from the weirs of [[dei:141|Nabdin]], still wet in the basket. The cook dressed it as he always had, with wild onion and salt, and Khenil ate her mouthful at the kitchen door and counted to a hundred and nodded, and the dish went in, and came out again whole. After the fish it was the bread. After the bread it was the honey, which the queen had always eaten the way a child does, off her fingers, glancing round afterwards to see who had seen. By the time the barley stood in the ear there was only broth: a mutton bone boiled white, a handful of barley, one green leaf for the look of the thing. No salt. Salt, the queen said, had begun to taste like a slap.

A taster eats what her lady eats, and in that house she ate nothing else; the queen's leavings were her dinner. That was the whole of the work, and in eleven years Khenil had never thought about its far end, which was that when the lady stopped eating, so did she. By midsummer her girdle went round her a hand's breadth further than it had. The kitchen women made a joke of it, the two of them going thin together like a yoke of oxen on bad grass, and in the evenings, when Khenil carried the bowl in herself, the queen made the same joke from the other side.

"Look at you," Fayatlmamxi said. "They'll say I'm starving my taster."

"They'll say you're poisoning me slowly, lady. To make sure of the broth."

"If I wanted you dead I'd give you the fish." She laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and Khenil held the bowl level until it was over.

She had come to the palace in the year [[evt:27520|Turamtu came apart]], when a widow of forty-two with no children found herself in a high seat that had not existed the month before and needed, among a great many other things, somebody to eat first. Khenil had been chosen, as far as she knew, because she had a good nose and no family anyone could buy. She had not expected to love the work. She had not expected to love anything in that house. But a mouth learns another mouth. She knew before the queen did when the cook had changed his salt merchant. She knew that Fayatlmamxi pretended to like mutton fat and did not. She knew it the way a wife knows which side of the bed her husband sleeps on, and the queen, who had buried her own husband at thirty and never taken another, seemed to find it a comfort to be known that well by somebody.

* * *

On the last evening the queen asked for salt.

The cook stared at Khenil when she told him, and then at the jar on its shelf, as if it might have spoken. Salt was dearer than grain that year, and grain stood near an [[cur:647|ili]] and a half of silver the measure, and since spring he had been keeping it out of her broth with a kind of private grief. He took a pinch, thought, took another. Khenil tasted at the door, salt and bone and barley and the leaf, and counted to a hundred against the drip of the stone channel that brought the river water through the kitchen wall, and carried it in.

Fayatlmamxi took four spoonfuls. Then she lay back and moved her tongue about her mouth, considering.

"There," she said. "That's what it tastes like."

Khenil did not ask what she meant. She sat on the floor by the bed as she had sat most nights that summer, and the lamp burned down, and the moon of [[dei:138|Tiwil]] came round from one window to the other. Some time before it set, the queen breathed out and did not breathe in again. It was the quietest thing she had ever done.

Khenil sat with her a while longer. Then she went out to tell the women, and on the way she noticed that her own stomach, which had taken the same broth an hour before the queen, was perfectly well.

* * *

By morning there were men with crossbows in the outer court.

Nobody in the kitchen could say exactly how it had happened, and that was the most frightening part. The steward came down at midday grey in the face, sat on the flour bin and told them what he knew. The seat went to the eldest son; everyone knew the law. There was no son. The great men had gone into the hall of audience to argue about it the way great men do, at length and with a great deal of looking at their own hands, and while they argued, as far as the steward could make out, a man named [[per:27199|Naxmi]] had come in by the side door with his people and sat down in the high seat, and nobody had found the right moment to ask him to stand. The priests of [[mov:21|Küniwmü]] had said nothing yet. Truth, for them, was a thing handed down in order, like a pot passed from hand to hand, and nobody had handed them this one.

"Who is he?" said the cook.

"He's twenty-eight," said the steward, as if that were an answer. Then, because it was all he had: "No one can tell me who his father was."

The other news came up from the river gate an hour later with the women who fetched the water. In the market they were saying the queen had been poisoned. They said it the way people say such things, not as a charge but as a kind of weather: a young man in the seat, an old woman dead in the night, what else would it be? By afternoon they were saying it must have been in her food.

The cook sat down on his stool, slowly, as if someone had cut the backs of his knees. He was a big soft man whose son had come home from [[evt:27592|Tuzhi]] the year before with all his fingers, and he had been thanking the gods for it ever since, loudly, to anyone who would listen. He said nothing now. He did not need to. Everyone in the kitchen knew what happened to the cook of a poisoned queen, and that it happened to him whether there had been poison or not.

* * *

Naxmi sent for her in the evening.

She had expected to be frightened of him and was mostly surprised by how young he looked close to, and by the fact that he was eating. Someone had brought him bread and a dish of curds, and he was going at them with his fingers in the queen's own room, sitting on a chest because he had not yet worked out, she thought, whether he was allowed to sit on the bed. He had a rider's thighs and a rider's way of sitting, and he wiped his fingers on his knee before he spoke.

"You're the taster."

"I was the queen's."

"You ate what she ate. Last night too?"

"Broth. A mutton bone, barley, a leaf." She hesitated. "Salt. She asked for it."

He looked at her for a while, chewing. "And here you are."

"Here I am."

"Half the market is down at the river gate saying I put something in her bowl. Tomorrow they'll say it louder. The day after, somebody will want a man to hang for it, and they can't hang me, so they'll want the next best thing, which is whoever stirred the pot." He tore the bread in two. "I didn't poison her."

"I know."

"You know?"

"I'd be dead."

That pleased him; she saw it. "Then go out and show them. Have your cook make the same broth. The same bone, the same leaf. Carry it out to the gate and eat it where they can watch you."

"So that they'll let you sit there."

He did not pretend she had said anything else. "So that they'll stop saying a thing that isn't true. What they do about me after that is between me and [[dei:136|Diwmno]]." He said the god's name the way the queen used to, as one says *my lord*, and Khenil wondered whether he had learned that from her. "I didn't kill her," he said again, more quietly. "I didn't need to. I only had to be awake."

She stood with her hands folded and thought of the queen saying *that's what it tastes like*, and of the cook on his stool.

"I'll do it for the kitchen," she said.

"Do it for whoever you like," said Naxmi, and went back to his curds.

* * *

The cook made the broth with his own hands and would not let anyone help. The same bone, as near as he could find it. The same barley out of the same bin. A leaf. Two pinches of salt, and then, after a long look at the jar, no more. When Khenil lifted the bowl he laid his palm flat on the rim for a moment, as if he were swearing on it, and took it away.

They had opened the river gate of [[set:265|Mono]], and the crowd had come in under the arch as far as the steps and no further, because the men with crossbows stood on the steps. Khenil saw herdsmen in from the grass in their felt hats, water-women with jars on their hips, a priest of [[dei:137|Xiydadtli]] in his dark cloth, come early about the queen's body and caught in the press. Children had climbed the gateposts. Someone near the front was eating a fried cake, and the smell of it reached her over everything else, hot fat and onion, and her stomach turned over and then, shamefully, growled.

She stood on the lowest step where they could all see her and tipped the first drop off the spoon onto the stone for [[dei:139|Nidkti]], whose barley it was. Then she ate.

It was cold by now, and the fat had set in white flakes on the top, and it tasted exactly as it had the night before: bone, barley, the leaf, the little salt. She swallowed and lowered the spoon and began to count, and because she counted aloud without meaning to, the children on the gateposts took it up. *Four. Five. Six.* By twenty half the crowd was counting with her. She heard the herdsmen counting in their flat hill voices, and the water-women, and somewhere behind her, not loudly, one of the crossbowmen. It was the strangest sound she had ever heard a city make: a crowd counting together toward nothing, very slowly, so as to be fair.

At sixty an old herdsman near the front called out, "Is there more in the pot? I'll test it too. I haven't tasted mutton since Tuzhi," and the laugh that went through the arch was so loud and sudden that the count broke and had to be found again. Somebody shouted sixty-one. Somebody else shouted seventy. They argued about it, good-naturedly, and settled on sixty-four.

At a hundred she was still standing.

Nobody cheered. But the sound of the crowd changed, the way the sound of rain changes when it slackens, and people began to turn and talk to one another about other matters, and the children came down off the gateposts. The priest made a small sign at her that might have been a blessing and went in past the crossbows to see to the queen. Khenil stood on the step with the bowl in her hands. Half of it was left. It seemed wrong, suddenly, to pour away the last thing the queen had asked for, so she stood there with the city going home around her and ate the rest, slowly, every drop, as if someone were waiting on the far side of a hundred to take it in.

Behind her, in the shadow of the arch, Naxmi had been watching. When she turned he was already gone.

* * *

That night the kitchen cooked for a king.

Nobody had told them to. But a young man who has sat down in a high seat must be fed on his first night like someone who means to stay in it, and the cook understood that better than any of the great men in the hall. He had sent out to the market for spices at a price he did not mention. He had a whole kid on the spit, rubbed with salt until it glittered. There were river fish in a crust of barley meal, and honey cakes, and a dish of the white curds the queen had not been able to look at since spring.

Khenil stood at the door with the spoon.

She tasted the kid first, and her mouth flooded so suddenly that she had to stop and press the back of her wrist to her lips. Salt and fat and smoke, and some spice she had no name for, sharp at the back of the tongue. She had forgotten. Since spring her mouth had been following an old woman down a narrowing road, bread and then broth and then four spoonfuls and then nothing, and she had followed willingly, and somewhere along the way she had forgotten that a mouth could want anything. Now it wanted. It wanted so much that she was ashamed, and she stood counting to a hundred with her eyes shut, hating it, while the cook watched her from the fire and said nothing at all.

At a hundred she opened her eyes and tasted the fish, and the cakes, and the curds, and did not die of any of them, and the dishes went in.

She would taste for him four years. At the end of them he would die in his bed at thirty-two, of nothing she had eaten, leaving a daughter too small to sit in the high seat alone, so that her mother had to sit beside her. Khenil knew none of that yet. She knew only that she was hungry.

When the kitchen was empty she took the end of a loaf from the bin and the salt jar from its shelf. She broke the bread and pressed it into the salt and ate it standing up, alone, in the dark: the first mouthful in eleven years that she had eaten for nobody but herself.

That's what it tastes like, she thought.
