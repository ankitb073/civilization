To my son, clerk in the counting-house of the royal granaries at [[set:86|Ndulamlule]], from your father at the summer folds above the dry river, in the year 2149 from the founding of the city: health, and the blessing of [[dei:48|Kudze]] of the sky, and of [[dei:51|Talu]] of the earth, who has been kinder to us of late than she used to be.

I have your letter, which the carter read to me twice, and I am proud of the hand it is written in, though I understand only half the words. You ask, as clerks will, for figures: how many head, how many lambs, how much wool. I will give you figures at the end. But first, since you were born after the worst of it and have only heard the stories at the fire, let me tell you why an old herdsman cannot see a cloud without counting it.

Thirty-nine summers ago the rains failed in Daadüdhih, and did not come back properly for twenty-two years. They call it now, in the city, [[evt:17598|the Great Drought in Daadüdhih]], and I am told the scholars have written it in their books as one thing, with a beginning and an end. To us it was not one thing. It was a thousand small things. It was the spring at the red rocks going to a trickle, and then to a damp stain, and then to a place where the sheep stood and licked the stones. It was my father walking the flock three days to water and two days back, and losing a ewe on every stage. It was your grandmother boiling the leather of the old saddle. It was my brother, your uncle, going down to the city for work and not coming back to the hills.

I learned in those years what the philosophers of the city learn from books, that a man owns nothing; not the flock, which the drought takes, nor the pasture, which the sun takes, nor his sons, whom the city takes. What a man has is the use of things for a while. I do not say this bitterly. I say it as one who has measured it.

Then, as you know, the rains came back. And in the very year they came back, the old king made the [[evt:17816|Treaty of Dambüzhi]], and the silver of the peace was taken from us in taxes, so that we were dry one year and poor the next. So it goes. The gods give with one hand and the treasury takes with the other.

Now, the matter on which I write. Since the [[evt:17948|Conquest of Lamlikulu]], it is said in the hills that their pastures are now the king's, and that the king's stewards will let them out to herdsmen of [[pol:630|Ndulamlule]] for a fee. Men who have driven flocks there say the grass is better than ours. Find out for me, my son, whether this is true, what the fee is, and to whom one applies. Our young lord [[per:17745|Nadamzi]], or those who govern in his name, are said to favour the families who sent men to the levy, and I sent two of your cousins.

Your figures: one hundred and eighty head of sheep, sixty-one lambs this spring, eleven goats, two dogs, one mule who hates me. The wool will fetch what it fetches. Barley at the market is near three [[cur:334|Guvu]] the measure, which is dear for a herdsman, though cheaper than in the dry years, when we did not ask the price because there was nothing to buy.

Your mother sends you a cheese and says you are too thin. I say a clerk is meant to be thin. Write soon. Give the carter a copper for his trouble.

Your father.
