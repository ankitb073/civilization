title: A Song with One Name in It

There were so many women in the inner room that there was no place left among them for a husband, so Navande stood in the cattle yard with his daughter on his hip and listened to his wife through a wall of reed and plaster.

Nagime did not cry out. She made a low, effortful sound, like a woman lifting a full water jar onto her head, and then nothing, and then the sound again; and between the sounds he could hear her mother talking to her in the voice she kept for calves that would not come out right.

Natughu could hear it too. She was two years old, and her mother was behind a wall making the water-jar sound and would not come out, and nobody would say why. She arched backwards in his arms like a fish on a line. She hit him in the eye with the flat of her hand, hard, and when he said her name she screamed his back at him, which she had never done before, and which on any other night he would have been proud of.

The door hide lifted. His wife's mother came out drying her forearms on her skirt and looked at the two of them the way she looked at an ox with its head through a fence.

"Walk her," Nangatsu said.

"Walk her where?"

"Away from this wall. She can hear her mother, and her mother can hear her." She pushed a wet curl off the child's forehead with one knuckle. "Not down to the water. And not up the hill."

He did not ask why not up the hill. All of [[set:41|Tahine]] knew why not. The old chief had been dying in his house at the top of the town since the rains, and at noon, when word came that the priests had gone in to him, Navande had watched his wife's father put on his good cloak and walk out of the yard without a word, with his son at his shoulder and a crowd of his herdsmen behind.

"She'll want the song," Nangatsu said from the doorway. "Sing it to her."

"I can't sing."

"Nobody asked you to sing well."

* * *

So he walked her: out through the cattle gate, past the oxen, who swung their heavy heads to watch them go; along the lane between the dung-plastered walls; and down to the canal path, which he had been told not to take and took anyway, because it was the only flat path in the town. The frogs were very loud. On the far bank the reeds stood higher than a man and moved without any wind. Somewhere past the fish racks the sea turned over in its sleep.

Natughu stopped screaming to listen to the frogs, and then remembered, and began again.

"Song," she said, between breaths. "Song. Song."

There was only one song she would sleep to, and every child in the town knew it before it could walk, because it was short and had a shout in it. The mothers sang the questions and the children shouted the answer.

*Who sat first in the place of reeds?*  
*Nandabe sat first.*  
*Who cut the reed, and the reed grew back?*  
*Ho, Nandabe, ho!*

Navande sang it under his breath, badly. On the last line Natughu flung back her head and shouted her "Ho!" at the frogs, and the frogs stopped, all of them at once, the way frogs do; and in the silence she laughed out loud.

"Again."

He sang it again. It had only one name in it, because there had only ever been one chief. Old men in the cattle market could remember when there had been none at all, when the herds went where the grass was and nobody sat anywhere but on the ground. Then [[per:412|Nandabe]] had sat, and had gone on sitting for thirty-two years; he was sixty-four now, older than anybody's grandfather. He was not, by most accounts, a clever chief. He was a kind one, people said, which was what people said when they could not say clever. His wife had died ten years before, and there had never been a child, and in the order the priests kept, the seat went from a chief to his eldest son. So the order ran up to the old man and stopped there, like a canal at a closed sluice.

Navande did not think about what water does at a closed sluice. He sang.

Natughu pointed straight up with her whole arm.

"Tumbe," she said. "Where Tumbe?"

There was no moon. There had been a thin one three nights before, low over the sea; now there were only stars, and the smoke of the town's fires lying across them.

"Gone," he said. "[[dei:120|Tumbe]] has gone away for a while."

"Come back?"

"He always comes back."

It was the first thing the priests of the [[mov:18|Khututse]] taught, before the names of the gods: that what ends begins again. The moon goes and comes. The reed is cut and grows. Navande had never doubted it and did not doubt it now, with his daughter's hot wet face in his neck; but it struck him, for the first time, as the kind of thing a person says when he wants you not to mind.

* * *

Then, from the top of the town, the women began.

It started at one house and went across the roofs the way fire goes along a thatch: a high, broken keening, then another voice under it, then many. A woman came out of a doorway with her hair already loose. Somebody blew a shell horn, badly, and then again, better.

So, Navande thought. He has gone.

Natughu, delighted by the noise, shouted, "Ho!"

He turned for home. He had reached the top of the canal steps when the torches came round the corner of the lane, and the oxen with them.

They were his wife's father's oxen. He would have known them in the dark by their horns; before he married into the house he had been one of the boys who walked behind them with a stick, and nobody's idea of a husband for Nagime, who was three years older, except Nagime's. Here came the white ones, all of them, rolling up the lane at a slow walk with herdsmen on either side carrying torches and bronze-headed spears; and at their head, swinging his torch like a boy at a feast, was her brother.

"Brother!" Natsinzhi was twenty-two, the same age as his twin, and nothing about him was like her. "Is it come?"

"Not yet."

"She's taking her time, my sister. She took her time being born, too; I came out first and had to wait." He blew in Natughu's face, and she shrieked with pleasure. "You heard?"

"I heard."

"He went just after dark." Natsinzhi lowered his voice, which for him meant only that the herdsmen at the back could not hear. "Father's sitting with him. Somebody has to sit at the door, and he had no son to do it. And the priests will want a proper sacrifice, for a chief." He swept the torch along the white backs. "So."

"All of them?"

"All the white ones. Let the town eat beef tonight and ask itself who the gods favour." He grinned. "There'll be a new song by the rains. You wait."

Natughu drew a long breath and sang at the top of her voice: "Ho, Nandabe, ho!"

The nearest herdsman laughed. The one behind him touched two fingers to his forehead against bad luck. Natsinzhi only laughed louder.

"Teach her a new one, brother. Father first. And after Father, me." He tapped her on the nose. "And after me, who knows? Maybe you."

He went on up the hill with the oxen. Natughu twisted round to watch them go, and screamed when they turned the corner; and because it was the only thing that would stop her, Navande followed them up.

* * *

The old chief's house stood at the very top of the town. They had carried him out before his own door and laid him on a bed of fresh-cut reeds, wrapped to the chin, his feet toward the sea. Women sat round him in the dust with their hair loose. The priests stood apart in a row, eldest first; they would stand like that until morning, and nobody would sit inside the house until they had said who.

His wife's father's herdsmen stood about the hilltop with their spears, not doing anything in particular, in the places where people would have to walk past them.

And at the door, on the ground, with his back to the doorpost and his good cloak spread under him, sat [[per:524|Natsimadhe]].

He was forty-seven years old and heavy in the shoulders, and he sat as if he had been sitting there all his life. He did not look at the dark doorway behind him, or at the priests. He looked at the dead man's face, and now and then he leaned forward and moved a fly off it with two fingers, gently, as you would for a sleeping child.

Down the slope, by the fires, they were killing the white oxen. Navande heard the first one go down, like a wet sack dropped from a roof. The blood went into a pit for [[dei:118|Talu]], who takes back everything that falls; the fat went into the fire for [[dei:116|Tsidhu]], who is lord over the other gods; and the smell of it came up the hill, so that even the mourners turned their heads.

Then someone began the song. Not quick, as the mothers sang it, but slowly, so that each question hung in the smoke a long while before anyone answered it. *Who sat first in the place of reeds?* The whole hilltop answered. The priests answered. And Natsimadhe, at the door, lifted his big head and answered with them, in a voice Navande had never heard him use, and sang the old chief's name over the old chief's body to the end.

Navande had lived three years in his wife's father's house and still could not tell when the man meant a thing and when he was using it. Perhaps in a man like that there was no difference. Perhaps that was what the favour of the gods looked like from close to.

Natughu had gone very still against him. On the last line she found her grandfather in the firelight, and pointed, and shouted her "Ho!" a breath after everyone else, alone, into the quiet.

Heads turned. The eldest of the priests looked at her for a long moment. Then Natsimadhe got up, which he had not done all night, and came to them through the seated women, and put his hand on the child's head.

"She sang for him," he said. "Good." And to Navande, low: "Is it come?"

"Not yet."

"Then what are you doing up here? Go home. A man should be at his own door tonight."

Navande looked past him at the reeds, and the wrapped old man, and the doorway with no one in it. "And you?"

His wife's father followed the look. For a moment he seemed almost surprised to find himself where he was.

"This door has nobody at it," he said, and went back, and sat down.

* * *

The child was born before he reached the yard. He heard it from the lane, a thin, furious, unmistakable sound, and ran the last of the way with Natughu bouncing and crowing on his hip. Nangatsu met him at the door hide with her sleeves dark to the elbow.

"A girl," she said. "Both well. Where have you been?"

"Following oxen."

She looked at his face and did not ask anything else.

Nagime lay on the mats with her hair stuck to her cheeks and the baby on her chest, a creased, indignant, red thing with a great deal of black hair. She looked up at him and her mouth went crooked.

"You were gone a long time."

"She wanted the oxen."

"My father's?"

"All the white ones."

Nagime shut her eyes. She was her father's daughter and needed nothing explained. "Well," she said. "Name her, then, while you're here. Before somebody else does."

He had thought only about boys' names. He looked at the small cross face for a long time, and the name that came to him was not one he had planned; it only seemed to fit the shape of her.

"Natumbidhe," he said.

"Natumbidhe," Nagime said, trying it, and the women round the walls nodded, and someone repeated it into a corner, as if putting it away somewhere safe.

They held Natughu up to see. She looked at her sister with deep suspicion. Then her face cleared, as if something hard had at last been explained to her.

"Tumbe," she said. "Tumbe come back."

The women laughed. Nangatsu laughed until she had to sit down. "Tumbe is a god, little bird, and a man," she said, wiping her eyes, "and your sister is neither, the gods be thanked." But Natughu would not be told.

* * *

Somebody must have run up the hill with the news, because before the night was out Natsimadhe left the dead man's door for as long as it takes to hold a child. He stooped in under the hide, smelling of smoke and fat and blood, and stood among the women like an ox in a reed boat, and took the baby from Nagime in his two great hands and held her under the lamp.

"Natumbidhe," Navande said.

"Natumbidhe." His wife's father said it carefully, as if it were a word in a language he was learning, and nodded once, to Navande, and to no one else.

On the mat by her mother, Natughu was nearly asleep, singing to herself in a slurred small voice that was mostly breath. It was the old chief's tune. It took Navande a moment to hear what she had done to the words.

"That's the old man's song," Natsimadhe said.

"She's put her sister in it."

"So she has." He crouched by the mat with the baby in the crook of his arm, and in the soft, questioning voice the mothers used, he sang the first line to her himself.

"Who sat first in the place of reeds?"

Natughu opened one eye. "Tumbe," she told him, patiently, as though to someone very slow, and went to sleep.

Her grandfather laughed without a sound, so as not to wake anyone, and gave the baby back to her mother, and went out again into the dark and up the hill, to sit at the door until the priests had said who.
