**Chapter the Fourth. The Four Summers**

I do not write as a general writes. The general's histories have been printed by the State Press, with their maps and their tables, and any citizen may buy them for a few agni at the station bookstall. I write as one of the many who went down to [[reg:26|Khanzhi]] in the troop trains, and who came back, which is more than can be said for a great number of my comrades.

It is the fashion now, among certain idle young men of the capital, to say that [[evt:48158|the Fourth Khanji Trade War]] was fought for the merchant houses and the shipping offices. Let them say it. I was there. The markets of Khanzhi had been shut against our goods; our mills at [[set:85|Nambi]] were standing idle and our workers were queuing for bread. [[pol:2010|Shudhunvidhi]] held the door of that country and would not open it except at a ruinous price. [[pol:1638|Khishkhih]] did not choose that war. It was laid at our door.

We went down in the first summer under General [[per:46843|Nazhish]], who commanded in every one of the four great actions and never once left the field to the enemy. I was a signaller, and my post was at the wire-station behind the third line, so that I heard the battle before I saw it: the tapping of the key, the call for more guns, the call for more men. At [[evt:48174|the first battle before Shudhunvidhi]] the enemy under [[per:46997|Naguyima]] held their earthworks with great obstinacy, and our casualty lists filled the columns of the Nambi papers for a fortnight together. But at evening the earthworks were ours.

I will not set down here the numbers of our dead. They are printed in the official returns, and every family in the Republic knows its own share of them. I will only say that they were very many, that they were not wasted, and that on no single day did the enemy hold the ground he had held in the morning.

In the second summer we fought again on the same ground, and it was a near thing; I remember the wire falling silent for a full hour, and the officers walking up and down without a word. But Nazhish held, and in the end it was Naguyima who drew back. In the third summer the enemy changed his general, and [[per:47011|Nagadhash]] came against us with fresh regiments; he fared no better. The enemy's own dead in those years, I am told by men who saw their burial grounds, were beyond counting.

In the fourth summer came [[evt:48211|the last battle before the city]], and then [[evt:48212|the siege]]. The guns spoke day and night. I recall the smoke of the city lying along the river at dawn, and the bells of their temples ringing for help that did not come. When the white flag went up over the gate there was no cheering in my company; we were too tired to cheer. We sat down where we were and slept.

In that same year Chairman [[per:47015|Nandi]], newly called to lead the State, set her name to [[evt:48213|the Treaty of Shudhunvidhi]]. By it the border districts passed to us, the enemy was bound to our State as its dependent, his ports were opened to our trade, and the peace was fixed for twenty years. That is what we bought. Let the young men of the capital weigh it against their bread.

I am a believer in the teaching of [[mov:70|Vimikhulu]], and I know what it says of wealth and appetite. But it also says that the good life is a life of duty, order and right sacrifice, and that what ends will begin again. We did our duty. We made our sacrifice. The wheel has turned once more.

For now it is the young men's turn. Since last year we are at war again, with Yidhe, and [[evt:48261|the victory at Ndulamlol]] has been in all the papers. Bread stands at eighteen agni the measure in Nambi. The troop trains are running south again from the same platforms, and I see the same faces on them that I saw on my own train, and I say nothing, because there is nothing a man of my years can usefully say. I take off my hat as they pass.

*Nambi, 20 Khidha 2513 of the Vimikhulu.*
