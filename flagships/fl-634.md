In AY 2877 a merchant house kept its day-book on rolls of papyrus, now water-stained and partly illegible. Two notes survive in the margin: "Losses written off after the Sack of Diviminnge" and "Losses written off after the Conquest of Dueminnga." To the clerk, the last conquest of [[per:25080|Toshen III]] was a debt that would never be paid. Posterity calls him the Conqueror, and the name is fair if one counts land. His reign added about 150 districts and some seven and a half million people to the [[pol:1004|Yeda]] Empire. This chapter asks who did the conquering and who paid for it.

## A prince of a settled house

He was born in 2808, a man of the Jego, into a dynasty that had held the throne for half a century. His grandfather, also called Toshen, reigned for twenty-two years. His father, [[per:24961|Makotnakhen I]], succeeded in 2833. His mother died when he was twenty. He married young: his wife was seventeen when their first son was born in 2830. A daughter born in 2844 died at eight, in 2852, the same year as her mother, who was thirty-nine. Two more sons survived to manhood. His brother, [[per:25081|Makotnakhen II]], was five years younger, and the reader should remember him.

The [[evt:25395|Accession of Toshen III]] came in 2842, when he was thirty-four. He inherited a large empire with about 301 districts and eighteen million people. Its internal order was strong for its age, and its treasury had grown for forty years. What he did with that inheritance is the substance of the reign.

## The city of Monu

His first war was against [[pol:1097|Monu]], a city-state. The [[evt:25426|Yeda–Monu War]] began in 2845, and at the [[evt:25432|Battle of Munu (AY 2846)]] the king led 57,793 men against 2,510. They were under the command of Monu's queen, [[per:25085|Iduvath]]. He won, as he could not fail to, and lost 106 men doing it. Monu lost 583. The city was besieged. Monu's chronicle, set down twenty-three years later by its palace scribe, claims that "140 of ours were lost, but 187 of the enemy". Its own losses are cut to a quarter of the truth, but the enemy's are less than twice it, and for once a defeated people's chronicler was the more accurate of the two parties.

Iduvath died in 2847, and her son [[per:25102|Vyimenin]], aged twenty-four, succeeded her. The next year one of Toshen's generals took 105,462 men against 2,128 defenders at Anavun. A diorite stele there gives the victory to Toshen and claims "4,400 soldiers" killed; the record counts 953. The [[evt:25444|Conquest of Monu]], sealed at [[set:265|Munu]] in 2848, annexed twenty districts. The record lists the young king as executed in its course. The war had cost Yeda 301 men and Monu 1,536.

## Viradhuthpi

The record traces the next war to Tomözel's own growth in the years after Monu. The [[evt:25518|Yeda–Tomözel War]] (2855–2859) was fought against [[pol:994|Tomözel]] around a single town, [[set:32|Viradhuthpi]]. A general named Manathben commanded three of its four battles. The king commanded one, the [[evt:25548|Battle of Viradhuthpi (AY 2857)]], with 107,387 men against 21,580 under [[per:25192|Milasimen]]. He won, and lost 2,475 men to the enemy's 4,133.

Yeda's own chronicle of the war, written in 2887, opens by blaming Tomözel's king, [[per:25046|Bozinfoyatl]], who, "swollen with pride, coveted the lands of Yeda". For the king's battle it doubles the enemy army, triples the enemy dead and reports only 961 Yeda dead. The [[evt:25575|Treaty of Viradhuthpi (AY 2859)]] took thirty-four districts and two towns and imposed tribute for thirty-five years. The chronicle says that "the land which they had stolen was returned to Yeda". The record names Yeda as the attacker. The war cost Yeda 4,786 dead against 11,477 for Tomözel. It was the last battle Toshen commanded; he was forty-nine.

## Conquest by other hands

The rest of the reign belonged to his generals. The [[evt:25623|Yeda–Veni War]] (2865–2867) followed a defeat [[pol:1053|Veni]] had suffered at another neighbour's hands in 2863. Yeda struck a kingdom already wounded. A Yeda historian writing sixty years later says that Veni's king, [[per:25062|Vunevyidun]], "had gathered an army against Yeda". A general named Evonen won both battles. The [[evt:25648|Treaty of Tungaruikhrimu (AY 2867)]], sworn at [[set:172|Tungaruikhrimu]], took twenty-five districts and three towns. Veni paid an indemnity and became a vassal. One of Toshen's sons, then twenty-two, married Vunevyidun's daughter. "The land rested," the historian writes of the peace.

In 2872 the king's eldest son died at forty-two. The next year came the [[evt:25696|Yeda–Dueminnga War]] (2873–2876), Evonen's again, against [[pol:1107|Dueminnga]], another city-state. Armies of some 120,000 men twice faced defenders numbering 211 and 138. Yeda lost 25 men to win one district; Dueminnga lost 114. The [[evt:25714|Conquest of Dueminnga]], sworn at [[set:168|Diviminnge]], ended with its king, [[per:25239|Imtubunen]], listed as executed, the second captive king of the reign to die so. The city had been sacked first. That sack and that conquest are what the merchant house wrote off in its margins.

## The brother's turn

Toshen III died in 2878, aged seventy, of natural causes. Yeda's law gave the crown to the eldest of the royal kin, not to a son. The [[evt:25735|Accession of Makotnakhen II]] passed it to his brother, then sixty-five, who reigned for twelve years. Two more rulers followed before the end. The empire's internal order, already weakening under Toshen, kept falling, and by the end it was gone. In 2911 Yeda broke into six successor states, thirty-three years after his death. Of his conquered neighbours, Veni was gone by 2886. Tomözel collapsed in 2894, and the record counts the Treaty of Viradhuthpi among the causes.

The archaeology of his reign is mostly the archaeology of other men's work. He commanded in two battles and won both. In the first he brought twenty-three men for every defender in the field; in the second, he lost three men for every five he killed. The conquests that made his name were won by Manathben and Evonen. They were sealed by treaties later Yeda historians rewrote as restitution and enforced by the execution of two kings. He was the Conqueror in the way that a house is the builder of its walls. The work was done by others. Their names appear in the record and the chronicles, not on his stele, and the losses in the ledgers of men like the clerk of 2877.
