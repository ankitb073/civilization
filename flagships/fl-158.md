*Delivered before the Academy of [[set:26|Vami]] in the year 43 of Vami, by [[per:22025|Fayatlximam]], maker of the heavy plough.*

Members of the Academy, citizens, and you young men at the back who have come, I suspect, less to hear me than to see whether a woman can stand at this lectern without fainting: I thank you all for your attention, and I shall try to deserve it.

You have asked me to speak of the plough which bears, in the mouths of the farmers, my name, and which they have been dragging across the fields of [[pol:953|Vami]] for two summers now. I shall do so. But I wish first to make a remark of a more general kind, since this is an Academy and not a cattle market, and you will expect a philosophical aside before you get anything useful.

It is commonly said that an invention is a gift of the gods, dropped into the mind of one fortunate person like a coin into a beggar's bowl. I do not deny that the gods give. I sacrifice to [[dei:7|Yüp]] of the fire, who taught the smith his trade, and to [[dei:6|Nili]] of the river, who waters every furrow I have cut. But I observe that the gods seem to give most generously to those who have spent twenty years staring at mud. Let us call it a partnership, in which the gods supply the spark and the citizen supplies the tedium.

Now to the mud. Our fathers ploughed with the scratch-plough: a pointed stick, shod with iron if one was fortunate, which a pair of oxen dragged through the soil. It serves well enough on the light uplands. It does not serve on the heavy bottomlands, where the clay clings like a creditor. And it is precisely such land, wet and black and stubborn, that the city gained in Iith by [[evt:22115|the treaty sworn at Imshi]] eighteen years ago. We won those fields with spears; we could not win them with ploughs. They lay half-sown, a reproach to every citizen who walked past them.

My plough does three things the old one did not. First, a knife of iron, set before the share, cuts the sod upright, as a scribe cuts parchment. Second, the share itself is broad and iron, and cuts the slice beneath. Third, and this is the part of which I am proudest, a curved board behind the share lifts the slice and turns it over, so that the weeds are buried and the clay is opened to the air of [[dei:8|Nixil]] and the sun of [[dei:10|Masto]]. The whole rides upon two small wheels, so that the ploughman need not hold its weight. It wants eight oxen, not two; but eight oxen on good land yield more than two oxen on land that will not yield at all.

What follows from this is not my concern as a maker, but it is my concern as a citizen. The fields of Iith are being sown. Grain is coming to the market at a fair price, under a [[cur:511|khina]] and a half the measure, and the poor do not stand at the doors of the granaries. A city that can feed itself from its own furrows need not go to war for its bread. I mention, with all respect, that [[evt:22206|the trade war over Yimla]] came to nothing; whereas the plough cost a few talents of iron and has paid them back already.

Our faith of [[mov:1|Yacuam]] teaches that prosperity is a sign of the gods' favour. I accept it. But I would add, as a philosopher may, that the gods appear to favour those who make good tools and use them well, and that the city which wishes to remain in favour should honour its smiths and carpenters at least as warmly as its generals.

To our lord [[per:21624|Maxi Tli]], who has looked kindly upon this work from its beginning, I give public thanks. To the Academy, I offer the drawings, which I have deposited with the librarian, so that any carpenter may copy them. A good thing kept secret is only half good.

So was [[evt:22258|the heavy plough]] brought into the world in Vami. I have done talking; the young men at the back may now go and see whether I faint.
