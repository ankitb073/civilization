To my cousin, in whose house I hope to find a roof, greeting from your kinsman the lamp-maker, lately of [[set:227|Nzhinduvi]] and now of nowhere in particular.

You will forgive the hand, which shakes, and the parchment, which I begged from a priest at the shrine where we sheltered the first night. I write in the third year of [[per:26698|Navavun]], our king, if he is still our king; I do not know where he is, and I think neither does he.

You asked me once, when we were boys and quarrelling over the last fig, whether a city is its walls or its people. I said walls, because I was younger and liked things I could touch. I have since had reason to change my opinion. Our walls are still standing. It is the city that is gone.

You will have heard that we were drawn into the [[evt:27084|17th Daadüdhih Trade War]], though I could not tell you what the quarrel was, except that it concerned tolls on the road. Such wars are made by men who own caravans and fought by men who own lamps. Our city was only four summers old; since the [[evt:27058|Rise of Nzhinduvi]] we had scarcely learned to say its name with pride before we were asked to die for it. And in the same season, another enemy declared the [[evt:27096|18th Daadüdhih Trade War]] upon us, as if one were not enough. A young city, a boy king, two wars. The philosophers say that prosperity is a sign of divine favour. I leave you to draw the conclusion.

The men of [[pol:1125|Gishugizi]] came in the month of the harvest. I saw them from the roof of the lamp-market: the whole plain moving, like a field of barley in the wind, but the barley was spears. On our wall there were fewer men than come to a respectable wedding. I knew some of them. One was the cooper's son, who owed me for a lamp. At the [[evt:27103|Battle of Nzhinduh]] they held the gate for longer than anyone believed possible, and the enemy left more dead before our wall than we had citizens upon it. I say that not as a boast, cousin, but as a wonder, and also as an accusation, though I do not know against whom.

Then they came in. Of the [[evt:27104|Sack of Nzhinduh]] I will tell you only what I saw: the lamp-market burning, oil running in the gutters, and the fire riding on the oil down the street toward the lower town. My shop went first. Forty years of my father's moulds, gone in the time it takes to say a prayer. We left by the fullers' gate with what my wife could carry and what I could not bear to leave, which proved to be a single bronze lamp, the first my father made. It is absurd. I have no oil.

The teachers of [[mov:38|Zhivintsizu]] tell us that all things turn on a great wheel, and that what ends will begin again. But a wheel does not ask what it crushes.

Now to business, since justice in dealings is also a duty. Bread here, in the town where we wait, is dear but not impossible: a measure of grain fetches a little over one [[cur:532|Dudvu]], and I have six Dudvu sewn into my wife's hem, which will feed four of us for a month if we are moderate, and we have become very moderate. I can make lamps; I can mend them; I can keep accounts. I ask for a corner of your workshop until the roads are safe, and I will pay you in labour, honestly measured.

Greet your wife. Tell your sons that their uncle is alive and has become a philosopher, which is what happens to men who lose everything.

Farewell.
