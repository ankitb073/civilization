title: One Battle Larger Than a War: The Fall of Tonnga in the Egu and Tonnga Chronicles

The [[evt:2307|Second Egu–Tonnga War]] (AY 582–585) was the end of a small state. The record has a royal army of about seven thousand marching three times on Virnadhu against a city-state that never fielded more than about 240 men. The surviving chronicles approach that disproportion from opposite ends. My argument is that the Tonnga tablet, though it is the worse witness on every number, is the better witness to what the war meant.

## The tablets

The Tonnga account (PAL·CH·00316, Radape) came out of Nambili's AY 4931 season at Virnadhu, the city whose fall it records. Its edges are fire-damaged, though the damage cannot be dated. Lukmagonin set it down "in the 70th year of Tonnga", AY 589, four years after the conquest. He was in his thirties during the war and a scribe of the palace that lost it. The Egu account (PAL·CH·00315, Temu, excavated at Igu in AY 4926) is Dunupmalin's, dated "in the 11th year of Tsibutalen", AY 631. That is forty-six years after the war, and Dunupmalin was born nineteen years after it ended. One tablet is close to the war and partisan. The other is distant and partisan.

## Field by field

*Who began it.* In the record, [[pol:58|Egu]] attacked, to take territory. Tonnga had already been paying it tribute since the [[evt:2103|First Egu–Tonnga War]] of AY 550–552. Lukmagonin agrees: "there came word that Pothudin of Egu had gathered an army against Tonnga." Dunupmalin reverses it: "Ramabidinin of Tunnga, forgetting the oaths his fathers swore, set his hand against Egu. Our king could not suffer the insult." For once the stock formula has a plausible referent, since Tonnga's fathers had indeed sworn oaths to Egu in AY 552. A real grievance has been borrowed to cover an unprovoked war.

*The battles.* The record has three engagements near Virnadhu in [[reg:7|Tunnge]]: the [[evt:2328|Battle of Virnadhu (AY 583)]], the [[evt:2333|Battle of Virnadhu (AY 584)]] and the [[evt:2345|Battle of Virnadhu (AY 585)]], all won by Egu under Minnun. It also has two sieges, the [[evt:2334|Siege of Virnadhu (AY 584)]] and the [[evt:2346|Siege of Virnadhu (AY 585)]]. Dunupmalin has all three battles and neither siege. Lukmagonin has one battle, the first, with the small state's natural excuse: "when the allies failed to come, our men could not hold the field."

*The dead.* The record gives Egu 2,565 dead (993, 427 and 1,145) and [[pol:72|Tonnga]] 147 (37, 68 and 42). Dunupmalin's enemy dead (97, 178 and 110, a total of 385) are each about 2.6 times the record's. His enemy musters (408, 652 and 345, against 149, 238 and 126) are inflated by a similar factor. For his own side he admits 8, 15 and 26: forty-nine dead, where the record has 2,565. Lukmagonin's single entry is the striking one: "It cost us 15 men, and them 3,950." The record's figures for that battle are 37 and 993. Lukmagonin's figure for Egu's dead in one battle is half as large again as the record's total for the entire war. A small state that could not win the field won the arithmetic instead.

*Commanders.* None was killed, and neither tablet says otherwise.

*Outcome and land.* The [[evt:2347|Conquest of Tonnga]] in AY 585 annexed all four of the city-state's districts. Dunupmalin is plain about the end: "Tunnga is no more, and its people serve Egu." But he counts eight districts won, twice as many as Tonnga possessed. Lukmagonin gives one line: "the royal city was taken. The gods had turned their faces from us." He records no surrender, no terms and no lost district.

## What the silences say

Dunupmalin's distortions are the routine work of a victor's archive two generations on: the cause reversed, the cost cut to a fraction, the prize enlarged. Lukmagonin's are of another kind. He wrote four years after the fall, perhaps in sight of the walls. He drops two of the three battles, and he gives Egu losses that no one at Virnadhu could have counted. But he does not pretend the city stood. The record lets us correct every figure he gives. It cannot correct his final sentence, which is not a claim about numbers.
