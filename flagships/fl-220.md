title: The Holy Part Omitted: Two Chronicles of the Holy War of Navnga against Bäni
---
Of the two chronicles of the [[evt:3553|Holy War of Navnga against Bäni]] (AY 744–747), one was written two years after the peace and the other thirty-eight. Livunlon, palace scribe of [[pol:105|Bäni]], finished his annal in AY 749 (PAL·CH·00560); a clay copy was excavated at Viradhuthpi in AY 4938, in the very town where the peace was sworn. Kubakibun, palace scribe of [[pol:40|Navnga]], wrote in AY 785 (PAL·CH·00559), in the forty-third year of the king who had waged the war. I shall argue that the gap in time matters less than it seems, and that both texts answer the same embarrassment: a holy war whose religious outcome neither court wished to describe.

## The record

The documentary consensus is clear. Navnga began the war, in the name of religion, against a kingdom it had already beaten once, in the [[evt:3430|Navnga–Bäni War]] of AY 724–727, and which had lost to Tonnga at the [[evt:3551|Battle of Tungaruikhrimu (AY 743)]] only the year before. Three battles followed in [[reg:15|Baan]]: the [[evt:3566|Battle of Zhiyiyzhikhti (AY 745)]], the [[evt:3572|Battle of Khuele (AY 746)]] and the [[evt:3580|Battle of Khuele (AY 747)]]. Navnga won all three, and no commander fell. Navnga lost 5,413 men, Bäni 346. After the [[evt:3581|Siege of Viradhuthpi (AY 747)]], the [[evt:3582|Treaty of Viradhuthpi (AY 747)]] gave Navnga ten districts and, on the record's reading of its terms, made Bäni a vassal and bound it to the victor's faith.

## Kubakibun

Kubakibun gets the battles right: three, all won. He departs from the record on cause, cost and gain. Bäni, he says, "had gathered an army against Navnga", which is hard to credit of a kingdom freshly beaten by Tonnga. He gives Navnga 2,389 dead and Bäni 1,255: the first is 44 per cent of the record's figure, the second more than three and a half times it. And he claims eighteen districts, nearly twice the ten the treaty gave.

Distance explains some of this. By AY 785 few men who had fought at Khuele were left to contradict him, and the claims of a long reign had had decades to harden into court memory. The court explains more. Kubakibun wrote for Dulomin, who had commanded at the second battle of Khuele himself; a chronicle composed in the king's old age was a memorial as much as a history. He turns the war into one of restitution ("the land which they had stolen was returned") and says nothing at all of the conversion. In a holy war, the victor's chronicler has omitted the holy part.

## Livunlon

Livunlon wrote with the defeat two years old and the king who had made the peace still on the throne. He too concedes three battles and three defeats, excusing each in turn ("through the cowardice of hired men", "in a great storm", "because the omens had been neglected"). His figures run the other way: 12,995 Navnga dead, nearly two and a half times the record's, against 153 of his own. By an odd symmetry, that is again 44 per cent of the record's figure for his own side. His outcome is a stalemate and his peace a technicality: "The border was settled." He mentions no cession, no vassalage and no change of gods.

## Silences in common

The two texts disagree on blame, on numbers and on the outcome, as rival chronicles do. What they share is more instructive. Neither mentions the siege of Viradhuthpi, though the record places it in the last year of the war and the treaty was sworn there. For Kubakibun a siege implied resistance; for Livunlon it meant the enemy at the gates. Nor does either mention the religious terms. That shared silence, in a war fought for religion, is the best evidence I know that the conversion was resented in [[pol:105|Bäni]] and was not felt in Navnga as a triumph worth recording. It is also the point at which the record fails us: it tells us what the treaty required, not how, or whether, the requirement was kept.

An archaeologist may add one note. Livunlon's tablet came out of the ground at Viradhuthpi, the town that was besieged and that saw the treaty sworn, and it says nothing of either. The silence of a source is sometimes loudest where it was found.
