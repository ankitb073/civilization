title: One Reed Each
---
At the fish landing of [[set:60|Birnadu]] they trusted Yibetnit because she counted every basket as one. The boat-owners wanted their baskets counted large and the buyers wanted them counted small, and she stood between them on the wet stones with a jar at her feet and a bundle of cut reeds under her arm, and for every basket that came over the side she dropped one reed into the jar. When the boat was empty she tipped the jar out on the stones and counted the reeds in tens, and that was the number. People argued with each other all day long at the landing. Nobody argued with the jar.

She had kept the jar thirty years, since the year after the second sack, when her father gave her to a spearman of Talenonoy who had been granted a strip of land by the harbour street for his spear. The spearman had been gone seven years now. In the fourteenth year of the queen the captains had called up every man who had ever held a spear for Talenonoy, and he had gone up the river with his bad knee and his grey beard, and no word had come back, not one tablet. Then Talenonoy came apart like a wet basket, and the city had a king of its own, and the king died, and his son was king, and the son was six.

It was the second year of the boy king, [[per:6857|Buneluknen]], and it had not rained properly for so long that the children of the harbour street did not believe in mud.

* * *

Her brother Pengaranin brought the spear before it was light. He had carried it on the wall against her husband's people, thirty years ago, and had kept it greased in his rafters ever since, as if he expected to need it again and was ashamed of expecting it.

Belun took it from him in the doorway. He was the younger son, the one with his father's long jaw and the harbour street in his mouth. The elder had gone up the coast two nights before in her cousin's boat, with his wife and his children and as many neighbours as would fit, and Yibetnit had stood on the landing and not counted them, for once, because she did not want to know the number.

'It held the wall,' Pengaranin said.

'The wall fell, Uncle.'

'The wall fell. The spear held.'

Belun laughed, which was what his uncle had wanted. He weighed the spear in his hand and looked past them both at the shelf by the hearth, where his father's gods stood in the lamp-smoke: [[dei:140|Binba]] of the earth with her hands on her knees, and [[dei:138|Tubel]] of the moon with the thin horns on her head. The men of [[pol:184|Egu]] said those were foreigners' gods. They said a city that spoke as they spoke and prayed to the [[mov:21|Kunelmu]] gods of Talenonoy was a city with its face on backwards, and that it was holy work to turn it round.

'They say we've no right to them,' Belun said.

'Your father prayed to them.'

'Father wasn't a foreigner.'

'Your father was from Talenonoy.'

'He was from the harbour street,' Belun said, and that was the end of it, because it was what his father used to say, and nobody in the house had ever found an answer to it.

Yibetnit wanted to say what his father would have said, this morning, in this doorway. She found she did not know. Seven years. She knew what he would have eaten and how he would have complained about his knee. She did not know what he would have said about his son and a spear and the men of Egu, and she was angry with him for that, for having gone where he could not tell her.

Belun touched the two figures on the shelf, quickly, the way his father had done going out of the door. Then he went.

* * *

So she counted at the gate, because there were no boats.

She had nothing to drop into a jar. She bent a finger for every man who went out through the gateway into the white morning, and at ten she let the fingers go and moved a pebble from her left hand to her right. The general Luknelomin went first, a broad man with a tired face, and she counted him: a general was one. Old men went who had stood on the wall with her brother. Boys went who had never stood on anything. Fishermen went with their gutting knives tied to poles. Belun went, somewhere in the middle, and did not look round, and she counted him, and went on.

Three hundred and fifty-five.

'It isn't enough,' Pengaranin said.

'It's the number.'

From the roof of the gatehouse you could see the Egu along the coast road, under their own dust. They came on in a long dun mass that did not seem to end. There were more of them than she had ever counted of anything, more than all the baskets of a good year. She did not try. The king of Egu had come with them himself, the people on the roof said, and in the hard light it was possible to believe that a whole country had got up and walked.

It did not take long. The city's men went down into the field below the old canal, where the reeds stood dry along the cut, and the Egu line came over them the way the sea comes over a child's wall of sand. For a while there was noise. Then there were men running back towards the gate, and Egu behind them, and nobody could shut a gate on their own sons. By the middle of the day the Egu were in the harbour street.

* * *

The one who came into her house was young, younger than Belun, with a hide shield on his back and the flat vowels of Egu. He did not hurt anyone. He stepped round Pengaranin, who sat on the bench inside the door with his hands on his knees and looked at nothing, and took the bronze bowl, and the lamp, and the oil, and the good knife. He lifted the lid of the grain jar and looked into the dark of it and put the lid back.

'Nobody has any,' he said. 'In Egu either. In Egu the grain's a woman too, and she isn't listening there either.' Here the grain was [[dei:139|Nidiktti]], and she had not listened for years. He seemed to want Yibetnit to know that much: that they had that in common, the silence.

Then he came to the shelf. He had a way of doing it that told her he had done it in every house since the gate: the butt of the spear, once, not angry, the way you knock a wasps' nest off a beam. Binba of the earth went to pieces on the floor among the crumbs of the dry earth she stood for. He picked up Tubel.

'Your moon's a woman,' he said, as if she had told him a lie.

'Here she is.'

'The moon is a lord. Everybody knows that.'

'Not here.'

He turned the little clay figure over in his fingers. 'You talk like us,' he said. 'You talk just like my aunt. Why do you pray like them?'

'I married them,' Yibetnit said.

He thought about that longer than she expected. Then he did not break the moon. He put her in the bag with the bowl and the lamp. 'My mother won't believe it,' he said. On his way out he tipped up her tally jar to see whether there was silver under the reeds, the way people hid silver, and the reeds went across the floor like spilt straw, and there was nothing under them, and he went.

Pengaranin spoke for the first time since the morning.

'Your husband burned the houses by the harbour gate once.'

'I know.'

'He was quicker about it.'

She got down on the floor and began to gather the reeds.

* * *

That night the Egu camped on the field below the canal, and from the roof she could hear them singing to the moon as it came up, singing to it as a lord, in words she understood. It came up over their fires all the same, the same size it had always been, and for a moment she could not tell whether it belonged to anyone. 'Tubel,' she said, 'light the door,' which was what her husband had said whenever he went out of it.

Some of the men who had gone out came back through the dark in ones and twos. Belun did not. People said in the street that the boy king was alive. People said he had been hidden in his father's tomb among the old king's things, and had been good, and had not cried. People said the Egu had broken every god in the temple quarter. Nobody knew anything.

On the third morning the Egu went home with their carts loaded and their own dead laid on top of the loads. A carter stopped at the well and she drew water for him, because in a dry year you pay a man with water to tell you things, and he told her: thirty. Thirty of ours, he said, wiping his mouth. She did not believe him. Then she stood at the gate and counted what lay on top of the loads as the carts went by, and did.

* * *

The women went out to the field when the dust of the carts had settled. The Egu had stripped the dead of anything bronze, and the heat had already been at them, and the flies. Yibetnit carried her reeds.

She did it the way she did the landing. One reed on each man where he lay, so that nobody was counted twice; then, at the end, back over the whole field, picking the reeds up again into her hand, ten and ten and ten. Other women saw what she was doing and left her room to do it, as people at the landing always had.

She found Belun by the dry canal, among the reeds, with his uncle's spear gone. She knelt by him for a while. Then she laid one reed on him. Only one. It would have been the easiest thing in the world to lay down two, or a whole fist of them, and she understood, kneeling in the stink and the noise of the flies, that if she did that the count would be a lie, and that every other woman on the field would have the same right to the same lie, and by evening the field would hold more dead than had ever gone out of the gate. She laid one reed on him and got up and went on.

When she had walked the field and come back she sat down on the canal bank and counted the reeds out on her knees in tens, and tied each ten with a twist of fishing line, as she did at the landing. Fifteen bundles. One hundred and fifty. Three hundred and fifty-five had gone out through the gateway. She took the one number from the other, as she did at the landing with spoiled baskets, and that was how many had come home.

The dead went down to [[dei:137|Shuyedarsi]] that day and the next, under the dry earth, by the canal where most of them had fallen.

* * *

The record has names for all of it now: the war is the [[evt:6527|Holy War of Egu against Birnadu]], and those three days are the [[evt:6541|Third Sack of Virnadhu]]. Two years later Tubatattpen, the king of Egu, set up a basalt stone near the city, and it says that he "killed 700 soldiers." In the house of tablets at Egu a scribe wrote that [[set:60|Birnadu]] "was taken and plundered by the army," which is true, and one line. Forty-three years later the palace scribe of [[pol:280|Birnadu]], who had been three years old that summer, set down the history of the war; on the tablet that survives, his account goes from the boy king's first year to his third, and this year is not in it. At the end of his account he asked, "Who will now remember the kings of old?"

Nobody asked Yibetnit for her number. She put the fifteen bundles on the shelf by the hearth, where Binba had stood and Tubel had stood, and they dried there in the dry air, and kept. One of the reeds was Belun's. She did not know which. On the canal bank, before she tied the first bundle, she had shaken all the loose reeds together in her two hands, the way she shook the jar at the landing before she tipped it out, so that no one of them could ever weigh more than the others.

She had made sure of that.

**Author's note.** The figures are the record's: three hundred and fifty-five went out against [[pol:184|Egu]], one hundred and fifty died, and Egu lost thirty. Tubatattpen's stele (PAL·IN·00449) claims seven hundred; the Egu annals (PAL·AN·00110) give the sack one line; the Birnadu chronicle (PAL·CH·01128) skips the year.
