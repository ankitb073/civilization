*A true and sober account of the strangers who have come to [[set:41|Dyigini]] from beyond the sea, set down by a citizen of that port, that what was seen may be distinguished from what is rumoured. Dyigini, 19 Vaava 2168 of the Vimikhulu.*

I begin with a caution. Since the strangers first came among us, there has been printed in [[set:85|Nambi]] and elsewhere a flood of sheets describing them: that they have the heads of dogs, that they eat no salt, that their ships are drawn by fish. I have seen them with my own eyes on more than a hundred occasions, and I have drunk with several of them at the harbour taverns, and I can testify that their heads are ordinary and that they salt their food more than we do. What follows is confined to what I have seen, or heard from men whose word I trust; where I report rumour I shall say so.

**Of their coming.** In the summer of the year 2165 of the Vimikhulu, our merchant captains, who had long been pushing further across the western water, reached a harbour on a coast that no chart of ours had shown. Its people call it [[set:63|Igu]]. There they found not savages, as some had predicted, but a town with quays and warehouses, belonging to a great state which its people call [[pol:1785|Gawli]]. Such was [[evt:44706|the meeting at Igu]], the first time, so far as any record shows, that ships of our continent and of theirs had lain in the same harbour. Within the year the ships of Gawli had made the voyage in the other direction, and since then hardly a month has passed without a sail of theirs in our roads.

**Of their persons.** They are of middle height, somewhat broader in the face than we are, and they wear their hair long and tied. Their speech has nothing in common with ours; but the harbour children learned enough of it in a season to cheat them at dice, which suggests it is not so difficult. Their merchants are shrewd and sober. Their sailors are like sailors everywhere.

**Of their goods.** They bring dyed cloth of a quality we cannot match, worked metal, and a bitter dried leaf which they steep in water and drink hot. They take from us grain, paper, and above all silver. I have watched the shikh flow out across the quay in a way that troubles me. A sack of good [[cur:901|shikh]] goes onto their ships, and a bale of dyed cloth comes off. The cloth wears out; the silver does not come back.

**Of their religion.** On this I can say little with confidence. They pray at sunrise facing the water, and they would not tell me to whom. The teachers of [[mov:70|Vimikhulu]] in the port have begun to visit their ships and to speak with them through interpreters, for our faith is for all peoples and its teachers do not wait to be invited. I am told the strangers listen politely and change nothing. Teachers are honoured; but, as our own teaching says, no one owns the truth.

**Of what it means.** Here I must argue, since my neighbours disagree. Some say the strangers are a blessing, for trade at Dyigini has grown beyond all expectation and the warehouses cannot be built fast enough. Others say they are a danger, for a state that can send ships across the western water can send soldiers by the same road. I hold a middle opinion. [[pol:1638|Khishkhis]] is strong; under our lord [[per:43814|Namivambi]] it won [[evt:44592|the peace of Jyijinjivji]] within living memory, and its neighbours know its weight. We need not fear Gawli today. But the wise withdraw from appetite, and the appetite of this port for foreign cloth is already unseemly. Let the council at Nambi watch the silver as carefully as it watches the coast.

I add one thing more. When I asked an old captain of Gawli, through an interpreter, what his people had thought on first seeing our ships at [[set:63|Igu]], he laughed and said: "We thought you had the heads of dogs." It is good to be reminded that a stranger is only a stranger from one side of the water.
