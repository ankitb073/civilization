title: The Broken Jar (AY 1250–1500)

In AY 1250 a scribe of the granary at [[set:7|Ane]] stood in the palace court and watched an empire end on foot. On the first day a runner came from Tlaiy and did not bow. His lord, he said, was a king now and would send no more grain to Ana. On the second day a runner from Mii said the same of silver, and on the third a runner from Lishkhu said it of men. On the fourth and fifth days came runners from To'i'io'a and Foyatlitie. The king heard them all in silence. On the fifth day, the scribe writes, "he rose, and he tore his garment from the neck to the belt, and he went into his house."

The tablet that records this, *What the Scribe of the Granary Saw in the Year Ana Was Broken* (PAL·CH·08444), was written nine years later and survives almost whole. It gives this chapter its image. The old [[pol:229|Ana]] empire, the scribe says, "was poured out on the ground like water from a broken jar, and it cannot be gathered up again." He was right about the jar and wrong about the gathering. The [[evt:8021|Collapse of the Ana State]] produced six successors, and one of them, seated in the same city, kept the old name. That new [[pol:349|Ana]] lasted until 1540. "Ana stands," he admits a few lines later. "The city stands."

The two and a half centuries that followed broke many jars, and the largest broke at their fullest. Yinnga reached its greatest size in AY 1290 and fell apart in 1295. Puvu peaked in 1300 and fell in 1307. Tagath peaked in 1360 and fell in 1382. Waga held more districts in 1380 than any other power of the age and broke the next year. Yeda counted 10.5 million subjects in 1440 and was gone in 1442. Yet the world as a whole hardly changed. People living under states numbered 62.2 million in 1250, 63.8 million in 1370 and 64.6 million in 1490, and the number of states went from 38 to 34 to 36. This was an age of breaking, not of ruin. The pieces were picked up, very often under the old names.

## The great houses at full stretch

In AY 1370 only 3 per cent of the land lay outside any state. Four empires divided much of the rest. [[pol:153|Waga]], ruled from [[set:31|Bakh]], held 13 per cent of the land. [[pol:237|Tagath]], from Dagafu, held 12 per cent. [[pol:306|Yeda]], from Taulin, held 9 per cent and [[pol:310|Pome]], from Himi, 7 per cent. Around them stood [[pol:291|Tantethi]] at [[set:152|Tanshedi]] and the heirs that had grown up in the wreckage of the old [[pol:175|Yinnga]] at Yinnge and the [[pol:178|Puvu]] empire at [[set:12|Kabaf]].

It was an age of very long reigns. [[per:8884|Simenmovi]] of Waga came to the throne at fifteen and held it for seventy years (1307–1377). [[per:9307|Belkolvan]] the Old ruled Yeda for fifty (1342–1392), and [[per:9678|Voltalen]] ruled Pome for fifty (1383–1433). In Ana, [[per:9685|Wuelazhi]] the Old reigned from 1383 to 1432. Women held some of the greatest thrones. [[per:8375|E'alada]] ruled Waga for thirty-eight years after [[per:8282|Mi'sömamshe II]]. [[per:8150|Adatakhshalath]] the Old ruled Pome until 1270, and [[per:8462|Nangade]] ruled Puvu between 1259 and 1274. Tantethi was governed by [[per:9273|Lilie]] from 1363 to 1394 and by [[per:10421|Wie'i'iluesh'a I]] from 1457 to 1486.

Long reigns are not the same as stable states, and the record keeps that difference in view. Tantethi is the exception that shows the rule. It came through the whole period unbroken, under rulers from [[per:8671|Sha Nim]] (1282–1313) onward, and reached 8.0 million people and 302 districts in AY 1490. Its capital also fed itself best. After the 1280s grain at Tanshedi seldom cost more than 11 grams of silver the measure, and it fell below 8 in the 1430s and 1450s. At Ane in the same decades it cost twice as much.

## The back stair: usurpers, claimants and the Blue Fever

How did a full jar crack? Waga gives the clearest answer, because the record traces its fall over eighty years. In 1300 Lotla took the throne. A satirical poem from Bakh, *Verses on the Storm Snake* (PAL·PO·00031, AY 1349), says he "climbed to the high seat by the back stair, when no one was looking." The record counts his accession as a usurpation and draws a line from it. In 1306 a claimant, Yieloshmi, took Mieshlethie out of the realm in the first [[evt:8550|Secession of Mieshlethie]]. The next year [[evt:8481|The Blue Fever]] broke out at Biyitl and spread along the trade roads to 41 more cities, Bakh, Ane and Pivithie among them. It killed about 1,023,769 people, roughly one in five of those it reached. In 1316 a second claimant, Yiedi'simen, raised Mieshlethie again, and the record lists the old usurpation and the fever among the causes of the [[evt:8659|Secession of Mieshlethie]].

Lotla's son Simenmovi then ruled for seventy years, and in the forty-third of them the Storm Snake poet mocked him: "The storm snake went down to Tliyiya with eighteen thousand spears. / Tliyiya came out with a thousand, and a few old men, and a dog." The satirist's figures deserve respect. The record gives 18,490 Waga troops against 1,081 at Tliyiya in 1334, with 1,331 Waga dead against 291. Those are almost exactly the poem's "thirteen hundred" and "three hundred." The mocker counted more honestly than the royal steles of the age usually did. By 1380 Waga held 371 districts, the largest territory in the world. On his death in 1377, Yueppa'obi I seized the throne. He died in 1380, his son reigned for a year, and in 1381 came the [[evt:9346|Collapse of the Waga State]]. Its six heirs included the kingdom of [[pol:408|Eshue'ethie]] and the [[pol:410|Pibetie]] empire, which would last until 1802. The record names its causes as the secession of 1316 and the usurpation of 1377. The crack was sixty-five years old when the jar finally broke.

The other collapses follow the same pattern with local differences. In Yinnga, [[per:8460|Ngothshen I]] took the throne in 1259 from the line of [[per:8071|Dolomen]]. *The City Weeps for Her Six Daughters* (PAL·PO·00028, AY 1297), a lament excavated at Yinnge, remembers it: "A hand that was not given the seat took the seat. / From that day the great houses looked at one another sideways." In 1276 the king's own younger brother, another Ngothshen, set up Toramto as a rival realm in the [[evt:8253|Secession of Toramto]]. In 1295, under [[per:8525|Ngothshen II]] the Last, came the [[evt:8442|Collapse of the Yinnga State]]: "The one cloak was torn into six pieces." One daughter stayed at home, "[[pol:370|Yennga]] the small, the last, who sits in my walls." The poet called her a comfort, but too small. She lasted until 1721 and at her height ruled 8.1 million people.

Puvu fell in 1307. The record blames old defeats and two usurpations, the second by [[per:8640|Naguvu I]] in 1280. Two of its heirs, [[pol:377|Kabave]] at Kabaf and the [[pol:382|Düvnutside]] empire, were at war within three years. The [[evt:8615|First Kabave–Düvnutside War]] (1310–1324) was fought for [[reg:21|Daadüdhih]]. Its victory stele (PAL·IN·00593) claims 5,800 enemy dead at Daadüdhih in 1313, while the record gives 1,480. The Treaty of Dyizhih (PAL·TR·00561) is more sober: the land went to Kabave, and Düvnutside paid 23 talents of silver a year for thirty years.

Tagath fell more suddenly. In 1376 the province of Kambizhin broke away. At Dadavli in 1380 a rebel army of 4,121 routed a Tagath host of 24,326, killing 7,896 for the loss of 430. That same year [[per:9075|Nakithu]] died and the throne passed to a ten-year-old girl. In 1382, with the [[evt:9363|Collapse of the Tagath State]], six states stood where one had stood. Among them were a new [[pol:414|Tagath]] at [[set:9|Dagafu]] and the city-state of [[pol:415|Duntsinle]].

Yeda's end is the hardest to explain. Under Belkolvan and his successors it grew to 10.5 million people and 343 districts by 1440, the most populous state of the age. Its silver Nevba had held at 8.5 grams since 1152. Yet the record's measure of internal order fell steadily over the last two reigns until almost nothing was left, and in 1442 the empire broke into six. No war, plague or famine is recorded among the causes. A new [[pol:439|Yeda]] at [[set:0|Taulin]] took up the name, beside the kingdoms of [[pol:440|Navnga]] and [[pol:442|Jenontono]]. The rest of this chapter returns to both.

## Drought, fever and the price of bread

The period was mild overall, about 0.13 °C warmer than the long-run mean. Its disasters were regional, and they show up best in the price of grain. The [[evt:8604|Onset of the Great Drought in Diebeguvebenga (AY 1312)]] began a fifty-year dry spell over 485 districts. At [[set:17|Himi]], the Pome capital in the stricken country, a measure of grain cost about 11 grams of silver in the 1290s, 18.5 in the 1310s and almost 30 in the 1330s. It fell back to 15.6 by the 1370s. The record ties the famine of 1330 in Mana, with about 98,600 dead, to this drought and to a battle at Akhtse. At Tivintukhe, in Mana, grain rose from about 10 grams in the 1280s to 54.5 in the 1330s. Pome's own civil war of 1323, the [[evt:8755|Secession of Lenedagoshal]], fell in the same dry years. The record gives it other causes (an old defeat, a usurper), and we should not merge the two stories too quickly.

The second cluster came around 1400. The [[evt:9450|Onset of the Great Drought in Dyimbi (AY 1399)]] helped bring famine to Tamthal that same year: 230,885 people died, about one in nine. At Ishwyithie, in Tamthal, grain reached 27 grams in the 1390s and 42 in the 1410s. Then the [[evt:9582|Onset of the Great Drought in Pyivi (AY 1407)]] began the longest dry spell of the age. It lay over 296 districts and did not lift until 1541. It brought famine to Khanji in 1412 (91,221 dead) and to Ayshi in 1413 and again in 1479. The Khanji famine set 194,899 Khudule moving into Ng'jinaguh. The [[evt:9687|Onset of the Great Drought in Nis (AY 1410)]] and the drought in Tunnge from 1470 to 1484 added to the strain.

Yet the median grain price across forty to fifty markets stayed between about 10.5 and 14 grams in every decade of the period. Not one of the great silver standards was debased. Pome's Rongel held at 10.6 grams and Tantethi's Ishcua at 10. Hunger was local and moved from region to region. It was terrible where it struck, and it did not touch the money. Trade kept going around it. Fifty-seven merchant houses were founded in these years. The house of Lena at Puthnimdu traded timber, copper and olive oil from 1257 to 1430. The house of Adhambi at Ajü, founded in 1418, dealt in copper, horses, bronze, grain and spices and was still trading in 1636. A horse at Taulin cost about 540 grams of silver in the 1250s and about 450 in the 1490s.

## The fields of Pyivi

Two of the greatest wars of the age were fought over one country. "Pyivi is a good land," a Tagath poet sang. "Its fields are black, its water is sweet." Tagath went to war for [[reg:24|Pyivi]] again and again. The first of the two was the [[evt:8152|Fourth Pyivi Trade War]] (1263–1270), against the kingdom of [[pol:334|Nelo]], which had adopted Uthungala in 1251. The war opens with a letter (PAL·LT·00982) from Nelo's king Nangasho I to Nandithim II of Tagath: "Your men have seized my merchants and taken their goods. Return what was taken, or I will come and take it. If you will not answer, let the sword answer." Nandithim was six years old.

Tagath lost the first two battles. At Dadifi in 1264, 12,063 Tagath troops were beaten by 4,659. Tagath lost 4,314 men and its commander Natsunge, Nelo only 342. The Tagath account (PAL·CH·01431) admits the defeat but gives the losses as 2,105 and 1,280, blaming "omens neglected." Tagath won the next five battles, and the peace of 1270 made Nelo a vassal. Nelo ceded eleven districts, opened its trade and paid an indemnity the record values at some 12,560 kilograms of silver. The victory cost Tagath 14,966 dead, against 5,924 for Nelo. Nelo's ally, the city of [[pol:335|Kavu]], changed gods in the middle of the war with the [[evt:8197|Conversion of Kavu to Khupande]] in 1264.

The second war was the greatest of the age. In the [[evt:10052|Tagath–Kabave War]] (1445–1458), king fought king. Nakthi of Tagath, who came to the throne in 1443 at about twenty-five, took the field against [[per:9868|Nagungi]] the Great of Kabave. Nagungi had been king since he was ten and was now nearly fifty. Twelve battles were fought around the walls of Dadifi and Khavazhu, and each side won six. In 1451 Tagath troops sacked Kabaf. The war cost Tagath 19,811 dead and Kabave 19,509, and the treaty of 1458 restored the old frontier with a ten-year truce. Nagungi died in 1455, in the middle of it, and Nakthi in 1457. Both died of natural causes, the record says, not by the spear.

Our best source is also our most partial. The *Song of Nakthi and the Fields of Pyivi* (PAL·PO·00005), a fragmentary tablet excavated at Dagafu, was written in 1481, in the fourth year of a later king. It celebrates the victories. Where it reaches a defeat it says: "Let the singer not count them." When it does count, though, it counts well. "Five hundred of ours lay down; three thousand of theirs," it says of Dadifi in 1453, and the record gives 503 and 3,198. The Tagath prose account (PAL·CH·01805) does the opposite. It records the defeats but changes their numbers: at Dadifi in 1446 it claims 1,046 Tagath dead and 4,526 of the enemy, where the record has 3,538 and 1,341. Read together, the poem's silences and the chronicle's arithmetic show the shape of the truth. The peace oath in the song may be the most honest line of all: "What is yours is yours; what is ours is ours." The war was fought in the middle of the Pyivi drought, over fields that had been parched for nearly forty years.

## New gods, new tongues and the bill for Pome

Faith shifted at the edges of the great states rather than at their centres. In 1370 La'im held 17 per cent of the land, Jinru 10 and Ya'cuam and Kieve 8 each. The kingdom of [[pol:352|Lishkhu]], one of Ana's heirs, adopted [[mov:13|Onsatlmom]] in 1250. In 1379 [[per:9613|Nwetl'a]] founded the [[mov:39|Ta]] cult at Tuzhi, and the end of the period brought a cluster of new cults. The [[evt:10286|Founding of the Soshöne Cult]] at Ishwyithie in 1460 was followed by Tanngompe in 1461, Dyivinduvu in 1470 and Füligümgi, founded at Dagafu in 1495. Speech changed more slowly and more steadily. The record dates seventy-two sound changes to the period, such as intervocalic voicing in Tasemem around 1350. It counts eight new languages, among them Puif and Khongoghe by 1299, Nenan and Milosh by 1399, and Inun and Khugan by 1499. In 1485 the war chariot appeared in Tantethi, credited to Ish'o Payal. It was an independent invention, and Luthe would arrive at the same weapon on its own eighty years later.

Pome's last decades show how winning could ruin a state. The [[evt:9293|Second Pome–Buneynga War]] (1376–1385), against the [[pol:373|Buneynga]] empire, another of Yinnga's daughters, ended early in Voltalen's reign with land in Baan and Diebeguvebenga ceded to Pome. In 1427, after another victory, he told his people at Himi (PAL·PM·00056): "Forty-five years I have sat on the throne of my fathers. Forty-five years the gods have not turned their face from me." In the [[evt:10409|Tonnga–Pome War]] (1480–1490), the kingdom of [[pol:403|Tonnga]] attacked to take land, and Pome won. Tonnga paid an indemnity of about 15,000 kilograms of silver. But Pome lost 18,145 men to Tonnga's 9,088. An old Tonnga spearman remembered the night march to Diviminnge in 1482 (PAL·CH·08434): "Of us there fell not two hundred. Of them there fell thousands upon thousands, five thousand and more." The record gives 170 and 5,868, and once again the man in the ranks counts better than the king. In 1496 Pome began the [[evt:10601|Second Pome–Navnga War]] against Navnga, one of Yeda's heirs. In the same years the new Yeda forced Jenontono to yield six districts of Tuvizhinge and pay 28 talents of silver a year for twenty-nine years, under the Treaty of Ramtuunge (PAL·TR·00684).

These voices are rare. From AY 1250–1500, besides some two dozen literary works, 808 documents survive in the record: 323 inscriptions, 203 chronicles, 101 treaties, 29 annals, 27 ledgers and 25 lawsuits. Most of what we can read was carved for kings, and kings inflate. The granary scribe, the satirist of Bakh and the spearman of Tonnga reach us by luck, and each of them corrects the stone.

The record lists Pome's defeats at Diviminnge and Nuwkennga in the Tonnga war, and two more at Ontiedzey against Navnga in 1498 and 1499, among the causes of its fall. Pome won both wars, and the losses broke it anyway. The next chapter begins in AY 1500 with that bill coming due, at Himi, in 1517.
