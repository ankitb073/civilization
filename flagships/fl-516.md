I, [[per:23924|Shuthkhan]], son of a grain-factor of [[set:76|Nimith]], once a server at the altars of [[mov:13|Unhatlmum]] and now nothing but a learner, set down this account of how I was turned, so that those who come to me at [[set:226|Kivuna]] may judge the teaching by its fruits rather than by its teacher.

I was raised in the old faith, and I do not repent of it. My mother took me as a child to the house of [[dei:85|Niwi]] of the river, and I carried the first sheaf to [[dei:86|Shu]] of the grain each harvest until I was a grown man. I learned that many gods share the world, each with their place and due; that the priests keep the truth and hand it down in order; and that a full granary is the sign of heaven's favour.

But I learned also to count, for my father wished me to follow him in the grain trade; and a man who counts will sooner or later count the wrong thing.

In the year of the [[evt:23945|Battle of Zhaalakhazhash]] I was thirty-two, and a clerk of the granaries on the road to the west. The emperor himself, [[per:23509|Millusha]], had gone out with the host, and we had sacrificed for him at every altar. The omens were good. In the autumn the carts came back. I was set to tally them. I will not write what was in them; only that the granaries I kept were full that year, fuller than any year I remember, and that the men who should have eaten from them were in the carts. If prosperity was the sign of favour, then heaven had favoured us exceedingly, and I could not bear to look at heaven.

The next year the field at Zhaalakhazhash was ours, and the priests said the gods had relented. Then came the two years at Lishimti, and the [[evt:23961|Treaty of Lishimti]], and the priests said the gods were angry again. I began to suspect that the gods were not angry or pleased at all, but that we were reading our own fortunes in their faces, as a man reads his own face in a bowl of water.

For years after I read every book I could buy, and argued with every teacher who would stop. I learned much and believed little, and was proud of my doubts, as other men are proud of their horses.

What turned me was no vision. At Kivuna, two springs ago, I sat by a dying neighbour, a carter, who had driven one of the carts back from Zhaalakhazhash and had never spoken of it. He asked me which god would take him. I began to name them, and stopped; for it came to me with great clearness that the names were many but the taking was one. Behind [[dei:83|Hath]] and [[dei:88|Mahtu]] and [[dei:87|Hutlhi]] who receives the dead, there is one Power, of which they are the servants, or perhaps only the shadows on the wall. I told him so. He said, "Then I need only be afraid once," and died easier than most men I have seen die.

That is my whole revelation. I have built on it since, slowly, with friends. We hold that the one Power turns the world as a wheel turns, and that what ends will begin again. We hold that justice in dealings and moderation in all things are its worship, more than smoke. We hold that teachers deserve honour but that no teacher owns the truth, not I least of all. We hold that a full granary may indeed be a sign of favour, but only where it was filled justly. And we hold that this is for every people, not for one, so that we will speak of it to anyone who asks.

The brothers have begun to call themselves the [[mov:84|Kivali]], after this town. I wish they would not; but one cannot prevent people from naming things.

To the officers of the emperor I say: I teach obedience to the laws and the paying of dues. To those who come to me I say: question me, as I questioned everyone.

*Written at Kivuna, in the year 182 of Tsiy.*
