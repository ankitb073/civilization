title: One House

The dead came in by the river gate, and Fispayueva counted them.

He sat on an upturned grain basket in the gate-passage with a tablet on his knee and a wet cloth over the half he had not used, and for every man the bearers carried past him he pressed one stroke into the clay with the butt of his reed. He did not look at the faces. That was the priest's work. The priest stood across the passage with a bowl of river water, and touched each forehead, and said each name aloud as though someone at the far end of the world were waiting to write it down. Fispayueva wrote no names. The palace had asked for a number.

Five hundred and fifty-nine men had gone out through this gate in the morning. He had watched them from the wall: a short brown column with the early sun on its spearheads, going out between the barley fields toward a dust that already stood over the river road. Under the dust was the king of [[pol:612|Pibotia]], who had come himself this time, a man of twenty-two who had been a king since he was eight, with more than eight men for every one of theirs.

And the host had not run. Fispayueva wanted that set down somewhere, if only in himself. They had stood in the barley all morning and given nearly as good as they got; out beyond the fields, though no one on the wall could know it, the men of Pibotia would bury forty-two of their own that night. Then, a little after noon, the line had bent in the middle, the way a reed bends, and come home.

His son had come in at the head of the living an hour before, with a cut along his forearm and his helmet gone, and Fispayueva had not counted him. The palace had asked only for the dead.

Forty-five. Forty-six. The bearers' feet slapped in the wet of the passage. On the inner face of the gate, where the lamps caught it, hung the bronze plaque a king of the old line had set there when he restored this gate, more than a hundred and sixty years ago. Fispayueva had read it on every evening of his life that he had come home this way: "Esiesh Lieze, king of Yi'otl, restored this gate in Mieshlothia. May Niele bless the city." Outside, the river went by, brown and unhurried, as it had gone by under every king on every tablet in the house.

Forty-seven. The forty-eighth came on a shield, and the bearers set him down a moment in the passage because they were tired, and the priest bent to him and straightened and said, "Esiesh Tlie." Then, as one does for a man the whole city knows, he said the day the name had come from, so that the gods would not mistake him for another: "One House."

Fispayueva pressed the forty-eighth stroke. It went in deeper than the others. He had not meant it to.

When he had wrapped the tablet and climbed, knees cracking, to the wall-walk, the fires of Pibotia had come up in the dark all round the landward side of [[set:2|Mieshlothia]], one beyond another, from the river round to the shore. Someone near him on the wall said aloud the word that everyone had been not saying since noon. Nobody answered him.

* * *

The queen's runner found him in the house of tablets an hour later. [[per:14206|Foyatlmamshe]] wished to hear the years of her house set down on one tablet, from the first year of her grandfather to this one. Tonight. She would come herself.

Nobody said why tonight, and nobody needed to. A house with fires around it writes itself down.

He woke Awshitl, his pupil, who was asleep against the clay bin, and sent him to the cistern for water, and trimmed a new reed. Then he sat with the cloth off the clay and his hands in his lap and went through the years.

There were thirty-four of them, and he had spent twenty-six in this room. He had come to the house in the year the city fixed the weight of the [[cur:321|Izal]], and his first tablet had been that weight, and the city had kept it honest ever since. He had written down the price of grain for twenty-six harvests. In the spring it had stood at about two Izal the measure. He did not know where it would stand by the new moon, and he found he did not want to.

But grain was not what the house kept. The house kept thrones.

He wrote the grandfather and the father: "Sho'a Yueva died; Ma'sha Niema sat on the throne." Nine years on he wrote, "Ma'sha Niema passed from the world; Foyatlmamshe sat on the throne, being a child." She had been thirteen. Her mother had held the seal for her three years after that, and he did not write it, because the house wrote who sat, not who held. Seals held for children were the diviners' business. Their tablets were full of them: "a young prince will rule, and another will hold his seal." The diviners kept a different account. They wrote down the gods' displeasure, sign by sign, so that kings not yet born might see it coming in the liver of a sheep. Defeats belonged to them. "The weapon of the enemy will prevail," they wrote, and named the king.

The house of tablets kept the other account. Every priest of [[mov:0|La'im]] taught it, and every child in the city knew it before he could hold a reed: prosperity was the sign of the gods' favour. What the house wrote down, it showed to the gods, and you did not show the gods their own anger.

For the seventh year he wrote, "Pibotia crossed the frontier in arms." That was not a defeat. That was weather.

The eighth year was Sase. Two hundred and twenty-three men had not come home from Sase, and he had never counted them, because they had not come home to be counted. The queen had led them. She had been twenty. He pressed nothing for the eighth year.

The ninth year he wrote with something that was almost the old pleasure. "The host fought at Mieshlothia; the enemy fled." Eight hundred and one men had gone out by the river gate that day, the queen at their head at twenty-one, against nearly six thousand, and more than thirteen hundred of the enemy had stayed in the barley. He had sat on the same basket in the same passage and counted a hundred and eight of the city's own, and gone home, and slept like a child. Afterwards a herald had come under a green bough to ask for the enemy's dead, their general among them, and had said the general's name at the gate the way the men of Pibotia said everything, with the vowels pressed flat: Esiesh Tlie. One House.

Fispayueva put down the reed.

He had heard it then and thought nothing; it was a day-name, and there were a great many days. He had heard it again tonight in the passage and thought nothing, because he was counting. He thought of it now. Two men named for the same day, who had died before the same walls, on the two sides of them. The wheel turns, the priests said; what ends will begin again. He had always taken that for a comfort.

On the shelf by his elbow lay the tablet he loved best in the house, a schoolboy's list of the kings of the old line, nearly two hundred years old. The boy had given one king forty-three years, and the teacher had struck the number through and written thirty-nine after it, and under the list, pressed hard enough to last, had added: "You will be beaten if this happens again." In all the house of tablets it was the only place Fispayueva knew of where a wrong number had been put right.

* * *

The queen came near midnight, with one lamp-bearer and the dust of the wall still on her.

She was twenty-three. She sat down on the edge of the clay bin without being asked, and Awshitl, who had come back with the water, stood against the shelves and tried not to exist.

"Read it," she said.

He read her the grandfather and the father. He read, "Foyatlmamshe sat on the throne, being a child," and she laughed once, without much in it.

"My mother will like that. Go on."

He read the seventh year and the ninth. Then he stopped, and she waited, and he did not go on.

"That's all?"

"That is all the house keeps, lady."

"Sase."

"The house does not write Sase."

"Two hundred and twenty-three," she said. "I don't need it written. I know it the way I know my own name." She looked at the lamp. "And today?"

"Today is not finished."

"How many?"

"Forty-eight."

"With him?"

"He was the forty-eighth."

She was quiet. Beyond the wall the camp of Pibotia had begun to sing. It came over in pieces on the wind off the land: a hymn to [[dei:5|Niezhashta]], who gives the victory. It was the city's own hymn, to the city's own goddess, with the vowels pressed flat.

"They pray to her too," the queen said. "Have you ever wondered how she chooses?"

"The priests say she gives it to the side that has given her her due."

"We gave her her due. I stood beside the priest this morning and watched him do it. Their king did the same in his tent, I expect, with a bigger ox." She turned her hands over in her lap and looked at the palms. "Did you know his name? Theirs. The one who died out there in my ninth year."

"Esiesh Tlie, lady."

"One House." She said it to the lamp. "I saw him go down. I was near enough to see his face. And afterwards, when they told me what he was called, I thought the gods were saying something to me. I thought it was a promise." She was silent for a while. "It was a promise. I heard the wrong half of it."

There was nothing in the house of tablets that answered that, and he did not try.

"Will you write him?" she said. "Ours."

"The house has never written a general's name, lady. Not ours. Not theirs."

She thought about that for a long moment.

"Then at least the tablet treats them alike," she said. "Someone should."

She stood, and the lamp-bearer in the doorway lifted his lamp.

"How far does it go?"

"To the ninth year."

"Write the heading to the eleventh."

"There will be nothing under the tenth and the eleventh, lady."

"I know what is under them. I was there for all of it." She was at the door. "They were my years. I want them counted."

* * *

When she had gone he wet the clay again, and wrote the heading across the top in the best hand he had, the hand he had been taught at a bench like Awshitl's: "The record of the years of Yi'otl, from the 1st year of Sho'a Yueva to the 11th year of Foyatlmamshe."

Then he sat and looked at it.

Eleven, said the heading. Nine, said the lines beneath it. There was no lie anywhere on the tablet. He had written nothing that had not happened, and nothing the house had not always written. He had only left a sum undone, the way the schoolboy's sum had been left lying for the teacher. Anyone who could count to eleven, in this city or in any other, in this year or in a year when nobody remembered what the city had been called, would come to the foot of the tablet two years short, and stop, and wonder where they had gone. It was not much. It was what a scribe had.

The two men named for the same day were not on it, and never would be. That was the only fairness he had to give them, and he gave it to them both.

At the foot, under the ninth year, he pressed the line that ended every tablet of the house, and the only name on this one that had never sat on a throne: "Written by Fispayueva, scribe of the house of tablets."

He set it on the shelf to dry beside the schoolboy's list, and woke Awshitl and sent him home, and went home himself through the grey streets as the drums started up again beyond the wall. He passed the granary an old queen had built, where the bricks still asked "May Muevas'a bless the city," and the night watch standing at its doors with spears. He passed the temple, where the priests were already up and the ox for the morning was lowing in the yard.

At home his son lay asleep on his side, with the bound arm across his chest, breathing. Fispayueva stood in the doorway a long time. Then, because it was the habit of his life and he could not help it, he counted him: one. He did not write it down anywhere. It was the first number in twenty-six years that he kept entirely for himself.

**Author's note.** The tablet is real: the Annals of [[pol:618|Yi'otl]] (PAL·AN·00292), whose heading counts to the eleventh year of [[per:14206|Foyatlmamshe]] and whose entries stop at the ninth. The gate, granary, omens and schoolboy are PAL·IN·04095, PAL·IN·04096, PAL·OM·00121 and PAL·SX·00189. The record keeps what the annals drop: Sase, and the [[evt:14326|Battle of Myishlithie]] of AY 1838, where the general Esiesh Tlie was killed at thirty-four, as Pibotia's Esiesh Tlie had been at the [[evt:14304|Battle of Myishlithie]] of 1836. It does not say how the [[evt:14327|Second Siege of Myishlithie]] ended. The next year the city fell; she signed it away.
