title: So I Know What I Gave
---
The men who came for Tadimavi's stones talked the way her mother had talked.

She was sitting on the floor of the back room with the weights in her lap, knotted into the blue cloth, when the street door gave, and the first voice in the courtyard said *there's an old woman, find her* with the long slow vowels of the up-country. Her mother had said *water* like that, and *daughter*, all her life, and the children of the river-quarter had laughed at her for it. Tadimavi heard those vowels now and something in her went looking for her mother before it remembered to be afraid.

There were three of them. Two were boys, burned the colour of wet clay and sweating the way hill men sweat on the coast at the end of the hot months. The third was older than she was, grey in the beard, his forearm bound in a strip of somebody's good linen. He looked at the cloth in her lap. Then he looked at the balance on its peg by the door, its beam worn pale by thirty years of her husband's thumbs and twelve of hers.

— You're the weigher.

— I weigh.

— Silver?

— Silver, barley, wool. Whatever's brought.

He nodded, as if she had given the right answer at a gate.

— Bring the stones. Bring the beam. Nobody will hurt you.

One of the boys had lifted the lid off the oil jar. The old man said a word to him, not loudly, and the boy put the lid back. The other boy was looking at the rolled sleeping-mat in the corner and the pair of sandals set beside it, toes to the wall, the way her son had left them. Tadimavi stood up so that he would look at her instead.

* * *

The news from Ta'i'nakhath had come down to the coast before the enemy did. The king's host had been broken there. The young king, [[per:1474|Inude]], was alive, people said; people said a great many things. Isi'igw'e had marched out with the host in his father's old leather cap, and nobody in the river-quarter had seen him since, and nobody had seen him dead either. Then the men of [[pol:42|Nimiti]] had come and sat down before the one wall, and the wall had held for a while, and then it had not. That was all she knew. In the morning there had been smoke over the reed-quarter and a sound of women, and after that a kind of quiet she had never heard in [[set:13|Tivintusa]], the quiet of a city in which nobody is selling anything.

They walked her to the market. She had walked that way every morning of her life, and she found she knew it now by its smells, because she would not look at the doorways: the dye-pits, the fish-drying racks, the hot breath of the shallow sea at the end of every street that ran down to the shore. The hill boys kept turning their heads toward it. They had never seen so much water.

The market of the river-quarter was full of everything the river-quarter owned. It lay on reed mats in heaps: cloth in one, copper pans in another, bronze in a third, and nearest the gate of judgment, on a mat of its own, the silver. Rings, coils, earrings, little spirals of wire, the cut pieces that merchants chop from a bar to pay with. Men of Nimiti sat in the shade of the awnings drinking the city's beer from the city's cups. Oxen stood yoked to carts by the granary, and boys were carrying barley out in sacks, a river of it, the whole harvest walking away.

In the middle of it all, on its old plinth, sat the stone.

It was black and the size of a sleeping cat, and it had been there since the king who made the city one, more than three hundred years ago, the king who did away with the clan gates and put up one wall and one gate and set down one stone for the weighing of silver. Every weight in the river-quarter had been tried against it. Hers had, at the new year, every year. The old women still said what that king had said when the clans grumbled: that "no one is cheated, for the stone is one stone."

The grey man saw her looking.

— That's why you're here. We want it weighed against that.

He squatted beside the silver. Close to, he smelled of smoke and onions.

— Every man of mine gets his share by weight, he said. If I weigh it with our stones, they'll say I cheated them. If you weigh it with yours, they'll say you cheated them. Either way they won't say it of each other.

He opened his own bag to show her. His stones were cut to the measure of the old empire his people had lately served, a little heavier than an [[cur:3|Awe]]. She had seen such stones in the hands of up-country traders and had always watched them carefully.

— Weigh true, mother, he said. [[dei:86|Shu'i]] is looking at the pan.

— She is looking at your hand in it, too.

He laughed, which she had not expected.

— She's my goddess as much as yours. Our king keeps the old empire's gods at his court, and the priests pour for them, and we let them. In the villages we pour to Shu'i.

* * *

She hung the beam from the awning-post and began.

She did not choose what came next to the pan. The boys brought it by the double handful, and she weighed it, and called the weight, and the younger boy, the one who had put back the lid, laid pebbles in rows on the ground to keep the count. She had thought it would be the silver she minded. It was not. It was that she knew it.

Here was a pair of anklets, worn thin on the inner side where they had rubbed: Al'bamavi's, the net-maker's girl, married at the last harvest. Tadimavi had weighed them for the bride-price herself, with the girl's mother standing at her elbow breathing on the beam. Here was a coil of wire from the oil-seller with the bad eye, who always paid in wire and always swore it was heavier than it was. Here was a scrap of cut silver she knew by the chisel mark, a twist at one corner like a bent finger, that had passed through her pans four times since the rains, from the dyer to the potter to the boatman to she could not now remember. Here was a child's bead on a cord. She weighed that too. A weigher does not choose what the pan holds.

Her husband had taught her every trick of the trade so that she would know them in other people's hands: the thumb that rests a hair too long on the beam, the damp cloth under the stone, the pan that is rubbed thinner on one side. She could have done any of them. The grey man would not have seen it; the boys could not count past the pebbles. She thought about it the whole length of the morning. She did not do it. She could not have said why, except that the stone was the city's, and the beam was her husband's, and the only thing left in the market that still belonged to the river-quarter was the fact that its weights were true.

In the heat of the day the grey man brought her water and sat with her in the strip of shade.

— You have someone at Ta'i'nakhath, he said. Not a question.

— My son.

He drank before he answered.

— We were two to your one there. Yidi'sim'i had us. Your men stood longer than they should have. After, we laid your dead in rows and counted them, because men will lie about a thing like that later. A hundred and sixty-seven.

— And yours?

— Thirty-six.

— How many went out with the king?

— Eight hundred, a little over.

She did what she did all day. She put eight hundred in one pan and the hundred and sixty-seven in the other and waited for the beam. One in five. Four in one pan, one in the other. Somewhere in the heavier pan, men who had run, men who had been taken, men walking home by the back roads with their sandals in their hands.

— I was born a subject of your king's grandfather, the grey man said, looking at the sea. Eleven years ago your king's father put his seal to a treaty and gave my town to the old empire to buy his peace. Now the old empire has fallen to pieces and we have a king of our own. So here I am.

— Here you are.

Her mother had come down from the hills to be married. Tadimavi had never once thought to ask her from which village.

— Your king is young. He'll come up our road next year and do this to us, I expect. That's how it goes between kin.

* * *

In the afternoon two men came across the market carrying the plaque from the granary wall between them.

She knew it as everyone in the river-quarter knew it, a slab of bronze the length of a forearm, green at the edges, set beside the granary door at the height of a man's eyes, rows of signs cut into it that nobody she knew could read. They had prised it off with spear-butts. One of the pegs was still stuck through the corner, bent.

— For the bronze heap, one of them said.

The younger boy, the pebble-counter, crouched and touched the signs.

— What does it say?

Nobody could read it, but every child in the river-quarter could say it, the way every child can say what the well is called. Tadimavi said it.

— "Nitllu Ash, king of Tivintusa, restored this granary in Tivintusa. May Lashli'i bless the city."

The grey man was quiet a while.

— My grandfather swore by Nitllu Ash, he said. And [[dei:84|Lashli'i]] is the god my mother lit lamps to. Weigh it.

It took the great stone and three of the small ones before the beam came level, and the awning-post bowed with it. She called the weight. The boy did not lay down any pebbles; he looked at the old man.

— Put it back, the old man said.

— Then why weigh it? said the boy.

— So I know what I gave him.

The two men carried it away, grumbling, back toward the granary, where there was no longer any barley for the god to bless.

* * *

By evening the heaps were gone into bags and the bags were gone onto the carts and the carts were creaking toward the gate, and the market of the river-quarter was a swept floor with a stone on it. The grey man came and stood in front of her with his share over his shoulder.

— Your due, he said. A weigher's paid.

He set the bag down at her feet and opened its mouth.

— Take it from mine.

She did not have to look for long. The anklets had gone into his share with the last weighing of the morning; she had watched the boy push them across the mat with the rest.

She took them.

— Those are heavier than your due, he said.

— Weigh them.

He looked at her for a long moment, at the anklets, at the beam she was already lifting down from the post.

— No, he said. I know what they weigh. You called it this morning.

He went away after his carts with his long up-country stride, and she did not see him again.

* * *

That night [[dei:84|Lashli'i]] rose nearly full and laid a path across the shallows to the end of her street. On her way home she went round by the granary. They had hung the plaque back on its one good peg and it was crooked, one corner low, and the moon was on the bronze.

At home she wrapped the anklets in the blue cloth with her stones, to carry to the reed-quarter in the morning, if there was a reed-quarter in the morning. She hung the balance back on its peg by the door, and the beam rocked, the empty pans swinging, the way it always did when it was put up. Her husband had told her that a weigher never reads the beam while it is still moving. You wait for it. You keep your hands away from it and you wait.

She sat down facing the door, with her back to the beam, and waited.
