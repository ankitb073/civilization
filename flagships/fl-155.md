title: Naguvu's Lecture on the Ordering of Knowledge

Fellows of the academy, honoured teachers, and you young men at the back who have come, I suspect, for the shade of this hall: I thank you. It is a year now since I first read to a few of you the pages that some have been kind enough to call [[evt:15824|a new philosophy]], and others less kind to call a dressing-up of old ones. I have come today, in the year 2715 from the founding of [[set:33|Kambizhin]], to say what I think I have done, and what I have not.

Let me begin with what I have not done. I have not invented reasoning. Men reasoned in [[pol:595|Kambizhin]] before I was born, and they reasoned in other lands before Kambizhin had walls. The merchants who come to our markets bring us, among their bales of cloth, certain books in which men argue from one thing to another, and I have read them as eagerly as any of you. Our own faith, the teaching of [[mov:11|Kyif]], tells us that teachers are to be honoured, but that no one owns the truth. I do not own it. I claim only to have set some part of it in order, as a steward sets a storehouse in order, so that a man who needs a thing may find it.

That is the whole of what I mean by a system. First, define each thing, so that we know of what we speak. Second, divide each thing into its kinds, so that we do not mistake one kind for another. Third, from what is agreed, proceed step by step to what is not yet agreed, and let no step be taken that cannot be retraced. It is very simple. It is so simple that it is nearly never done.

Permit me an example, and forgive me if it touches a wound. Fifteen years ago, at [[evt:15732|Mbilu]], our king [[per:15308|Natyif]], whom the gods have kept to a great and honoured age, stood with ten thousand men against fourteen thousand, and he won, and the captain of the enemy lay dead on the field. Two years later, at [[evt:15753|Dangafu]], our army stood five against six, and lost. The next year at [[evt:15758|Dangafu]] it lost again, and the next year at [[evt:15764|Dangafu]] again. Then came [[evt:15765|the treaty of Dangafu]], by which that town is no longer ours, and we pay the price of it still.

Now, how do men in the market explain these things? Some say the gods favoured us at Mbilu and turned from us at Dangafu. Perhaps they did; I would not presume to say what [[dei:73|Tithu]] intends when he sends rain upon a battlefield. But observe: the explanation explains everything, and therefore it explains nothing. Others say that numbers decide battles. Then Mbilu is a riddle. Others say that courage decides them. Then Dangafu is an insult to our dead, who were not cowards.

A systematic man will do otherwise. He will define what a victory is. He will divide the causes of victory into their kinds: the ground, the season, the supply, the commander, the order of the ranks, the spirit of the men. He will set down, for each battle, what is known under each head, and only then compare. I do not say this will tell us the will of the gods. I say it will tell us what lay in our own hands, which is the part of the matter we are responsible for.

Let me not be misunderstood. I am a loyal son of this city. I pour to [[dei:74|Binge]] at the harvest and to Pugazhu at midsummer as my father did. I believe, with all of you, that the good life is the life of duty and right order. But I believe also that [[dei:76|Tsiumu]], whom we call the giver of wisdom, did not give it to us that we might leave it folded in a chest.

Some of my colleagues say this method will make young men quarrelsome. Perhaps. But a young man who defines his terms will at least quarrel about the same thing as his opponent, which is more than can be said for most quarrels in this hall.

I submit these pages, then, to the academy, under my own name, [[per:15722|Naguvu]], to be corrected, divided, defined and improved by any one of you. That too is part of the system. I thank you.
