**1 Shith 2368 of the Vimikhulu.**

*Six in the morning.* Woken by the trams, or rather by their absence. Not a bell on the boulevard. The night porter says the drivers came out at midnight, the printers at two, and that the wire to the provinces has been dead since three. He said it as he might report a frost. I dressed in the court coat out of habit, and then took it off and put on a plain one, also out of habit, the habit of the last month.

*Half past seven.* The Secretary-General of the Household has not come in. His deputy has, and is burning paper in the grate of the small library, which has not had a fire lit in it since the old furniture went. I asked what he was burning. "The engagements diary," he said, "and the guest lists." I asked why. He said, "Because they have names in them." I went back to my own room and looked at my own desk for some time.

*Nine.* From the north windows one can see the crowds coming down from the mill quarters towards the Assembly. Red-and-white armbands, which until lately belonged only to the men who came back from the [[evt:46412|Revolt of Fièvi]] and talked late in the cafés. Now everyone in the street has one. A woman selling bread on the corner had pinned hers to her apron.

Bread, by the way, is the whole matter, whatever the newspapers will write. A measure of grain is near a [[cur:901|Shikh]] and a half this week; my own cook says so, and she does not read newspapers. Since the [[evt:46106|Debasement of the Shikh]] no wage has kept pace with the price of anything, and the Assembly has argued about it ever since and done nothing at all. I have heard deputies laugh about it at our own dinners. I wrote that down at the time and did not think I would ever read it back.

*Noon.* Shots, not many, from the direction of the Assembly. Then nothing. Then cheering, which was worse.

*Two in the afternoon.* A delegation of the Party at the east gate: six men and a girl of about twenty with a typewritten paper. The officer of the guard, a decent man, read it, looked at his own soldiers for a long while, and opened the gate. Nobody fired. I record that, because it will be said later that someone did.

*Four.* We were assembled in the long gallery, all of us who remained: secretaries, ushers, the two chaplains of the [[mov:70|Vimikhulu]], the women of the linen-room. A man from the committee read to us. The Republic is dissolved. The Assembly is dissolved. The state of [[pol:1638|Khishkhih]] is henceforth to be guided by the Party and the Party alone, and [[per:45822|Nambulavi]] is first in the state. He was not present. Those of us who would serve the new order were to give our names; those who would not were to go home and stay there. Most gave their names. I gave mine. I am not proud of it, and I am not ashamed of it, and I suspect that is what most of my colleagues feel.

The elder chaplain afterwards said a thing I want to keep. "The wheel turns," he said, "and every man thinks the turning is his doing. Our teachers have always said that no one owns the truth. Now we shall see whether anyone can own a country." Then he went to say the evening office, as if nothing had happened, which I suppose is the meaning of an office.

*Nine at night.* The trams are running again, very slowly, with flags on them. From my window the whole of [[set:85|Nambi]] seems to be singing. I do not know the song. Tomorrow, I am told, it will be taught in the schools.

They call it the [[evt:46609|Khishkhis Revolution]] already, in the evening sheets. It took one day. I have served in this house for nineteen years.
