Two clay tablets are all that [[per:54800|Nguttun]] left. Both were dug up at [[set:79|Taalinnuy]] in AY 4908, when Nambili excavated the citadel mound and temple precinct; one is burned at the edges, the other water-stained. He was born a Kunelmu in AY 938, his name meant *horse fire*, and he was priest of [[pol:119|Tonnga]] from AY 958 until his natural death in AY 1003. The record is silent on his family and on how a man of twenty came to the office. It names only three priests of Tonnga in three and a half centuries; he was the last, succeeding Batdeden, who had died the year before. The [[mov:21|Kunnelmu]] faith held that truth was kept by an ordained priesthood and passed down in order. Whether Batdeden passed it to him, we cannot say.

## A temple under a usurper

In the [[evt:5351|Fourth Tonnga–Tungarvikhrim War]] (AY 962–965) King Tyenkutnakin's armies crushed and annexed a city-state that never fielded more than 173 men against them. In AY 969 Tyenkutnakin died, and [[per:5701|Nuetterten]], of another house, seized the throne. Nguttun was thirty-one. The new king courted the gods in bronze: a plaque of AY 979, from the same dig, says he "built this temple for Winba, his lady", the goddess of the earth (PAL·IN·03530). Nguttun kept his records at the temple of Duemino, the storm god, "lord of the oracle".

## The darkened year

His earlier tablet (PAL·RE·00020), written at forty-three, is dated "in the 13th year of Nuetterten", AY 981:

> Snow fell in the month of harvest. The vines did not ripen. The old men had never seen its like. The birds [...] in the spring.

Mount Ashni, inside the empire, had erupted that year; eighteen of the twenty-one districts the record lists as touched by it were Tonnga's. The record's climate series puts the 980s about four-tenths of a degree colder than the 970s, and from AY 983 drought lay on forty-six of the empire's districts for twelve years. Another priest, in AY 748, wrote of an earlier eruption that "Travellers spoke of a mountain that burned in the far country" (PAL·RE·00010). Nguttun names no mountain and blames no god or king. His faith taught that prosperity was a sign of divine favour, and two years after the usurper's gift to the earth goddess the earth had failed. The accusation was there to be made. He wrote down what the old men said instead.

That year the [[evt:5534|Tonnga–Yida War]] began. Nuetterten died in 982, and his son Nuemin I fell at Nivi in 983, where 8,765 men of Tonnga met 14,759 and lost 2,302 to the enemy's 381. Defeat at Zhiyiyzhikhti in 984 brought an indemnity, and a boy of twelve, Nuemin II, reigned under his mother Nomumut, regent until 987.

## Tablet 23

In AY 1002 Nguttun signed tablet 23 of the series "When the Moon", eighteen omens of the kings of former days (PAL·OM·00053), "Written according to its original and checked by Nguttun, the diviner, for Nuemin, great king of Tonnga". Each pairs a sign in the heavens with a past event.

"Original" should not be read literally. Batdeden's tablet 24 (PAL·OM·00052, AY 931) tells much the same history under other signs; it gives a sun darkened at noon in the month of Tyenmom to an eruption in the days of Queen Odet. Nguttun moved Odet's eruption to the month of Duemi and gave Tyenmom to the year he had lived through:

> If the sun is darkened at noon in the month of Tyenmom and stars are seen: ash will fall upon the fields. It is the omen of the year when the mountain burned and the sun was dark, in the days of Nuetterten.

No original held that line. The man who had recorded the snow without comment now set it among the omens of kings, for later diviners to read. His is the earliest surviving omen tablet to number that eruption; diviners in Tomözel, Tallin and Waga followed.

The next omen reads: "If the sun rises red as blood: a servant will seize the throne of his lord. It is the omen of Nuetterten, who seized the throne of Tyenkutnakin." It was written for Nuetterten's grandson. Courage, or convention? Batdeden had set Dyeyshamtumin's seizure of the throne ("one who is not the heir will take the throne") on a tablet made for Dyeyshamtumin himself. Two omens foretell that "a young prince will rule, and another will hold his seal", for a king who came to the throne at twelve under his mother's regency. Whether he read them so, nothing tells.

The omissions speak too. There is no omen for Nivi, where the king's father died, nor for the [[evt:5680|Second Tonnga–Birnadu War]], then in its fifth year. Nuemin II had lost twice near Virnadhu with the larger army, and in AY 1002 another army, 4,605 against 2,516, was beaten near Jerathnu. Yet omen 17 recalls a general of King Budonin who "routed the army of Birnado near Deratno": Jerathnu again, in AY 848, against an older kingdom of that name. Whether it was checked before or after that defeat, it offered the king a precedent the present denied him.

## The end of an empire

Nguttun died in AY 1003 at sixty-five, after forty-five years in office, the longest service of the three priests the record names. That year the Tonnga Empire broke into three kingdoms; which came first, the record does not say. At the end its treasury held 94,520 kg of silver, against 159,301 at the end of the 970s.

Apart from Nuetterten's own plaque, Nguttun's tablets are the only surviving writings that name the usurper: a king remembered by a priest who first dated by his reign and later called him a servant. Of Nguttun we have no face, no family, no prayer. We have a habit of mind. He wrote down plainly what the old men had never seen, and in old age made sure the diviners after him would know it when it came again.
