*To the Honourable the Wardens and Brethren of the Exchange at Mbilu, from the King. To be read at the opening of the Exchange and not otherwise published.*

Gentlemen,

You have addressed to us a petition setting forth the miseries of trade in this present year, and asking what remedy the Crown intends. We have read it twice, and we answer it with our own hand, not as your sovereign only, but as one of your number; for you know that we hold, as our predecessors held, the largest share in the royal trading houses of [[pol:1674|Mbilu]], and that what has befallen you has befallen us. If you are ruined, gentlemen, you are ruined in good company. The King's warehouses on the river stand as full and as idle as yours. The King's ships lie at the same quays. The King's bills are refused at the same counters. We do not say this to excite your pity, which we do not want, but so that you will believe us when we say that we understand the case.

Let us reason upon it as men of business, and not as frightened men.

The first cause is not of our making, nor of yours. It is [[evt:45548|the drought]], which began eight-and-thirty years ago and has never truly lifted. Every year the harvest of the interior is a little less, and the farmer who once bought cloth and iron of our merchants now buys bread, or nothing. A market is only a crowd of buyers; where the buyers are gone, there is no market, though every shop stand open.

The second cause is the long disorder of our neighbours, which has been the work of forty years. When [[evt:45567|Dadthifè fell to pieces]], its roads went with it. [[pol:1924|Shimbayi]], which rose from its ruins, was itself [[evt:45642|swallowed by Dagafu]] within eight years; [[pol:1911|Khandage]] was [[evt:45728|taken by Fuvu]]. Each of these changes of master raised a new line of toll-houses and a new set of officers to be bribed, and the trade that went from [[set:136|Mbilu]] to the southern coast, which in our grandfather's time passed three frontiers, now passes none that it can pass cheaply. [[pol:1892|Dagafu]] and [[pol:1894|Fuvu]] suffer from this as we do, and their merchants have written to ours in the same complaining terms.

The third cause you have not named in your petition, from delicacy, and we will name it for you. It is [[evt:45875|the conquest of Unzhinlathu]]. We did not begin that war; it was concluded in the last year of our late predecessor's reign, and we came to the throne to pay for it. The victories at [[evt:45868|Dabithifu]] and at [[evt:45873|Unzhinlathu]] were honourable to our arms and costly to our treasury. The province is ours. But a province that has been besieged and conquered does not buy, and the merchants of [[pol:1893|Unzhinlathu]], who were your best customers, are now your poorest subjects.

What then shall be done? We propose four things, and we ask your counsel upon each.

First, that the debts of the Exchange to the Crown be deferred for two years without interest, and the debts of the Crown to the Exchange likewise.

Second, that the inland tolls of the Crown be halved upon grain and cloth for the same term, so that what trade remains may move.

Third, that the [[cur:926|dathu]] be maintained at its present weight. We have been advised to lighten it, and we refuse. A merchant may forgive a prince for being poor; he will never forgive him for being a cheat.

Fourth, that the preachers of the [[mov:24|Uthungala]] be asked to stop telling the people from the pulpit that the One has withdrawn His favour from Mbilu because our purses are empty. Prosperity may be a sign of favour, but adversity is not a sentence, and the judgement is at the end of the line, not in the middle of it. We will not have a bad year read as a verdict.

We do not know how long [[evt:45919|this contraction]] will last. No one does, and you should distrust any man who tells you otherwise. We know only that the Crown and the Exchange will come through it together or not at all.

Given at our palace in Mbilu, 24 Livi 4675 of the Uthungala.

**[[per:44931|Nangzhinange]]**, King
