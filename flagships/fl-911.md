In AY 2500, in the city of Izimue, a man named [[per:21184|Nitllu Himim]] stood before a crowd that had buried, as he put it, "more sons in five years than in the generation before them." His city, the small kingdom of Ihamiw, had lost battle after battle and signed away provinces and tribute. His sermon survives (PAL·SC·00127, "Sermon of Nitllu Himim in the Year of the Three Defeats"), and at its centre is a sentence that could stand over this whole chapter:

> Courage did not fail us. Numbers did not always fail us. Something else failed us.

The priests told the city that the gods were angry and asked for more bulls. Nitllu Himim told it that the gods were only the stewards of a single master who could not be bribed, and that a city would be judged by its justice, not its granaries. Those who believed him called themselves [[mov:72|Ishiu]], and the [[evt:21328|Founding of the Ishiu Faith]] is a fitting first event for our period. Between AY 2500 and 2750 armies won their wars and their states fell anyway, coins grew lighter while bread grew dearer, and people in cities that had never heard of one another concluded that the many gods had a master.

It was not an age of decline. The people living under states numbered 81.3 million in AY 2500, 87.1 million in 2620 and 104.3 million in 2740; in these years the total passed a hundred million for the first time. The climate was kinder than in the last two chapters, with a mean anomaly of −0.05 °C, almost exactly the long-run mean. The number of states rose from 30 to 46 and fell back to 41, and that swing began with a generation of collapse.

## The cold generation (AY 2517–2560)

Between 2530 and 2560 six empires broke apart, among them three of the great powers that the previous chapter left standing.

[[pol:756|Ana]] went first. In 2517 [[per:21244|Ue'mamamshe]] seized the throne from [[per:20730|Tlamfen Yueva II]], son of the usurper of the previous chapter; in 2521 the province of Niz rose in revolt. The record names both among the causes of the [[evt:21524|Collapse of the Ana State]] in 2530, only ten years after Ana had reached 9.1 million people and 323 districts. The pieces were large: a new [[pol:922|Ana]] at the old capital, which would last until AY 3086, [[pol:923|Movosh]], and [[pol:924|Yiotl]], whose first king, [[per:21368|Ipil Foyatl I]], reigned for thirty-seven years.

Yennga's fall is better documented, and stranger. Its queen, [[per:20911|Natdatenoth]], was past seventy when she followed her army into Tonnga in the [[evt:21424|Holy War of Yennga against Vami]], and her letter from the camp (PAL·LT·01667, "Letter of Natdatenoth to the Elders of Yennga from the Camp at Anavoro") is accurate where it boasts: nearly fifteen thousand enemy dead at Diviminnge in 2521 (the record gives 14,564), nearly ninety thousand men at Anavoro the next year (89,217). On her own losses she says nothing: "Of the count of our losses I will speak to the elders in person, not on parchment that passes through many hands." She had reason. Yennga lost 10,310 men at Anavoro to win a field on which the enemy lost half as many.

It was her successor, [[per:21230|Valotholon II]], who sealed the Treaty of Viririnu in 2530 (PAL·TR·01453). On paper it was magnificent: 31 districts, a yearly tribute of 542,970 Ashfa for 38 years, protection for the shrines of Zhenro and toll-free markets. The price was 95,429 Yennga dead against 48,965 of [[pol:876|Vami]], and three battles lost on the way; at Nuwkennga in 2523 alone, 30,985 men fell out of 82,019. Under [[per:21391|Omanen]] Yennga won a second war, against Tomözel, and in 2543 took 54 more districts and a tribute for 37 years. By 2550 it had 10.3 million people and 347 districts, the largest it ever was, and the record's measure of its internal order had fallen to nothing. The [[evt:21632|Secession of Zhieyeyzhekhte]], raised in 2541 by the claimant Lokolofath, lists among its causes the very battles lost in the holy war. In 2551 came the [[evt:21719|Collapse of the Yennga State]], into six kingdoms, among them [[pol:938|Vieneynga]] and [[pol:939|Rieney]].

The same pattern repeated itself elsewhere. [[pol:866|Yide]] beat [[pol:834|Tivinnu]] in the [[evt:21373|Yide–Tivinnu War]] and in 2528 took 24 districts, a town and an indemnity of 50,300. Its king Shuyalun I set up a stele claiming 66,400 enemy dead at Ometh (PAL·IN·01574). The record gives 8,680, an eightfold boast. Shuyalun fell in battle against Tomözel in 2541, and Yide paid an indemnity in its turn. Then the [[evt:21601|Famine of AY 2543 in Mana]] killed or drove off 574,951 of Yide's people, 22 per cent of the region. Tivinnu, the loser, broke up in 2553; Yide, the winner, in 2554.

Tomözel completes the set. [[per:21220|Ola]], remembered as the Victorious, ruled it for forty-eight years and did beat Yide. But she lost to Yennga in 2543, after a single field at Viririnu had cost 17,189 men and the life of her commander. A sack in 2542 and civil wars in 2544 and 2551 followed, and the [[evt:21837|Collapse of the Tomözel State]] came in 2560. Ola outlived it by four years.

It is tempting to blame the weather. The 2530s and 2550s were the coldest decades of the period, at about −0.44 °C, with eruptions of Punimongu in 2537 and Aneyo in 2553. But the record lists cold among the causes of none of these collapses, and it traces even the Mana famine to war: to the battle of Tivintukhe, fought in 2538. The empires of the cold generation were not frozen to death. They were bled.

## Light coins and dear bread

War had to be paid for, and the easiest way to pay was to lighten the coin. The best-documented case is the If of Ihamiw, Nitllu Himim's city. Struck in 2511 at 13.9 grams of silver, it was cut to 10.833 grams in 2528 and to 7.638 in 2544, after a victorious war against Toyinmiwa. A dealer in cloth and oil protested to the elders that year (PAL·LT·01731, "A Merchant's Remonstrance on the Light If"). He had weighed old coin against new before witnesses: "of every ten parts of silver in the coin of our fathers, the coin of our sons holds scarcely seven." His verdict is still the best short account of debasement we have: "The treasury has not paid its debts; it has merely made all of us pay them, by stealth, and in proportion to our honesty." He begged that this be "the last time, and not the first of many." It was not. The If fell again, to 6.739 grams in 2672 and 5.945 in 2688.

The neighbours did the same. Toyinmiwa's O'a went from 4.7 to 3.961 grams in 2559, and the Ihal of the shrunken kingdom of Tivinnu from 8.6 to 5.663 grams in 2581. Two years later a grain dealer of Tivinnu's lower quay complained that a measure "once went for a coin and a third, it now goes for nearly two" (PAL·LT·01732). Across the world's markets a measure of grain cost on average 13.7 grams of silver in the 2500s and 16.4 in the 2540s and 2550s. Local disasters were worse: in the decade from 2600 grain at Izimue cost about 55 grams the measure. A dialogue set in that market (PAL·SC·00128, "On Favour, or the Dialogue at the Grain Market") has its hero remark that the price "stands at seven if the measure". Seven coins of 7.638 grams make just over 53 grams: the philosopher and the price record agree.

Money also spread. Seventy-six new coinages and weight standards appeared, [[pol:826|Puvu]] invented coinage for itself in 2533, and 161 merchant houses were founded. The customs tariff of Pivithie of 2590 (PAL·TF·00017) taxed grain at one part in forty "that bread be cheap" and spices at three parts in ten. And in Nilu in 2538 a pupil named Naniji copied out a model letter, and the teacher wrote beneath it: "Write it again, ten times" (PAL·SX·00354).

## One master over the gods

Ishiu was the first of eight faiths founded in these years, and its founders' texts nearly all begin with a lost battle. [[per:21786|O'e Shimen]], who founded [[mov:73|Povi]] in 2560, had as a young man helped carry home the dead of Fyinan, where "our general fell, and more than ten thousand of our men with him" (PAL·LT·01695). [[per:22024|Shoe Muea]], who founded [[mov:76|Yenshe]] at Myizinithie in 2589, wrote to her queen of two defeats at Ivu and a civil war at her door, and asked: "is it reasonable to go on paying a debt to gods who never send a receipt?" (PAL·LT·01696). In 2602 Kithilianwu founded [[mov:77|Ashahatl]] in the grain market of Izimue, on the day news came of a second defeat at Ashmath. In 2604 Simanieme, a priest of Tomözel, founded the Weitlegi faith after defeats at Fyinan; his Confession (PAL·SC·00129) has the sharpest line of the movement: "A heaven divided into portions is a heaven at war with itself."

The teachings differ: Ishiu and Ashahatl keep ordained priests and await a final judgement; Povi and Yenshe have no priests and trust the inner light, and Povi sees all things turning on a wheel. But all four say the same central thing: there is one divine power, and the old gods are its servants, "as a steward serves the master of a house." All four say, too, that the teaching is for every people, the enemy included. We should be careful about cause: most cities lost battles without founding a religion, and the record ties none of these foundings to a particular defeat. What the texts show is how defeat came to be explained, no longer as the anger of gods short of sacrifice but as a failure of justice that no sacrifice could buy off. Ia (2625), I'ashakh (2694) and Kebali (2735) followed, and systematic philosophy appeared independently in Vakha in 2570 and in Tsiy in 2624.

None of this swept the map. In 2620 the largest shares of land still belonged to older traditions: Mie'to'ieshio with 21 per cent, Jinru 14, Uthungala 10, La'im 7. But a faith could outlast every state: the Treaty of Akhtse of 2727 (PAL·TR·01594) was sworn "in the name of I'ishiu", a faith born in chapter 9, and dated by its era.

## Plague, drought and two good tools

The worst killer of the age, the Silent Cough, broke out at Ndulamlol in 2578, reached 21 cities and killed about 417,000 people. The record counts it among the causes of four political disasters: the collapse of Nambe in 2581, the collapse of Kabave in 2595, a secession in 2610 and, in 2612, the [[evt:22494|Collapse of the Puvu State]]. Puvu had been a conqueror. [[per:21046|Navuvage]] reigned for fifty-nine years, and in the [[evt:21745|Eleventh Pyivi Trade War]] his son [[per:21502|Nakathash II]] took nineteen districts and two towns from [[pol:829|Tabüthif]]; the son's stele for the battle of Hil claims 58,600 enemy dead (PAL·IN·01592), where the record gives 10,050. In 2610 Puvu reached its height, 10.2 million people and 336 districts, with its internal order again at nothing. Two years later it broke into six, among them [[pol:979|Khambe]], and its last king, [[per:21848|Nakathash III]], lived on for nineteen years. From the ruin of Nambe, meanwhile, rose the [[pol:967|Khishkhis]] Empire in 2581: modest at first, 1.5 million people in 2600, but a name readers will meet again.

Drought did its work more slowly. The [[evt:22122|Onset of the Great Drought in Khishkhih (AY 2580)]] began thirty years of dry seasons, and the record traces to it the famines of 2586 in Khishkhih, which killed or drove off 352,511 people, and in [[reg:11|Tsiey]], which took 227,501. Khishkhih had already suffered the period's worst famine in 2548: 602,788 dead or fled. The [[evt:23490|Onset of the Great Drought in Tsiey (AY 2693)]] began the longest dry spell of the age, 45 years across 348 districts.

Against these stand two inventions. In 2595 Fayatlximam of [[pol:953|Vami]] built the heavy plough, and her lecture before the city's academy (PAL·SP·00008) is a delight. The old scratch-plough failed on the heavy bottomlands, "where the clay clings like a creditor"; hers cut the sod with an iron knife, turned it with a curved board and rode on two wheels behind eight oxen. She left the drawings with the librarian: "A good thing kept secret is only half good." In the 2590s and 2600s a measure of grain at Vami cost about 8.3 grams of silver, little more than half the world average. Vami, invaded again and again in this age, gave it its most useful tool. In 2651 Niloshfish, a master of works in Yiotl, introduced arch and aqueduct engineering. "The arch is a city," he told his academy (PAL·SP·00009): "every stone is held by the others and holds them in turn." Grain at Thimisitsiy, above 25 grams in the 2620s, cost under 12 in the 2650s, but the record does not connect the arches to the price, and neither shall we.

## Tallen: the largest, and the last to learn (AY 2548–2675)

[[pol:777|Tallen]], ruled from [[set:0|Taulin]], grew largest by feeding on the ruins of others. After [[per:21516|Jeyshapavbokhen]], called the Usurper, took the throne in 2548, Tallen spent sixty years preying on the heirs of Yennga. It conquered [[pol:937|Diebegovebenga]] in 2564, beat Tobejenga twice and Rieney once, and conquered Tuennga in 2608. The Tobejenga annals (PAL·AN·00439) record the raids and treaties but only two of the kingdom's five battles. Under [[per:21986|Shenkoth]], who reigned for thirty-six years, Tallen came to hold 11 per cent of the world's land, nearly twice as much as any rival.

It paid strangely for its size. When Zhienro rose in 2633, the rebels never fielded more than 39 men. Tallen sent up to 166,346 against them, won all three fights and lost 41,929 men; the rebels lost 31. Whatever killed those soldiers, it was not the enemy.

The [[evt:23001|Second Tallen–Rieney War]] was worse. At Riniy in 2651, 156,463 Tallen men attacked 24,641 and lost 67,840, against 1,298 of Rieney's. The new king, Balsheyononen II, wrote home from the camp (PAL·LT·01666) to blame the marshes and the crush of his own ranks, "the ranks behind pushed upon the ranks in front", and to scold the rumour-mongers: "do not believe the criers who go about the city counting our dead by the hundred thousand. Grief is a poor arithmetician, and fear a worse one." The criers were nearer the truth than the king. Tallen won the war, and by the Treaty of Riniy in 2664 took eight districts, a town and a tribute for 36 years, at a cost of 155,095 dead against Rieney's 17,306. The king's rock relief at Yishdhagshal claims 3,900 enemy dead (PAL·IN·01688); the record gives 1,221.

In 2670 Tallen reached 17.2 million people and 329 districts, the largest state of the age. Its measure of internal order had fallen from 0.57 in 2490 to 0.06. In 2675 it collapsed into six, and the record names the battle of Riniy and the revolt of Zhienro as causes. One heir, the [[pol:1004|Yeda]] Empire, kept Taulin as its capital; another was [[pol:1006|Peme]].

## Heirs, and what the stones remember (AY 2675–2750)

Yeda, under [[per:23109|Balsheykolvan I]] and [[per:23400|Balsheykolvan II]], called the Old, who reigned for forty-four years, grew from 67 districts and 5.0 million people in 2680 to 174 districts and 10.7 million in 2740, and conquered Yiennga in 2749. [[pol:887|Turamtu]], under the long reigns of [[per:22617|Lunuerninath]] and [[per:23226|Litathen]], both called the Old, conquered Nivanga in 2685. In the [[evt:23823|Holy War of Turamtu against Vami]] it took 44 districts and three towns by the Treaty of Bami in 2721. But the Yellow Flux broke out in the city of [[set:16|Turamtu]] itself in 2692 and killed some 397,000 people, and the record lists it among the causes of a secession in 2717 and a revolt in 2736. Turamtu peaked at 9.1 million people in 2740 with barely twenty years left. In Yiotl, [[per:23457|Khisha]] reigned for forty-three years before [[per:23952|Mashe Yueve I]] seized the throne in 2743.

[[pol:823|Tangath]], ruled for fifty-three years by [[per:21763|Nabul]], shows how thin our knowledge can be. In the [[evt:23123|Fourth Dyimbi Trade War]] of 2661–2676 it fought Tabüthif to a stalemate, losing 67,152 men to Tabüthif's 27,078. Almost everything we read of that war comes from the attacker. The Tabüthif chronicle of Namli (PAL·CH·04231) puts the Tangath host at Dyithinkulu at 171,204 men, where the record gives 58,422, and passes over four of the ten engagements. The Tangath account (PAL·CH·04232) is lost but for one line, quoted 146 years later in another work. Tangath collapsed in 2690, and its own version of its last war went with it.

That is the shape of the whole record. Of 743 documents that survive from the period, 423 are inscriptions, many of them victory stones, which overstate the enemy dead three-, six- and eightfold. Court annals keep what courts wished remembered: the annalist of Vami mentions two of the twelve battles his kingdom fought (PAL·AN·00448), the annalist of Dviminnga three of twenty-two (PAL·AN·00440). Losers' chronicles perished with their states. What corrects the stones is humbler: merchants' letters, a teacher's note, a plough-maker's lecture, the sermons of prophets of defeat.

By 2740 there were 104 million people under 41 states, and their languages had kept moving: 86 sound changes are dated to the period, among them the voicing of consonants between vowels in Tasemem around 2571. Natdatenoth spoke of a wheel that turns toward those who keep their duty, and Balsheyononen II told his council, "What turns, turns again." Niloshfish offered another image, the arch, in which no stone stands alone. The next chapter belongs to the states that tested his image: Yeda at Taulin, Yiotl at Thimisitsiy and the young Khishkhis, the three great powers of AY 2750 to 3000.
