title: Sound Enough for a Queen
---
The stone rang everywhere but at the foot.

He went over it the way he had gone over every block he had bought in forty years of cutting querns, with the little bronze hammer that had been his father's, knocking his way down the face in rows as if he were sowing it. It was diorite, black and white together like salt spilled on a hearth, and it had come down out of the hills on rollers behind a team of oxen, and nearly all of it answered him the way a sound stone answers: a clean high note that went on a little after the hammer had left it.

At the foot, a hand's breadth above the line where it would go into the ground, the note died.

He knocked again. There was nothing to see. No seam, no rust-coloured thread, nothing a buyer's eye would catch; only a sound, small and dry, like a fly shut in a jar. A finger's width higher: the fly. Lower: the fly. He followed it across the stone with his ear almost on the face, the way his father had taught him to follow a hair through a quern before you put a day's work into it, and it ran from edge to edge.

In his trade a stone with a hair in it was a stone you did not sell. A quern with a hair would grind sweet for a year and then split under a woman's hands on a cold morning with the bread half made, and she would remember who had sold it to her. He had broken good-looking blocks to gravel rather than be remembered that way. On the lower river they said his stones were like his word, and he had liked hearing it.

He sat back on his heels and looked across the flats at the walls of [[set:83|Khiea]].

They stood brown above the green line of the river, with the morning smoke of the town going straight up behind them, and the camp of [[pol:994|Tomözel]] lay round them like a dog round a bowl. His wife was in there, and his daughter. They had gone in through the gate with the goat on the morning the horsemen were seen on the river road. He had stayed with his tools, reckoning that an old man with a hammer was not worth killing and the tools were worth stealing. He had been right about both.

This year's battle in [[evt:24627|the queen's war]] had been fought in his barley. He had watched it from his roof: the queen's general, Ipil Luesha, with more men than the old man had ever seen in one place, and the men of [[pol:1066|Khiea]] under Wewetlo, fewer by far; then the dust; then the men of Khiea running back toward the walls through the trampled stalks while the horsemen rode along beside them like boys beside a flock. In the evening the men of Tomözel counted the dead in the barley, because a queen likes to know. Eight hundred and ten of Khiea's. Eighty-nine of their own. The next morning soldiers went through the village looking at hands. His had stone-dust in every crease.

The young scribe who had kept the count told him the numbers on his first night in the camp, at the fire, the way one man tells another the price of something, and the old man had not been able to put them down since.

"Is it sound, uncle?"

The scribe had come up behind him with a potsherd in one hand and a brush in the other, red ochre to the wrist. He was perhaps twenty, and he had the long flat vowels of the queen's country, which were the vowels the old man's grandmother had used.

The old man got up, which took him a moment, and knocked the top of the stone. It sang.

"Listen to it," he said.

The scribe listened, and smiled. "Sound enough for a queen," he said, and went back to the shade of the ox-cart. The old man stood with the hammer hanging from his hand and understood that he had just told the first lie of his working life without saying one word that was not true.

* * *

For days after that he ground the face. River sand, a jar of water, a flat rubbing-stone pushed in long strokes until his shoulders burned. The grey skin of the block came away as slurry, and the black and white came up under it the way fish come up through water that is clearing. When the face was wet the hair showed: a thread a little darker than the stone, running from edge to edge at the foot, and it was always the last thing to dry. He learned how long it took. When he saw the scribe coming, he stopped wetting the stone.

The scribe practised in the shade. He painted signs on sherds, studied them, wiped them out with his thumb and painted them again, and when he was pleased with a line he read it under his breath, the way a child counts.

"What will it say?" the old man asked him one afternoon.

The scribe held up the sherd as if the old man could read it, then remembered, and read it to him.

"Fieowfoyatl, queen of Tomözel, defeated Khiea in Khiea. She killed 2,700 soldiers. She erected this stele for Etlie, her lord."

The rubbing-stone stopped. "How many?"

"Two thousand seven hundred."

"You told me eight hundred and ten."

"That was the count." The scribe did not look ashamed. He looked like a man explaining weights to a child. "This is the stone. Last year's said six thousand five hundred, after [[evt:24648|the other battle]], and nobody went out with a basket to check. Nobody reads a stone to find out how many, uncle. They read it to find out who won."

"The priests say justice in dealings."

"They also say moderation in all things." The scribe smiled. "She could have said ten thousand."

The old man went back to grinding. He had heard the priests of the [[mov:25|Mietseyeshio]] all his life, in the market and at the shrine by the ford. Many gods share the world, each with a place and a due. Deal justly. Want little. The true words are kept in the priests' mouths and handed down from one to the next in order, so that nothing is lost. He had never heard them say anything about stones.

"And after that?" he said. "After her lord."

"After that, what every stone says." The scribe did not need the sherd for it. "May Etlie destroy the name of the enemy."

"Which name?"

The scribe looked across the flats at the walls and did not answer, because there was nothing to answer.

"Where does that go?"

"At the foot. It's the last line. It always is."

The old man nodded, and wet his stone, and went on in long strokes, and the slurry ran down over the place where the hair was and filled it and hid it.

"You talk like us," the scribe said another day. "Not like them."

"I was born in Tomözel."

The scribe looked round at the village, the canal, the walls. "Here?"

"Here. The empire came when I was seven. Then a king from somewhere else, for four years. Then the empire again. Then, four summers ago, the city [[evt:24606|made itself its own master]], and we were Khiea's." He tipped water on the stone. "I have never been further than the market. It's you people who move."

* * *

At dusk the camp prayed. He had not known what to expect from the men of Tomözel, and what he got was his mother's words. They poured a little water on the ground for Etlie, who sits over everything, and a little for the god of the river, and a little for the goddess of the earth, each in their place and each their due, and they said it all in the order his mother had said it, with the same small bow at the end. On the wall across the flats, he supposed, his wife was saying the same words to the same god at the same hour. He wondered how Etlie told the two prayers apart, and then whether he bothered.

Then the lamps came out along the walls, one and then many, and the old man sat with his back against the stone and tried to work out which was the street where his wife's sister lived. There was less smoke from the town every day. People inside were burning less, or had less to cook. He did not let himself think about it for long.

Most evenings the scribe brought him half his bread. He was a decent boy. He had a mother in the queen's country who would be counting the days, and a way of asking the old man's opinion of a sign, as though a quern-cutter might know. It would have been easier if he had been worse.

At night the old man weighed it. He had weighed barley against stones all his life, and he was good at it; he could tell a short measure by the way a sack sat on a man's shoulder. In one pan he put two thousand seven hundred where there had been eight hundred and ten, cut in a stone that would stand longer than anyone's grandchildren. In the other he put one hair, which he had not made, and had only not mentioned.

It seemed to him that the beam came out about level. Moderation in all things.

* * *

The gate opened on a morning near the end of summer.

He saw it from the stone. First the horns; then the gate itself standing open, strange to see after so long, like a mouth; then men coming out on foot, slowly, the way men walk when they have decided not to be afraid. In the middle of them was [[per:24412|Esie Tlie]], who had made the city a state four summers ago and was a heavy man now, near fifty, walking without hurry toward the awning the men of Tomözel had put up on the flats. From the other side came the queen. The old man had expected her to be tall. She was a woman past fifty, a few years older than the man she was waiting for, in a plain cloak, and she stood under the awning with her hands folded the way a woman waits at her door for a neighbour who owes her.

They swore by Etlie. He was too far off to hear the words, but he saw them both lift their hands in the same way at the same moment, and he knew which words they were.

Afterwards a herald of Tomözel stood out in the sun and called [[evt:24663|the terms of the peace]] in a voice meant for the walls. Silver, a great weight of it, to Tomözel. Fifteen years without war. And five districts of the city's land, named one by one; and one of the names was the name of the old man's village.

So he was going back to [[pol:994|Tomözel]] after all, without moving.

The scribe, standing beside him, glanced at him and away again, as if he had heard a man's private business through a wall.

It was evening before his wife and daughter came out through the gate with the goat and found him by the stone. His wife was thinner. She took his hands and turned them over and looked at the palms, which the sand had worn shiny, and then past him at the stone, at its polished face and the first red signs the scribe had laid out across the top.

"Whose stone?"

"The queen's."

She put her palm flat on it, the way she had put it on every stone he had brought home in twenty-six years of marriage, and took it away again.

"Is it good stone?"

He looked across at the scribe, who was packing his brushes into a box thirty paces off, and who looked up and lifted a hand to him. The old man lifted his own.

"No," he said.

His wife asked him nothing else. In all those years she had never heard him say that about a stone he had worked, and she did not need it said twice. She took the goat's rope from their daughter and turned toward home, which was the queen's now, and he took up his hammer and went after her. Behind them the girl looked back once across the flats at the walls, and said the city's name aloud, the way you say the name of someone you have been afraid for, and then ran to catch them up.

* * *

The stone was set up in the spring, by the river road below the city, where the barley had been. A cutter from the queen's country had finished it over the winter with his iron chisels, line by line down the face, and the last line at the foot, the one every stone says.

The old man passed it on his way to the market at Khiea with two querns on the donkey. He stopped, and looked about him, and knelt in the road as if to see to the girth. Then he put his knuckle to the foot of the stone, under the last line, where the god was asked to destroy a name, and knocked twice, the way you knock at a neighbour's door.

Inside the diorite the hair answered him, small and dry, the way a hair always answers.

Not yet, it said. Not yet.
