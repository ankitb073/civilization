title: Burn His Barley
---
The new cook brought his own salt. He wore it round his neck in a leather bag the size of a fist, and when he came into the kitchen behind two spearmen, blinking at the smoke, the first thing he did was close his hand over it, the way a man in a crowded market closes his hand over his silver.

Nalandi saw that, and made up her mind about him, and went back to her pot.

She had cooked in this house for twenty-two years, thirteen of them for a man and nine for a king, and it had been the same stomach all the way through. [[per:13564|Natsunge]] liked his barley cooked long in the black pot, so long that it caught, and the brown crust at the bottom lifted with the paddle and brought to him in a bowl of its own. The rest he ate because it was there. The crust he ate because he wanted it. Four days ago he had sent the bowl back full. Three days ago he had died in his bed, fifty-one years old, of nothing the priests could name and nothing she could tempt, and the priests of [[dei:73|Tithu]], from the temple he had built for their god, had carried him out to the tombs.

She had put the barley on this morning all the same. Her hands had done it before she was properly awake.

"They said this was the kitchen," the new cook said.

"They said right."

He looked round at it: the low beams black with smoke, the strings of onions and dried mushrooms, the bread oven, the two hearths, the kitchen boy scouring a pot that had been clean since yesterday so that he could stay and listen. "It's bigger than ours," he said, and he sounded sorry about it. "I'm Nadigi. I cook for [[per:13693|Navyimde]]." He stopped, and then said it the new way, carefully, as if the word might crack in his hands. "For the king."

"The king is in the tombs."

"The other king."

* * *

For three days the men of the court had sat in the hall over the matter of the seat. The law of [[pol:599|Kane]] gave it to a king's eldest son, and Natsunge had had two. The elder had died at six, of a fever, before Kane had a king of its own. The younger had died at two, in the winter after [[evt:13557|Kane stood up]] from the falling empire and Natsunge made himself its king. Their mother had followed them five years ago. The law had nothing to say about a king whose sons had gone into the tombs ahead of him, so the court had talked, and the talk had gone round and round like a dog that cannot settle; and this morning, before the frost was off the courtyard stones, Navyimde had driven in at the gate with men behind him, and walked into the hall, and [[evt:13651|sat down in the seat]], and the talking had stopped.

"Who opened the gate for him?" Nalandi had asked the kitchen boy, who had run out to see and run back with his face red from the cold.

The boy had thought about it honestly. "Who didn't?"

Now the men who had stood in the courtyard since before light were in the hall, stamping, and they wanted something hot. Nadigi said so, standing between her hearths with his hands open.

"There is a king three days in the tombs," she said.

"I know. He knows. They're cold all the same."

She looked at him properly. He was a broad, soft-spoken man with burn scars up both forearms, as every cook has, and a way of standing well back from another person's fire, which every good cook has also. She disliked him a little less, and resented it.

"That one," she said, and pointed with her chin at the second hearth. "It smokes when the wind's in the north."

"Is the wind in the north?"

"It's [[set:144|Kane]]," said Nalandi. "The wind's always in the north."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon without much talk. He had brought a haunch of mutton and a sack of his own onions, as though the onions of this house might be disloyal, and he salted from the bag at his neck, grey coarse salt, a pinch at a time, rubbing it between finger and thumb first as if to make sure it was still his. Her salt was white and lived in a jar by the oven. Neither offered the other any.

At the bottom of her pot the barley caught. She heard it go, the bubbling sound thinning and a dry tick coming up under it, and she let it.

"That's burning," Nadigi said.

"It's meant to."

He came and looked, and she showed him: how long, how hot, how you worked the paddle under the crust so that it came up in a sheet and not in crumbs. "Every night," she said. "Nine years he was a king, and every night that bowl went up to the hall before anything else did."

Nadigi was quiet for a while. Then he said, "Isn't there something about burnt barley? The curse. The one from Tagath."

There was, and he knew it, and so did the kitchen boy, whose scouring had slowed to nothing. In the year [[evt:13555|the empire came apart]], the last king of Tagath had laid a curse on the governors who stood up against him and had it written in clay, and the words had travelled the roads faster than any clay could. By the next harvest there was no one in Kane who could not say them. "Whoever broke his oath to the king, may Tithu break his roof. May [[dei:74|Binge]] burn his barley. May his sons sit in the dust and his name be forgotten."

"In the first year of the kingdom," Nalandi said, "I told this whole kitchen: I burn his barley every night. I save Binge the trouble."

Nadigi laughed before he could stop himself, and the kitchen boy laughed because Nadigi had.

"They laughed then too," she said. "Then the little one took a cough in the winter. After that we didn't say the rest of it."

Nadigi stopped laughing. After a moment he took the paddle out of her hand, gently, as one cook takes a thing from another, and lifted the crust for her in one piece, and laid it in the old bowl, and did not ask whom it was for.

* * *

The girl came in when the lamps were lit.

She came round the door sideways, the way children come into rooms that are not theirs yet, in a cloak that had been cut down from a grown woman's and not cut down enough. Nadigi's whole face changed. "Here she is," he said. "Here's the one who's hungry." He had a heel of bread in her hand before she had asked for it, and she ate it standing with her back to the warm bricks of the oven, looking at everything.

"His girl," Nadigi told Nalandi, low. "Navande. Her mother went last year."

The name meant harvest. She was seven, with her father's heavy brows, and she frowned at the kitchen as though she had been sent down to see that it was run properly.

What she found to frown at in the end was the bowl.

Nalandi set it every night on the hearthstone, at the edge of the warm: a little of everything that had been cooked that day, for whoever had gone ahead. Her mother had done it, and her mother's mother. The priests of [[mov:11|Kyif]] taught that all things turn on a great wheel and that what ends will begin again; and if the dead were coming round, her mother used to say, they ought to find something on the way. The priests also taught that no one owns the truth, and Nalandi had taken them at their word. She did not know whether she believed in the bowl. That was no reason to stop.

Tonight it held a slice of Nadigi's mutton, a spoon of barley and a curl of the brown crust.

"Who's that for?"

"The king," said Nalandi.

The girl glanced at the door to the hall, where her father was.

"The other one," said Nalandi. "And his boys. And whoever else."

The girl considered the bowl for a long time. Then she broke a piece off her bread and looked at Nalandi, not at Nadigi, because she had already worked out whose kitchen it was. "Can my mother have some?"

Nadigi turned to the wall and became very busy with a hook.

"It's a bowl," said Nalandi. Her voice did something she had not given it leave to do. "There's always room."

The girl put the bread in beside the crust and pressed it down with one finger, so that it would stay.

* * *

Navyimde came for her himself, late, when the hall had gone quiet and she was asleep on the flour sacks with her knees drawn up. He was forty-four, a heavy man going grey at the temples, and he moved through the kitchen like someone who had been on his feet since the middle of the night and had stopped expecting to sit down. The kitchen boy dropped a lid. Nobody picked it up.

He did not go to the girl at once. He stood over the hearthstone, looking down at the bread pressed in among the barley.

"She did that," Nalandi said, since someone had to. "For her mother."

He nodded slowly, and for a while said nothing. Then he said, "Natatha. Her name meant stranger. She used to say it was a poor name to give a girl you meant to keep." He looked up. "Has anyone eaten in this house today?"

"Cooks eat standing."

"So do kings, today." He looked at the black pot. "What's left in that?"

"The bottom," said Nalandi. "Burnt."

"Give me that."

She did not move at once. Nine years that crust had gone up to the hall in its own bowl. Then she scraped what was left into a plain one and gave it to him, and he ate it standing, with his fingers, the way a boy eats at a kitchen door.

"That's good," he said, surprised.

"It's burnt barley."

"I know what they say about burnt barley." He did not look at her when he said it, which was how she knew he had been thinking about it all day. "May Tithu break his roof. May Binge burn his barley. That was for the governors who broke their oath to Tagath. [[per:13564|Natsunge]] was one of them. I wasn't. I only took a seat a dead man had finished with, and nobody has written a curse for that yet."

"Give them time," said Nalandi.

He laughed once, through his nose, and behind him Nadigi let out his breath.

"His sons," Navyimde said. "That part came true."

"The little one went the next winter."

He turned the empty bowl in his hands. Then he looked across at the flour sacks, where his daughter slept with the cut-down cloak over her shoulders, frowning in her sleep like an old woman who has a great deal to see to and means to live long enough to see to all of it.

"Well," he said. "I haven't any sons."

He picked her up. She did not wake; children of seven sleep like stones in warm kitchens. At the door he stopped, with her heavy on his shoulder.

"Nadigi says your hearth smokes."

"Only the one I gave him."

"Keep the other one, then," said the king, and went out with his daughter into the cold of the courtyard.

* * *

In the morning the bowl on the hearthstone was empty, as it always was by morning. Mice have to eat too, her mother used to say, and who is to say who sent them.

Nadigi's leather bag hung flat on the peg by the door. When Nalandi lifted the lid of her salt jar, the salt in it was grey on top.

She stood looking at it for a while. Then she stirred it in with one finger, and put the barley on, and let it catch.
