title: Somebody Upstream

Her father said that water had a pulse, like the soft place under a goat's jaw, and that you found it the same way: with the flat of your hand, and without hurry.

So the girl lay on her stomach on the bank of the Seventh Board ditch with her arm in to the elbow and her palm turned flat against the current, and waited for it to push. In other years it pushed like a kid at the udder. You could feel the whole canal in it, her father said, up to the place where it came out of [[dei:85|Niwi]]'s side, and past that the river, and past the river the far hills where the rain fell. This morning it leaned against her hand like something asleep. A water-beetle rowed across her wrist and she let it.

"Well?" said her father.

She thought about it the way he had taught her, which was to think about what the water said and not about what you wanted it to say.

"It's going," she said. "It isn't going much."

He crouched and put his own hand in, the big hand with the white scar across the knuckles, and kept it there longer than she had. Then he dried it on his thigh, stood, and looked up the ditch toward the tamarisks and the flat roofs of the next village. She saw his jaw work, as though he had bitten on a stone in his bread.

"Sixth Board," he said.

* * *

There were seven boards on the canal and seven villages, and every village knew its place on the water as it knew its place at a feast. First Board took the water first because it lay nearest the river; Seventh Board took it last because it lay at the end, where the ditch gave out into reeds and then into sand. Each village had its days, and each keeper went by the name of his board. On its days its keeper drew his board up out of its slot in the brickwork and turned the water into his own fields; on the other days he slid the board home, packed the cracks with wet clay, and let the water go on down. That was the law of the canal, and like all law it rested on the certainty that everyone above you was a thief.

It was the hundred and fortieth year of [[pol:949|Tsiy]], and a good time to be of Tsiy. Four years before, in the [[evt:23487|Conquest of Kivuna]], the king, [[per:23200|Millusha]], had joined a whole kingdom's land to his own, and the men who had marched with him had come home with stories and blankets and, some of them, iron knives. Barley sold in [[set:76|Nimith]] at about a [[cur:542|Hazhni]] the measure, as it had for as long as the girl had been alive. The priests of [[mov:13|Unhatlmum]] said what they had always said, that the gods' regard for a people could be read as plainly as its granaries, and nobody on the canal had seen a reason to argue. The river had risen every year of her life. This year it had been late, and then thin.

No one had walked the water in her lifetime. The old men said it was the right of the lowest keeper, in a bad year, to walk up the canal and look into every slot, and the right of every keeper above him to curse him while he did it. Her father's grandfather had walked it once, and come home with a broken head and an extra day of water, which the village had kept ever since.

Her mother put bread and onions in a cloth and filled a skin from the house jar, because she would not have him drinking out of other people's ditches. The girl carried it, because she was ten and nobody thought to stop her.

* * *

Sixth Board was sitting on his board when they came up.

He was a small bald man with a voice like a goose, and he had been her father's enemy for twenty years, since the two of them had fought in this very ditch with mattocks over a single night's water. Sixth Board still had the dent above his eyebrow and her father the scar across his knuckles; and every year since, at the feast of [[dei:86|Shu]], each had sent the other a jar of beer, and neither had ever admitted it was good.

"I knew it," said Sixth Board. "I said to my wife, he'll want to look in my slot like a man buying a goat looks in its mouth."

"Get off it, then."

"Look at the clay first." Sixth Board slid down, grunting, and jabbed a finger at the seam. "That's my last day's clay. Put your thumb in it. Go on."

Her father knelt and put his thumb in the clay. It was damp and took the print cleanly, and there was no seep on the far side. Behind the board, in Sixth Board's own channel, the water lay brown and shallow over cracked mud, like the last of the beer in a jar.

"Fifth Board," said Sixth Board kindly, as one explains a thing to a child. "Everybody knows Fifth Board's sister married into Fourth Board, and the two of them have been sharing out an extra night since the last flood."

"I didn't know that."

"You never know anything. That's why you're at the bottom." He took his stick from the tamarisk. "I'm coming. I'm not having you go up there and tell lies about me."

* * *

So there were three of them, and then five, because Fifth Board came, saying it was Fourth, and brought his brother; and at Fourth Board seven, because Fourth Board came, saying it was Third, and brought his son.

The son had been to the war. He had stood in the line at the [[evt:23475|Battle of Lishimti]], where the king commanded in person, and at the [[evt:23485|Battle of Kivunge]] the year after, and he had come home with an iron knife and a way of walking at the front of any group of men as if someone had told him to.

"I saw him," he said, and nobody needed telling who. "At Lishimti. Close as that palm. [[dei:88|Mahtu]] is his lady; he says so himself. A goddess doesn't hand a man Kivuna one year and drink his river the next."

"Then who's drinking it?" said Fifth Board's brother.

"The herders. They bring the goats down at the head and the bank goes like bread in water. My father says."

"I never said that," said Fourth Board.

"You said it at the feast."

"I said it at the feast," Fourth Board agreed, as though that were a different thing.

At Third Board the clay was honest. Third Board, who was pious, said that it was [[dei:87|Hutlhi]] drawing the water down into the ground to her own house, and then, when they laughed, that it was Second. At Second Board the clay was honest, and Second Board, who was not brave, looked at the eight of them and the two dogs coming up his ditch with mattocks on their shoulders and said quietly that First Board was a woman, after all, and a widow, and who was there to watch her in the night?

The girl had been watching the ditches. Below Sixth Board the water had lain in the channels like something left behind. Above Fourth it moved a little; above Second, enough that she could see the weed lean in it. And the barley stood taller the higher they climbed, as if the whole plain had been tipped toward the river and everything good had run uphill. She said so to her father, and he did not answer, and she understood from his face that he had been seeing it for a long time.

* * *

First Board was a big slow woman sitting in the doorway of the little house of Niwi at the head of the canal, shelling beans into her lap. She looked at them all and said, before anyone had asked her anything, "Come and see."

As they passed the shrine the girl looked in and saw the niche full: a whole loaf, a good one, not a heel; a little bowl of oil; barley ears tied with red thread. Whatever First Board was, she was not stingy with the goddess.

Then they came out of the tamarisks onto the head of the canal, and everyone stopped.

All her life the girl had imagined this place from the other end of the ditch, through the palm of her hand: a great mouth, the river pouring sideways into the canal the way beer pours from a tipped jar. What there was was a sill of fitted stones, worn pale, and in front of it, where the river ought to have been, a long slope of grey mud cracked into plates that curled at their edges like old bread, and then, a long way out, the river. It was brown. It was moving. It lay a full forearm below the lip of the sill, and what reached the canal at all came through a single low gap at the end of the stones, a trickle you could have stopped with your heel.

This was the first year of what the record calls the [[cli:138|Great Drought of Tsiey]]. Nobody on the bank knew that. What they knew was that across the water, on a gravel bar, a herd of goats stood in the shallows with their heads down, and a boy and an old woman leaned on their sticks among them, watching the men of the canal come out of the trees.

Fourth Board's son took a step forward. Then he looked at the goats, which were on the far side of the river, and at the dry lip of the stones, and did not take another.

The old herder woman called across the water. Her speech was a cousin of theirs, half its words their own words bent out of shape, and the girl caught *water* and *year* and nothing else. Then the woman lifted her stick and pointed at the sky, and then at the river, and shrugged, a big shrug that went all the way up to her ears, the way the girl's grandmother shrugged at the price of salt; and the girl understood her perfectly.

"Well," said Sixth Board at last. "There is somebody upstream, after all."

He pointed his stick at the white sky, as the herder had. Fifth Board's brother laughed, a short bark that he swallowed. Nobody else did.

"If it isn't a thief," said Fourth Board's son slowly. He was no longer standing at the front. "If it's [[dei:83|Hath]]. Then what have we done?"

That was the question under all the others, the girl saw. Every one of them had carried it up the canal, and every one had been glad of each new thief along the way to set it down on. If the river had failed because Fifth Board's sister married into Fourth Board, that was a matter for mattocks and old men. If it had failed because the gods had looked at Tsiy and taken back their regard, nobody knew what it was a matter for.

"I've given her a loaf every day of the rise," said First Board. "Every day."

"Nobody's saying you haven't," said the girl's father.

He took the mattock off his shoulder and stepped down onto the sill, and from the sill onto the mud, which held him, cracking. He went to the gap at the end of the stones and swung the mattock into the mud and levered up a plate of it.

"What are you doing?" said Sixth Board.

"The keeper's work. If the river won't come up to the canal, the canal can go down to the river. You can stand there and ask the sky questions."

Sixth Board looked at him. Then he said a word the girl was not supposed to know, and climbed down beside him.

* * *

They dug the rest of the day. They cut a trench from the gap out across the mud toward the river, knee-deep and then thigh-deep, and the girl and First Board carried the spoil up the bank in the bread-cloth and the bean basket. Fourth Board's son dug like a man who had dug before the walls of a city, which he had. Third Board stopped once to pray, and went on digging with his lips still moving.

In the afternoon the herder boy came across the shallows with a skin of sour milk and held it out until Sixth Board took it. Sixth Board drank, made a face, drank again, and passed it on. When it reached the girl it was warm and sharp and tasted of goat, and she drank as much as she dared. The boy stayed. After a while he picked up a flat stone and began to dig with it, on his knees beside her father, and nobody told him not to.

The water came when the shadow of the tamarisks had reached halfway across the mud.

It did not rush. It came up the trench the way a cat comes into a room, a brown tongue of it, stopping and going on, nosing round the stones. It filled the bottom of the trench, a finger's depth, then a hand's, and found the gap in the sill and went through it into the canal: toward First Board's board, and Second's, and on down, the girl thought, all the way to Seventh and the reeds. The men cheered. Fourth Board's son cheered loudest, both arms up, as if they had taken a city. Sixth Board leaned on his mattock with his lips moving, and the girl thought he was praying, like Third Board, until she came close enough to hear that he was saying, very softly and one after another, every name for her father that he knew.

"Well?" said her father.

The girl lay down on the warm stones of the sill, as she did at home, and put her arm into the new water to the elbow, and turned her palm flat against it, and waited. Behind her the men had gone quiet to let her listen, even the herder boy, even the dogs. Up on the bank the house of [[dei:85|Niwi]] stood with its good loaf in it.

"It's going," she said. "It isn't going much."

It was the truth, and her father nodded as though it were what he had walked all that way to hear. The river would tell the same truth to every hand that asked it, at every board on the canal, for forty-five years, until the girl on the stones was older than Sixth Board: that it was moving, and that it was not much.
