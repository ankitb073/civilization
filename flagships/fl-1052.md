title: Four of Them Are Nambale
---
"Four of them are Nambale," the woman said.

Nakadhash had seven bronze pins in her mouth and did not answer. She was kneeling with a hem across her lap, the wrong hem of the wrong robe on the wrong woman, and she had not slept.

"Four of the boys, I mean. There are six boys and a girl." The woman looked down at the top of Nakadhash's head as if it might help. "The eldest I named, and the girl, and the little one. The four in the middle he named, and he only had the one name to give them. You'll have heard."

"No," said Nakadhash, round the pins.

Her name was Nambugu. She was forty-two and had borne seven children, and she carried all of them still: in the hips, in the forearms, in a way of standing with her weight on one foot as if something were about to need lifting. Since a little after the second watch of the night she had been the wife of the king of [[pol:254|Nambe]].

The queen had died at the second watch. Nakadhash had been there, on the floor with her back against the bedframe, listening to [[per:10266|Navande]] breathe in the dark as she had listened forty years before to a small girl with a cough; and at some point she had stopped hearing it, and had gone on listening a while, to be sure. When she came out into the passage with the lamp, the men at the end of it were not men she knew. By the time the gulls started up on the harbour roofs, everyone in the palace knew whose they were.

[[per:10366|Nambale]]'s. She had known him twenty years: a big, slow, careful man about the palace who had never once, in her hearing, said a thing worth carrying home. He knew which doors swelled shut in the wet season. It turned out he had also known which ones would open in the night.

At midday a boy came up from the gate to say that the king would show himself at the temple of [[dei:104|Shilimu]] before the lamps were lit, that his wife would stand beside him, and that the queen's women were to make her fit for it. The queen's women had looked at one another. In the end it was Nakadhash who went, because she was the oldest, and because she could think of no way to refuse that would not get one of the young ones hurt.

The trouble was the girdle. It was the long red one with gold beads knotted into the ends, made to go twice round a narrow woman and tie at the front, and on Nambugu it went once round and a hand's width over.

"Breathe in," said Nakadhash, taking the pins from her mouth. It was the first thing she had said of her own accord.

Nambugu laughed and let all her breath go instead, so that she settled in the middle like a sack set down. "I've carried seven," she said. "There's nothing you can tie round me that will hurt."

Navande had held her breath for the girdle all her life. She had begun at five, the first time she was put into a grown woman's sash for a festival, puffing out her cheeks until her face went dark; and Nakadhash, who was seventeen, had laughed at her and told her it was cloth, not a snake, and would not squeeze unless she let it. Navande held it anyway. She held it at thirty-four, with the court waiting outside the door to see her made queen. She held it at forty, to bury her husband. This last month, when she could hardly sit up, she had still drawn the breath and held it, and Nakadhash had tied a knot so loose it was barely a knot, and neither of them had said a word.

The red girdle would not do, and the robe would not do either. The green with the banded cuffs had begun to whisper at the seams before Nambugu had both arms in it. Nakadhash took it off her again, went to the queen's chest and knelt, and went down through it layer by layer, through forty years of a narrow woman: three greys for mourning, worn soft at the elbows; the white for the temple; the yellow she had been married in; and at the bottom, folded in on itself, the one she had known would be there.

It was a plain robe of dark blue wool with a panel let into each side, the stitches of the panels larger and clumsier than the rest. Two women had sat in the window and put them in, twenty-five years ago: Nakadhash, and the queen's young sister Nagubu, who could not sew and had insisted. Navande had been nineteen and so big with the boy by the end that she laughed at herself in the bronze mirror. The boy was Navavun. He had lived to fourteen. There had been no second child to let it out for.

Nakadhash held it up, and Nambugu saw her face.

"Whose was it?"

Nakadhash told her.

Nambugu put her hands behind her back, like a child told not to touch in the market. "Then no. Find me something else. Pin me into something. I'll stand very still."

"There is nothing else. She was narrow. Everything she owned was narrow but this." Nakadhash shook it out. It smelled of the chest, and under that, faintly, of something she could no longer name. She held it open. "Arms."

Nambugu looked at her a moment longer. Then she put her arms in.

It was not a good fit. The shoulders had been cut for someone else. But it closed, and the panels lay flat over hips that had carried seven, as they had once been put in to lie over hips carrying one. Nakadhash went round her with the pins. Nambugu stood very still, as she had promised, and because she could not move she talked.

"He doesn't know whose son he is," she said. "Did you know that? Nobody ever told him, and the one who could have died when he was small. The first baby I named. When the second came he took it up in both hands and said, Nambale. And the next. And the next. I said, they'll never know which of them is being shouted at. He said a name with nobody before it had better have plenty after."

"Four times."

"I got the girl away from him. By the baby he'd stopped arguing." Nambugu almost smiled. "At home we call them by how tall they are. It works until somebody grows."

Nakadhash set a pin at the shoulder. She was thinking of a name with a great many people before it. Navande, daughter of Nashinzhi, who was the son of Navinu, and so on back, king and queen and king, to the first king of all, who had made the kingdom. She had heard the priests go through it at the accession, ten years ago; it had taken a long time, and the court had shifted on its feet. A name like a road you could walk back along to the very beginning. And no one after it. Not one.

"What do they put over a queen?" Nambugu asked. "On the lintel."

"Her name. Her father's name. That she dwells there. And they ask the goddess to keep her."

"Her father's name." Nambugu looked at the far wall. "He's been thinking about lintels since he was a boy, I think. He's never said so."

Downstairs that morning, over the bread, Nakadhash had already heard it said: that the gods had shown plainly enough where their favour lay. Seven living children on one side. On the other, a woman who had buried a brother, a mother, a son, a father and a husband, and had now lain down herself with nobody left to stand at the door. The [[mov:16|Ninzhe]] taught that prosperity was the sign of the gods' favour, and the kitchens had worked out the rest for themselves. Nakadhash had left without her bread.

Nambugu said, as if she had heard every word of it, "I've fed nine on a servant's share, with grain near two [[cur:143|Shambi]] the measure. I know what favour looks like. It looks like being tired." She turned her head as far as the pins allowed. "You were with her."

"Yes."

"Then you should be with her now. Not pinning me."

"The priests have her. They are washing her. I'm to dress her after." Nakadhash put in the last pin. "Her sister and I."

Nambugu said nothing to that, which Nakadhash, despite herself, thought well of.

At the door she stopped. "How do I walk in it?"

"Small steps on stairs. It's long in front. She went down on her knees in it once on the palace stair and laughed so much they had to carry her up."

* * *

She told herself she went down to the temple for the hem.

The whole lower town of [[set:85|Nambe]] had come up the hill, and the harbour people with the salt still on them, and boys sat along the wall of the little shrine of [[dei:108|Bumbe]] for the view. In the temple court the diviners had their bowls of water out and were pouring oil on them, and whatever the oil did, they kept it to themselves. Nakadhash stood at the back among the porters' wives, where she could see the doorway and the bronze plaque fixed beside it. She could not read it. She did not need to. She had heard it read out on the day it went up, eight years ago, with the queen standing in front of it pretending not to listen: "Navande, queen of Nambe, built this temple for Shilimu, her lady. May Shilimu protect the queen."

The new king came out under it. He was bigger than she remembered, or he was standing differently. His family came behind him: a grown son; a girl with her hair done up tight; a little boy who sat down on the top step and had to be lifted by the back of his tunic; the four in between; and Nambugu in the dark blue, taking small steps.

The priest called on [[dei:104|Shilimu]] to look upon the king, and the crowd gave the name back to him, raggedly and then all together, "[[per:10366|Nambale]]! Nambale!" — and on the temple steps four boys of four different heights turned their heads at once.

Nakadhash laughed. It was out of her before she knew it was there, loud enough that a porter's wife turned to stare, and she put her hand over her mouth and kept it there. On the steps the eldest son, who had been spared the name, reached out and cuffed the nearest of his brothers on the ear.

The hem was right. She had known it would be.

* * *

[[dei:106|Kumbe]] was up over the harbour by the time the priests had done, and the room smelled of the water they had used and of lamp oil. Nagubu was sitting on the floor by the bed with her back against the frame, where Nakadhash had sat the night before. She was forty-three. She had been in that place most of the day, someone said, and nobody had asked her anything.

"They came to tell me," Nagubu said, without getting up, "that what ends will begin again."

"Did they say when?"

"They did not."

They dressed her together: the white for the temple, because it was clean and because Navande had liked it. When Nakadhash went to the chest for the shawl, Nagubu watched her go down through it, and saw what was not at the bottom.

"The blue."

Nakadhash told her.

Nagubu was quiet a long while. Then she said, "We put those panels in crooked."

"You put them in crooked."

"Did it lie flat on her?"

"It lay flat."

"Good," said Nagubu, and got up off the floor at last to lift her sister's shoulders while the girdle went under.

It was the red one with the gold beads. It went twice round [[per:10266|Navande]] and more than a hand over; she had grown thinner at the end. Nakadhash brought the ends to the front and crossed them, and forty years did not let go of her all at once. She heard herself say it, low, as she had said it to a child of five.

"Breathe in."

Navande lay easy under her hands and let it be tied, the way she never once had.

It was Nakadhash who held her breath, all the while she drew the knot, and for some time after, because one of them should.
