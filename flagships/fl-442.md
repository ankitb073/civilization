*[A fair copy on parchment, retained in the royal chancery of Wemo. The original was read aloud to the delegates on the day of their departure.]*

In the name of the One Power, whose servants and whose shadows are all the lesser lights of heaven, and in the year 1363 of the [[mov:69|Kievi]].

[[per:34862|I Lisil]], by the patience of the One king of [[pol:1461|Wemo]], least of the delegates and host of this assembly, to the fathers and brothers of the council now departing from [[set:104|Yethithi]], and to every seeker who reads this after them: grace, light, and a safe road.

Fathers, I write because I am too old to say all this standing, and because a thing written stays when the speaker is gone.

You have seen with your own eyes in what state you found us. You came to a city that had lately had enemies under its walls. You passed the trenches of the [[evt:35375|Fifth Siege of Yiethithi]] on your way to the gate. You have heard the widows in the lower town. Three years ago our host was broken at the [[evt:35338|Battle of Yiethithi]], within sight of these towers; last year again at the [[evt:35361|Battle of Mueaxi]]; and this spring, at the [[evt:35374|Tenth Battle of Lus]], our banners went down for the third time. I was not yet king when the first of these befell; I am king now, and it was my hand that set the seal to the [[evt:35376|Treaty of Yiethithi]], which gave away fields that my fathers held, and silver in [[cur:808|Mimi]] that my people must find somehow.

I tell you this not to be pitied. I tell it so that you understand why Wemo kept its word and opened its hall to you, when wiser kings would have sent word that the hall was full of wounded. The Teaching says: the wise withdraw from wealth and appetite. The One has withdrawn our wealth for us. Very well. We could still give you bread, and a roof, and a fire, and the long table. We did.

And yet the [[evt:35434|Council of Yiethithi]] has failed. I will not soften that word. The delegates of [[pol:1405|Yit]] came, and spoke, and listened, and went away unreconciled. We argued for eleven days. We read the old sayings aloud. We prayed through the night of the new moon. On the twelfth morning their benches were empty.

I have asked myself, as you have all asked yourselves, whose fault this is. I have come to think it is no one's fault, and that this is the hardest part of our faith. We do not keep the truth in a locked box, under the hand of an ordained few, to be handed down like a crown. We hold that each seeker may find the truth by inner light and reason. That is our glory. It is also why no council can command a conscience, and why no king can. I could not command them. I would not have done so if I could.

Therefore, fathers, I ask three things of you on the road.

First, that you speak of the men of Yit with justice and moderation, as the Teaching requires, and not as enemies. The wheel turns. What has ended in this hall may begin again in another.

Second, that you carry the word to all peoples, as we are bidden, but with a lighter hand than we have used here. We have talked much. Perhaps we should listen more.

Third, that you remember Wemo in your prayers, and its people, who fed you in a lean year and asked nothing in return.

I am old, and the winters are long in Yethithi. I may not host another council. If I do not, let it be said that I hosted this one, and that the door was open.

Given at Yethithi under my seal, in the year 1363 of the Kievi, in the first year of my reign.
