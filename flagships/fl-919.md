In AY 4626 a citizen of the port of Dyigini sat down to correct the newspapers. Since the strangers had come, he complained, the presses of [[set:85|Nambi]] had printed "a flood of sheets describing them: that they have the heads of dogs, that they eat no salt, that their ships are drawn by fish." He had drunk with them in the harbour taverns and could report that "their heads are ordinary and that they salt their food more than we do." They came from a great state they called Gawli, which we know as [[pol:1785|Tallin]]. They brought dyed cloth and a bitter leaf steeped in hot water, and they took silver. He ended with a story. He had asked an old captain from Gawli what his people thought when they first saw ships from Khishkhis at Igu, and the captain laughed: "We thought you had the heads of dogs." (PAL·CH·08456, "A True Account of the Strangers from Gawli at Dyigini").

He was writing three years after the first meeting at Igu in 4623, when, as far as the record shows, ships from the two continents of Jümbividhi and Lamshüthi first lay in the same harbour. Two years after he wrote, a Tallin newspaper printed a four-line notice under the heading "THE MARSH COUGH": "The sickness has broken out at Igu. Ports are closing to ships from the stricken cities. Physicians advise the boiling of water." (PAL·NP·00021, "The Tallin Mercury, AY 4628"). The meeting of the two worlds and the worst pestilence of the age began in the same port, five years apart. The rest of this chapter follows from that.

The period from AY 4500 to 4750 was one of ships, presses and paper money, of an epidemic that killed nearly thirty million people, and of the first revolutions in the record. The numbers frame it. In 4500, 240.4 million people lived under states. By 4620 there were 232.7 million, and by 4740 the total had recovered to 268.6 million. The number of states fell from thirty-five to twenty-four. The climate turned cold, with a mean anomaly of 0.35 degrees below the long-run mean, and grain grew steadily dearer.

## The powers at noon

The century opened with the empires of Chapter 18 at their height. [[pol:1785|Tallin]], ruled from [[set:0|Taulin]] by [[per:42887|Pavbukhshifin I]] and later by [[per:43516|Buyuen]], called the Conqueror, grew from 39.4 million people in 4500 to 54.3 million and 430 districts by 4610. [[pol:1670|Dagafu]] under [[per:43042|Nabizhi]] and [[per:43607|Nangandumbe]] reached 46.2 million. [[pol:1710|Binge]] did not last. In the [[evt:43714|Binge–Wemu War]] of 4525 to 4536 Binge attacked the younger empire of [[pol:1769|Wemu]] and lost 754,184 men to Wemu's 378,321. A secession and the lost battles of that war are the recorded causes of the [[evt:44012|Collapse of the Binge State]] in 4553, during the reign of [[per:43180|Vaahavbukhin]], whom the record calls the Last. One of its six heirs was the city of [[pol:1863|Shivange]]. It began as a city-state, became a republic in 4750, and in 4601 was the first place in the record to adopt the joint-stock company.

Dagafu showed how even a winning empire could be pulled apart. In 4580 a province broke away as the [[pol:1873|Nilu]] Empire. For three years it held 15.9 million people, and then it was gone. Khishkhis chose that moment to attack, and lost the [[evt:44316|First Khishkhis–Dagafu War]] of 4582 to 4588. A usurpation in 4600 put Nambili I on the Dagafu throne. Under his successor [[per:44013|Nambili II]] the [[evt:44813|Collapse of the Dagafu State]] came in 4634. Its recorded causes reach back fifty-four years to the Nilu secession. Its heirs included a smaller Kingdom of Dagafu and the [[pol:1894|Fuvu]] Empire, which grew to 44.3 million.

The rising powers lay on the edges. The Kingdom of [[pol:1755|Tsey]], seated at [[set:76|Nimid]] and ruled for sixty-one years by [[per:43372|Esh Mua]], reached 35.6 million by 4740. The Kingdom of [[pol:1656|Ane]] reached 34.6 million. Khishkhis, under [[per:42658|Nabuli]], called the Victorious, and later [[per:43814|Namivambi]], grew from 14.2 million people in 4500 to 28.7 million in 4740. The Kingdom of [[pol:1674|Mbilu]] grew from a small realm of twenty-one districts and 1.2 million people in 4600 into one of 169 districts and 19.2 million.

## Across the western water

Ocean navigation was first adopted at Bige in 4491, at the very end of the last chapter. It was then invented again at Khazhini in 4501 and at Nunnga in 4515. A century later the long voyages had begun. In 4606 Tallin founded a colony at Nivina on the coast of Shithiu, and that same year the elders of the coast sent the governor a petition that deserves to be read in full. "When your ships came into our bay this year," they wrote, "we brought them water and fish, as our fathers did for every stranger driven onto this coast. We did not know that we were welcoming owners." Their grove of the dead had been cut down for the palisade. Their children were being told their fathers' gods would not save them. And they were paid in Tallin's silver nivbe, weighed on the colony's scales, "which give, by our reckoning, a third less than the old weights of this coast." (PAL·LT·01703, "The Humble Complaint of the Elders of Shithyu to the Governor at Nivinga"). The petition survives because Tallin's clerks filed it in their own state archive. Whatever the elders wrote for themselves does not.

Other colonies followed: Fuvu's at Ntulamkhau in 4665, Kankush's at Tathtidh in 4684, Ane's at Myizishie in 4687. The founder of Ntulamkhau, King Nakhumbu of Fuvu, told his settlers to buy any native land they wanted, "set down the price in writing, and pay in good Ngathe", because "a title seized will not outlast the first quarrel." He asked for a register of every birth, death and sickness, "so that our physicians can learn what the air of Nishu does to people born on our side of the water." (PAL·LT·01704). He wrote in 4665. By then everyone knew what the air of the other side could do.

## The Marsh Cough

[[evt:44707|The Marsh Cough]] broke out at Igu in 4628 and ran along the trade routes to fifty-three more cities. By the time it ended, around 4638, it had killed some 29,651,255 people. That is roughly one person in eight of all who lived under states, and in many stricken cities two in five. The record marks it as a virgin-soil epidemic, a disease meeting a population that had never known it. It lists one cause only, the first contact at Igu five years before. Contemporaries were not sure. A form of prayer printed at Taulin in 4640 set out the competing explanations and declined to choose: the physicians blamed "the marsh air", the merchants noted that it "first appeared at Igu within a few years of the First Contact", and the preachers spoke of "a judgement on our sins". The prayer asked the faithful to remember "Tallin, the seat of the Empire, whose streets were silent", and it confessed, unusually, that Tallin had not been sorry to see a neighbouring enemy, Zhivampive, break apart under the same sickness (PAL·SC·00132, "A Form of Prayer for the Abatement of the Marsh Cough"). A clerk at Taulin was briefer: "A sickness came by the ships. The dead were carried out by the cartload … Those who nursed the sick fell sick in turn." (PAL·RE·00201).

The figures for Tallin are terrible. In the 4610s the empire held 54.3 million people. A decade later it held 35.3 million, even though it had gained sixty-six districts in the meantime. The city of Taulin had about 572,000 people in 4600 and 342,000 by 4700. Binge's successor state fell in 4642, and the Marsh Cough stands among the recorded causes of that second [[evt:44902|Collapse of the Binge State]].

The cough was followed by cold. An eruption of Mount Linuvukhnir had already chilled the 4550s, and from 4640 to 4689 and again from 4730 the record marks long cold spells. In the 4650s the world ran more than half a degree below its long-run mean. Droughts came on top of the cold: in Daadüdhih from 4561, in Baan from 4571 and twice more, and the [[evt:44705|Onset of the Great Drought in Mikhukhizhi (AY 4628)]], which began in the plague year and did not lift until 4704. The famine in Yiutl in 4651 took 1,565,797 people. Across the three hundred markets the record follows, the average measure of grain rose from about 10.1 grams of silver in 4500 to 15.1 in 4600 and 18.3 by 4750, almost twice the price at the start of the period.

## Presses, paper money and the first republics

Through these bad decades the tools of the modern world kept arriving. Movable-type printing was first adopted at Ane in 4593, credited to Ipil Nyime, and in time the presses were printing newspapers. Of the 1,605 everyday documents that survive from this period, 323 are newspapers, a kind of source this book has scarcely met before. Experimental science was first adopted in Tsey in 4613. Paper money spread even faster than print. Tsey issued the Ta in 4603, Ane the Withe in 4615, Kankush the Tyigi in 4635, Iith the Il in 4651 and Zhinontunu the Lodhu in 4674. Paper made inflation a slow and steady thing. The Withe was worth six grams of silver when it was issued and about 3.6 grams in 4749. The Il fell from thirteen grams to about nine over the same span. Every new state still struck coins as well, thirty-six new coinages and standards in the period, and merchants founded 210 houses.

The printing press, the paper note and the unpaid army together brought something new. The [[evt:44674|Tsey Revolution of AY 4617]] is the first revolution in the record. Its causes include two old defeats, of 4569 and 4571, and one fresh one: at Tunavükhini in 4615 a Tsey army lost 143,628 men and its general. The deposed king, Mezhash II, later wrote one of the most disarming memoirs in the archive. "I was, I maintain, the first revolutionary in Tsiey," he begins. "Netleniem made the revolution with his speeches; I made it with my mistakes." Of the crowds in the capital he writes: "They did not, as some pamphlets claim, break the palace gates; the guards opened them, which is worse." He even admits that the republic's regiments had been raised in his reign and "paid in the Ta notes that I first caused to be printed." (PAL·CH·08451, "Memoir of the Revolution, by the King It Unmade").

Others followed. The [[evt:45019|Iith Revolution of AY 4654]] grew out of the sacks of its capital in 4606 and 4607 and a humiliating treaty. Its leader, Lili, laid down the presidential seal when his term ended and admitted in his memoir that bread stood at "nearly two il the measure" and that the people blamed him (PAL·CH·08452). The [[evt:45287|Ane Revolution of AY 4681]] has three famines as its recorded causes, in 4638, 4651 and 4673. They are the same three that its leader, the councillor Isuilyian, listed in his journal on the night he carried the resolution upstairs to the king. "Three times in one life the granaries of the realm were found empty," he wrote, "and three times the Crown ordered prayers and taxes in that order." Bread was "above two withe the measure", which is about 9.4 grams of silver and close to the record's price for Ane that decade. He also wrote down a question he had put to the king about a costly victory: "whether that was a victory or an invoice." (PAL·DI·00010).

The [[evt:45759|Mbilu Revolution of AY 4732]] needs a word of explanation. It took place not in the great Kingdom of Mbilu but in a younger state of the same name, founded in 4680 at the harbour city of Mbilu in Ng'jinaguh, a land of eighteen districts and about 1.6 million people. Its president, Nashièvive, told the crowd that in 4688 the crown had answered an army of two hundred thousand with "five-and-twenty men". The record gives 25 men against 204,692 at Khambizhinu, so the figure is exact. "Keep the archive doors open," he said. "Read what is in them." (PAL·PM·00059). In 4734 Bene followed (PAL·PM·00060). Between 4617 and 4734 five kingdoms became republics, and Tsey and Ane went on to become federations.

Faith split too. The [[mov:157|Bangekhulu]] reform of [[per:43655|Navande]] began at Mbilu in 4560; the Tuedinatlshi, Duthladul, Ishyidig and Vamtsikulu schisms followed; and in 4704 Yinush founded the Shilesh reform movement at Ishwyithie. Wemu had made the [[evt:43564|Conversion of Wemu to Ndingilathul]] in 4504. Even so, Veshethmekh still held more than a quarter of the land in 4620. The Lidhil and Vishithdanki schools of philosophy were founded, and new languages emerged: Shyem in about 4599 and Yithotlim in about 4699.

## Cold years, steam and the widening wars

The wars of this period grew larger at every turn. Tsey went to war against Tomzel in the name of religion in 4649. The [[evt:44944|Holy War of Tsey against Tomzel]] ended in a Tsey victory in 4656, and eight years later the [[evt:45115|Collapse of the Tomzel State]] removed one of the old powers from the map. In the [[evt:45716|21st Pyivi Trade War]] of 4727 to 4739, Khishkhis, by then ruled by successors of [[per:44457|Nang'ji]], who had reigned forty years, defeated Lamlivje.

In 4728 Tallin faced Biniynge again. It was the second time in four centuries that the city had risen against whoever ruled at Taulin. The [[evt:45721|Tallin Civil War]] opened in 4729 with a rebel force of 39 men against 648,708, the same mismatch we saw in Chapter 18. The rebels did win one real battle, and at their height they held about 240 districts. Tallin's population in the 4720s fell to 37.0 million. In 4738 the rebellion was crushed. Tallin lost 418,436 men putting it down, and the rebels 124,623. The fifty-three-year reign of [[per:44559|Tushen]], called the Victorious, had ended in 4726, and the empire went into its last century with its treasury falling.

The steam engine was first adopted at Faamni in 4741. Within nine years the fields outside Faamni were the scene of the largest battles yet fought. The [[evt:45860|First Wemu–Ane War]] began in 4745, when Wemu attacked Ane. A year later Ane was also at war with Khishkhis over colonies, in the [[evt:45867|Ane Colonial War in Daadüdhih]]. Fighting two great powers at once, Ane won the first war and drew the second. At Faamni in 4750, 1,501,908 men of Wemu met 1,500,928 of Ane and lost 426,115. Before peace came in 4753 the war had cost the two sides more than 2.5 million men. In Daadüdhih, Khishkhis lost 381,569 men in a single battle at Nzhivi in 4752. The next chapter begins in the same years and must explain what those defeats did to Nambi itself.

Our sources for this period are very uneven. Tallin's state archive has kept the complaints of the colonised, the prayers for the plague dead and its own newspapers, and so it tells this story more fully than anyone else. The coast of Shithiu, the cities stricken by the Marsh Cough and the towns burned in twenty-one sacks that destroyed their archives speak mostly through others. What survives from the period is mainly the paper of the victors and the presses of the capitals. The paper they printed on would carry the next two and a half centuries further than any earlier age had travelled.
