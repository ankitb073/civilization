title: Heirs and Fragments: The Age of Bronze Reconsidered

The Age of Bronze, conventionally dated AY 0–1866, is usually taught as an age of founders: the first kings, walls and empires. I propose a different emphasis. If we ask what the record of the age most often describes, the answer is not foundation but fission. The great bronze states rarely simply died; they broke, and the pieces carried their names forward. My evidence tonight is eighteen great collapses between AY 481 and 1295. (The record knows of more than two hundred collapses in the age as a whole, most of them of small polities.)

## The shape of a collapse

Take the first, the [[evt:1589|Collapse of the Yida State]] in AY 481. The [[pol:25|Yida]] empire had stood since AY 234. In a single year it gave way to six successors, among them the [[pol:61|Tallin]] empire and the kingdom of [[pol:62|Buneynga]]. Tallin would last almost three and a half centuries and Buneynga two; the other four were gone before AY 680.

That shape recurs with striking regularity: a rump that keeps or revives the old name, one or two durable heirs, and a fringe of short-lived states. The [[evt:2822|Collapse of the Talenonoy State]] in AY 652 produced the [[pol:119|Tonnga]] empire beside a [[pol:120|Nonenga]] that lasted some sixty years. The [[evt:4130|Collapse of the Nelo State]] in AY 833 left a [[pol:177|Nelo]] beside a new [[pol:178|Puvu]]; the [[evt:4213|Collapse of the Egu State]] in AY 840 left an [[pol:184|Egu]] beside [[pol:185|Tomözel]]. Names outlived the states that bore them. A [[pol:165|Yida]] rose again from the ruins of Navnga in AY 819, and [[pol:169|Bäni]], born in the same collapse, would itself break apart in the [[evt:6685|Collapse of the Bäni State]] of AY 1111.

This is the first thing I would have students remember. At least half of my eighteen collapses struck states that had themselves been born in an earlier collapse on the list: both Tallin empires, the second Nelo, the second Yida, Bäni, Ana, Khyeka, Mikhuthitsu and Yinnga. The bronze state was very often an heir, and it died as heirs do, quarrelling over the estate.

## Why they broke

The record links each collapse to the events that preceded it, which lets us weigh causes rather than guess at them. War comes first by a wide margin: by my count more than nine in ten of the age's collapses follow on recent battles. But war rarely acted alone. The most destructive conjunction is the early ninth century, when four of my eighteen fell within twenty-one years: the [[evt:4038|Collapse of the Navnga State]] (AY 819), the [[evt:4077|Collapse of the Tallin State]] (AY 825), Nelo (833) and Egu (840). [[pol:40|Navnga]] and Tallin had fought each other through the 760s, and the record lists the same pestilence of AY 765 among the causes of both their falls. Navnga carried, besides, a disputed succession in AY 810. Two great powers exhausted each other, were struck by the same plague, and broke within six years.

Disputed successions are the second great solvent. Usurpation crises stand among the causes of the falls of Yida (in AY 476), Navnga, Nelo, Egu and, in AY 1054, [[pol:204|Khie'a]], whose [[evt:6358|Collapse of the Khie'a State]] in AY 1077 also followed a great migration into its lands. Plague appears; famine almost never does. Of all the age's collapses the record attributes only two, in any part, to famine. Whatever else the bronze kingdoms feared, they did not, on this evidence, fall because the harvest failed. A record built from wars and reigns may see hunger less well than armies, but later ages, in which famine does appear among the causes, suggest the silence is real.

## What defined the age

If the age has a single character, then, it is this: states made by inheritance and unmade by war and succession. An archaeologist would expect to meet that pattern in the ground as continuity of settlement under changing names; the record lets us state it with numbers. One caution, however. A list of collapses describes how states ended, not how people lived. For the farmers of [[pol:93|Mii]], whose empire broke in the [[evt:5441|Collapse of the Mii State]] of AY 973 into an [[pol:229|Ana]] and a [[pol:230|Miezanitia]], the year a dynasty changed may have mattered less than the year the river failed to rise. That lecture the record of collapses cannot give.
