In AY 4698, near the town of Zhivampive, [[per:44559|Tushen]] of [[pol:1785|Tallin]] took the field in person for the only time in his reign. The official history, printed eleven years later, describes a neat and soldierly affair. The enemy general waited with 7,909 men, and the king "turned their flank at noon, and the discipline of our troops told". Some 6,400 of the enemy fell and 127 of the king's men. The record gives a different picture. At the [[evt:45440|Battle of Zhivampive (AY 4698)]] Tushen commanded 324,225 men against 4,621. He won, and 316 of his soldiers died, more than twice the history's figure, against 1,782 of the enemy. Tradition calls him the Victorious. No battle of his fifty-three-year reign was lost, and some ten thousand of his soldiers died in them. Both facts belong in any honest account of him.

## The usurper's son

He was born in 4646, a Jego subject of the great Tallin empire. His family was not royal. In 4671 his father, [[per:44533|Tukhulven]], a man with no recorded parents, seized the throne, and he is remembered as the Usurper. He held it for two years. The [[evt:45229|Accession of Tushen]] in 4673 made his son of twenty-seven ruler of about forty million people, governed from Taulin.

This was no longer an age of stelae and chariots. Tallin's armies brought up guns, its histories were printed, and chartered trading companies were being founded in its cities: the Linidhe Company in 4666, the Duvay in 4682, the Tulne in 4691, and in 4703 the Shikhshil Bank. Tushen's first wars were fought, in name at least, for trade, and they were fought on a scale that earlier ages could not have imagined. His standing army numbered about half a million men.

## The trade wars

Within three years of his accession Tallin was fighting two wars at once over the region of Tunnge. The [[evt:45240|25th Tunnge Trade War]] was Tushen's own, against the ancient city of [[pol:1916|Turamtu]], now a small city-state under [[per:44500|Nathbitafbafin I]]. In 4678 a Tallin army of more than 285,000 men fought about a thousand at Turamtu and won. It lost 102 men. The Turamtu account of the war, written in 4692, blames "a night of rain that bogged the guns" for its own defeat and triples the Tallin dead. The truth was smaller. The [[evt:45281|Treaty of Turamtu (AY 4680)]] gave Tallin two districts and trading rights. Tallin had lost 166 soldiers to win them, and Turamtu 769.

The [[evt:45241|26th Tunnge Trade War]] was started by the city-state of [[pol:1877|Kunnga]] under its queen [[per:44569|Jiwkhelith]], later called the Old, who would reign for forty-three years. In four battles her armies of three to seven thousand men faced Tallin forces of 150,000 to more than 250,000. In 4680 she commanded one herself. Tallin won every battle and lost 1,324 men, against 3,359 of Kunnga's. The [[evt:45295|Treaty of Jerathnu (AY 4681)]] made Kunnga a vassal and obliged it to pay an indemnity. The Kunnga chronicler later blamed "the blunders of the general staff" for his side's final defeat and claimed 1,413 enemy dead in it. The true figure for that battle, 357, was a quarter of that.

## Nivi and Zhivampive

The [[evt:45336|23rd Baan Trade War]] of 4687–4691 against the kingdom of [[pol:1903|Nivi]] was larger still. In 4690 Tallin put more than 320,000 men into a single battle. The war cost Tallin some 1,200 dead and Nivi about 5,700. Its treaty survives on sealed parchment. It is an instrument of a new kind, in the language of chanceries rather than temples. The [[evt:45404|Treaty of Tinlamvukhnir (AY 4691)]] proclaims "a firm, sincere and perpetual peace". Nivi renounced twelve districts, including Tinlamvukhnir and Yirantudhu, "as delimited in the annexed map". It paid an indemnity, placed its foreign relations under Tallin's protection, and promised Tallin's goods and shipping "treatment no less favourable than that accorded to any other nation". It is signed simply "For Tallin: Tushen" and, for Nivi, by its new king [[per:44751|Runguwnilin]], remembered as the Convert.

The private record in these years is grim. His three daughters died in childhood: one in her first year in 4684, one at seven in 4690, and the last at seven in 4693. His mother died the same year.

In 4697 he began the [[evt:45433|Tallin–Diviguvivinge War]] against the city-state of [[pol:1898|Diviguvivinge]], and at Zhivampive he commanded himself. The enemy general, [[per:44801|Dimintun]], was killed. Zhivampive was besieged, and then sacked for the eighth time in its history. The [[evt:45450|Conquest of Diviguvivinge]] in 4699 ended the reign of [[per:44680|Budhdudhen III]], called the Last, and added eleven districts. The defeated city's chronicler wrote only two entries, the invasion and the fall, and closed with a question: "Who will now remember the kings of old?"

## The last war and the empty throne

His queen died in 4702 and his brother in 4712. In 4714, at sixty-eight, he began the [[evt:45584|Tallin–Tushi War]], his largest conquest. Armies of 190,000 to 240,000 fought a real enemy this time, up to forty thousand strong. Tallin lost some 6,700 men, and Tushi nearly 22,000. The [[evt:45619|Treaty of Taalinnuy (AY 4717)]] took thirty-six districts and three towns from [[per:44859|Nislifaya II]], along with a large indemnity. By then the empire held some 567 districts and 47 million people, more than at any earlier point in his reign.

Tushen died a natural death in 4726, aged eighty, with no child and no brother living. The [[evt:45709|Usurpation of Nathbipimtin]] followed at once, and the record marks it as a crisis. [[per:45051|Nathbipimtin]], a man of forty-eight without recorded parents, ended the house of Tukhulven after fifty-five years. In 4728 Biniynge seceded and civil war followed. By the end of the decade the empire had lost two-fifths of its districts. It recovered them, and Tallin endured for another century.

The epithet is accurate in a narrow sense. Every battle fought in Tushen's name was won, and his empire grew by some sixty districts and six million people. The cost in his own soldiers' lives was some ten thousand, two-thirds of it in the war against Tushi; the city-states he crushed could field at most some seven thousand men. The printed histories of his court cut those deaths by half or more, and the enemy chroniclers multiply them. Only the record gives a figure that can be checked, and it is the figure that should be remembered.
