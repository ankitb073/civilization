title: A Collapse Without a Battle: Drought, Disease and the Throne in Nelo

Many of the states that broke apart in the eighth and ninth centuries carry a lost battle somewhere in their list of causes. [[pol:80|Nelo]] does not. The documentary consensus explains the [[evt:4130|Collapse of the Nelo State]] in AY 833 by two usurpations, a pestilence and a secession, and when the causes of that secession are traced in turn, the chain ends in a drought. This article follows the chain to its start and then asks which link actually broke.

## The chain

The earliest link is climatic. The 740s were the coldest decade the age had yet seen, the decade of the eruption of Mount Fumbundu in 746 and of the Great Drought of AY 746–759. The Famine of AY 746 in Khanji killed some 57,800 people, about one in eleven in the stricken province. The record counts that famine among the causes of the Kagene secession of 803, fifty-seven years later. As an archaeologist I am wary of chains this long. Granaries, abandoned fields and shallow burials can show that a famine happened, but they cannot show what a famine meant to a rebel two generations on. What can be said is that the famine is the first crisis in the sequence, and that Nelo met it under a ruler, Nakathash, who had already reigned for four decades.

The second link is political. In 770 Nagovo I seized the throne from Queen Navambe, daughter of Nakathash, an event the record lists as the [[evt:3752|Usurpation of Nagovo I]]. Nelo passed its crown by seniority within the royal house, a rule that produces many plausible claimants and few uncontested ones. Nagovo's line nonetheless held. His son, Nagovo II the Wise, reigned from 782 to 816, the longest reign since Nakathash's.

The third link is disease. [[evt:3876|The White Pestilence]] broke out at Kavu in 798 and reached thirteen towns, killing about 460,000 people across Nelo and the Luthe Empire, roughly a quarter of those where it struck. Five years later came the [[evt:3937|Secession of Kagene]], when Nambovame the Fair raised a rival empire at Ajü. Nelo won the war that followed, and by 808 Kagene had been re-annexed.

The last link is the [[evt:4026|Usurpation of Nashenjè]] in 816, the year Nagovo II died. Nashenjè ruled until the collapse and outlived the empire by seventeen years.

## The map

Here the material evidence is clearer than the chain. Two of the six successor states sit exactly where the record's causes point. The Kingdom of [[pol:179|Kavu]] (833–859) was seated at Kavu, where the White Pestilence began. The [[pol:180|Kagüne]] Empire (833–941) was seated at Ajü, the capital of the suppressed secession, and its name is, as far as I can judge, the same name in a later spelling. The core continued as [[pol:177|Nelo]] at Nilu until 997. The others, the [[pol:178|Puvu]] Empire at Kabaf, which outlasted them all until 1307, the City-State of [[pol:181|Kangushe]] and the Kingdom of [[pol:182|Yade]], had no recorded grievance of this kind.

## Weighing the links

Climate began the sequence but cannot have ended it: Nelo grew for most of the century after the drought, from about 4.9 million people in AY 730 to about 6.5 million by 830, and its territory expanded by more than half. The economy weakened late. The treasury fell by about a sixth between 820 and the collapse, years that included a holy war against Tatudüfe, which Nelo won at the price of some 500 dead. The pestilence and the secession wounded the empire in two specific places, and those places became two of its successors.

The decisive link, on this reading, is the last. The secession of 803 was put down while Nagovo II held an uncontested throne. The same provinces left for good under a ruler who had taken his throne by force, the founder of the third ruling house in less than fifty years. The record's measure of internal order stood at nothing at the end of the 810s and again at the end of the 820s, its lowest value. A collapse without a battle is still a collapse of authority: drought and disease prepared the ground, and the usurpation of 816 removed the one claim that might have held it.
