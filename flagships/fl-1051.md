title: Mind My Teeth
---
To shave a man who has no teeth, you must lend him your thumb.

Welmimash learned it the winter the last of the king's teeth went, twenty years ago, when the old man's cheeks fell in like a tent with the pole pulled out and the razor began to skip in the hollows. There was no other way. You rinsed your hand in the warm water, you said "Lord," and you put your thumb inside his mouth against the cheek and pressed the skin out round and tight from within, and then the blade went down over it as clean as over a boy's jaw. The gums were soft. The inside of the cheek was warm and faintly sour with beer. And every morning, when the thumb went in, [[per:6865|Wa'al'ash]] said around it, "Mind my teeth."

It came out *mime my teef*. Every morning for twenty years, and every morning Welmimash answered, "I'll mind them, lord," and the king's shoulders shook under the cloth, and the barber lifted the blade away and waited for them to be still. It was funny every time because it was the same. That was the whole of the joke: that there was another morning, and it was the same.

He was a small man by the end, the king of [[pol:156|Uem'o]], eighty-seven years old and in the fortieth year of his reign, and he had outlasted nearly everything. He had outlasted the old queen, whose throne he took when she died, though Uem'o chose its kings and always had; there had been no choosing that year, and he had been king so long since that only the very old remembered there had ever been a question. He had outlasted his wife by forty years. He had outlasted his daughter and all three of his sons. He had outlasted the [[evt:6791|war with Tantethi]], nine years of it, one great day at [[evt:6824|Yiethithi]] and in the end nothing gained by anyone; and when the [[evt:6891|peace]] was sworn last year, ten years without war, he had sat in the chair with his chin up and said, "Ten. Somebody else can have the eleventh."

The men of [[set:127|Lus]] let their beards grow for their dead; you could read a street's griefs in its chins. The king never did. A king's face, he said, belonged to the city. So on the morning after every burying Welmimash came to the chair as on any other morning, and the king said *mime my teef*, and they went on.

Only once was it different. The last son was Im'amiuf, a heavy grey man of forty-seven, and on the morning after they carried him out the king sat down and let the cloth be tied and the oil be rubbed in, and when the thumb went into his cheek he said nothing at all. Welmimash drew the blade from the ear to the corner of the mouth. Then the king spoke, thickly, past the thumb.

"I had a boy who was five."

Welmimash held still.

"Before any of this. Before I was anything. Wa'aliuf. Wise fire. I named him for myself, the way young men do." A breath through the nose. "He'd be fifty-eight."

The blade waited at the jaw.

"You think the new one will lie down on top of the old one and press it flat," the king said. "It doesn't. They lie side by side. Like children in one bed."

Then: "Go on." And Welmimash went on, and in twenty years that was the only time the king spoke of any of his dead under the razor, and the barber never told it to anyone, not even to his wife.

* * *

The knock came in the black middle of the night.

Alsish was up before him with the lamp. In the corner their granddaughter Ta'oshlu slept with her fist shut round the reed she had spent the afternoon shaving the yard dog with, while the dog lay with his eyes closed and his chin on his paws and bore it like a king.

The man in the doorway was no one Welmimash knew. "The king," he said, and nothing more, and Welmimash took down his case.

They went down through the harbour quarter, past the boats knocking at their ropes and the smell of fish-guts and wet rope, past the little lamp that always burned in [[dei:6|Nili]]'s niche above the water-steps, and up the long street to the palace. At the gate the spearmen were not the gate-men he knew. One of them opened the case on the ground and took out the razor and turned it in the torchlight, a leaf of bronze ground so thin it had gone pale along the edge, and looked at Welmimash for a long time before he put it back.

In the inner room the women had already washed the king. A priest of [[mov:1|Ya'cuam]] sat by the bed and sang, low, the old song of the wheel, that all things turn and what ends will begin again, and the old man lay under a clean cloth, smaller than he had been in life, his mouth fallen in.

"He is shown at noon," the priest said, between verses. "His face must be fit to be seen."

Welmimash warmed water at the brazier. He oiled the cheeks. He began, as always, at the left ear. At the hollow he rinsed his hand and said "Lord," and put his thumb inside.

The cheek was cool.

He had shaved the dead before; every barber had. He had not thought this would be different. He found that he was waiting, the blade lifted, for something that was not going to come, and that the priest had stopped singing to watch him wait.

"I'll mind them, lord," Welmimash said, and went on.

When it was finished, and the face lay as smooth as a stone from the river, he wiped the bronze and asked the oldest of the women, quietly, when the choosing would be.

She looked at him as though he were simple. "There's a man in the hall already."

"Who?"

"[[per:7203|I'a Igwi]]. Since before this one was cold." She tucked the cloth under the king's chin. "With those." She did not need to point at the spearmen.

Welmimash knew the name, as the whole harbour knew it. I'a Igwi was the man who had walked behind his daughter when they carried her out this year, a girl of five, and behind another daughter five years before that, a girl of three, and who had not let a blade near his face since. You could see that beard from across a street. People stepped out of its way.

* * *

They came for him again before the fish market opened, and brought him to a small room off the hall, where a man of thirty-three sat on a stool with his hands on his knees and his chin sunk in a dark beard.

It was a mourning beard, months long, uneven and left ragged at the edges as such beards were meant to be. Above it the face was square and tired and younger than Welmimash had expected. In the doorway behind the spearmen stood a woman of thirty, his wife, whose eyes went to the case when it was opened and did not leave it.

"You're his barber," said I'a Igwi.

"Yes, lord."

The word came out of him easily, and Welmimash heard it come and did not know what he thought of that.

"They're bringing him out at noon. They tell me I'm to stand beside him." I'a Igwi touched the beard, not as a man touches his own face, but carefully, as if it belonged to someone else. "Not with this, they say. They say a king's face belongs to the city."

"He used to say that."

"Did he." A pause. "And he let no one else do it. Not in twenty years."

"No one, lord."

"Not even the mornings after his sons were buried?"

"Those mornings first."

I'a Igwi looked past the spearmen at his wife. She said nothing. He turned back.

"It's hers," he said. "The beard. It's Ninsimin's. You know that."

"The whole harbour knows it, lord."

He let his breath out through his nose, for a long time. "Then do it."

Welmimash laid out the oil and the bowl and the stone, and drew the razor across the stone six times each way as he had every morning of his life, and tied the cloth round the young man's neck, and began.

The beard was too long for shaving, so first he took it in his fist a hank at a time and cut it close. He laid each hank on a cloth spread on the floor, and the wife came in past the spearmen and knelt and gathered them into the fold of her shawl, every one, without a word, as though they were the months themselves.

Then the oil, and the blade.

He laid it at the throat first, as he always did, to let the skin learn it. It was a young throat, thick, with a pulse in it as slow and strong as an ox's. One of the spearmen at the door shifted his feet, and Welmimash knew why, because the thought had come to him too: that a barber's hand had only to turn a little, and Uem'o would have its choosing after all. He found the thought in his hand the way you find a hair in your bread, and he picked it out and set it aside. A barber has one opinion, and it is the skin under the blade.

He went from the left ear down along the jaw. The skin came up clean, paler than the brow where the beard had kept the weather off it. Down to the corner of the mouth, to the hollow of the cheek, where twenty years had taught his hand what to do; and his hand did it. He rinsed it in the bowl, said "Lord," and reached for the young man's mouth.

I'a Igwi jerked his head back so fast that the blade had to leap away from him. "What are you..." And then, as the barber's thumb hung in the air between them: "Mind my teeth."

Welmimash stood very still.

Then he laughed. He could not help it; it came up out of him like a cough. And then his eyes were stinging and he could not see the young man's face at all, and he put the razor down on the cloth, because a barber does not hold a blade he cannot see past.

"Forgive me, lord," he said. "He had none. Not one, these twenty years. The cheek had to be held out from inside, so. And every morning when I put my thumb in, he said that. Those words."

I'a Igwi stared at him. Something moved in the half-shaved face, and gave way, and he laughed: a short astonished bark, as if he had been struck between the shoulders. In the doorway his wife turned her face to the wall.

Welmimash took up the razor again. With teeth behind it, the cheek held its own shape, and the blade went over it easily, the way it went over anyone's. His thumb had nothing to do.

"What did he talk about," I'a Igwi said, when the barber had come round to the other ear, "with your thumb in his mouth?"

"Fish, lord, mostly. Whether the boats were in. The price of grain. He liked to know it, though he never paid it. More than a [[cur:100|Tiva]] and a half of silver for a measure, these days, and he'd say somebody had made the gods cross and he hoped it wasn't him." Welmimash drew the blade along the jaw. "And once about a boy."

He had told no one. He had not meant to tell this man. But the throat was under his hand, and the beard was in the woman's shawl, and the old king was lying smooth-faced in the next room waiting for noon, and it seemed to Welmimash that the thing was no longer his to keep: that he had only been holding it, the way he held a cheek.

So he told it: the boy who was five, the name, the fifty-eight years. He told it as the king had told it, and at the end he gave the king's words as they were, because they were the king's and he could not improve on them. "They lie side by side. Like children in one bed."

The room was quiet for a long time. The blade went on, under the chin, along the lip.

"Two," said I'a Igwi at last. He was not speaking to the barber. "I have two." And after a while, lower: "Then they have each other."

Welmimash finished the lip and the chin, and washed the face with warm water, and held the polished bronze up to it; and the king of [[pol:156|Uem'o]] looked for a long time at the face the city would see at noon, as if he were trying to remember whose it was.

"Come tomorrow," he said.

Welmimash folded the razor into its cloth and went out past the spearmen into the noise of a city learning the news. All the way down to the harbour he walked with his thumb shut tight in his fist, the way a child in a crowd holds on to a finger.
