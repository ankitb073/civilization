In AY 1001 a scribe marching with the army of [[pol:171|Tallin]] pressed a report into clay for his queen. It is addressed, with seven bows and seven more, "to the Great Queen, to Kalidath, lady of Tallen", and most of it is what a queen expects to hear: the omens were good, and at dawn the army went down. Then the scribe turns accountant:

> We counted the dead of Yeda. We cut the right hands and counted them before Dudhithshin. Nine thousand five hundred and sixty-eight hands we counted.

And before his closing blessings he asks for bread:

> Now the army is hungry. The grain of this land is burned. The men eat the grain of Nidhikhti slowly, one measure for two men. Let my lady send grain.

The tablet, PAL·LT·01660, the "Dispatch to the Great Queen from the Field at Virathkanu", was dug up at Taulin in AY 4948. It is one of the few war reports of the age that hold to the man: the record gives the same 12,217 Tallin soldiers against 22,977, the same 9,568 enemy dead and the same 2,859 of Tallin's own. Victory and hunger in one letter make a fair emblem for this chapter. Between AY 1000 and 1250 the great states of Yüvinzhi went on winning their wars, and one after another they broke apart. The number of people living under states rose only slowly, from 58.8 million in AY 1000 to 60.5 million in 1120 and 61.5 million in 1240, while the number of states fell from 36 to 32 and rose again to 33. By 1120 only 3 per cent of the land lay outside any state.

## The Great Queen and the house of Yida

[[per:5808|Kalidath]] had come to the throne of Tallin in AY 981, at sixteen, and reigned for fifty-five years; later ages called her the Great. Her war against [[pol:165|Yida]], with which the last chapter closed, had already killed one king of Yida: [[per:5678|Beroybethun II]], the Brave, fell at Piyikhzhidhe in 1005, and the next year [[per:6021|Luvanaven]] seized his throne. In 1008 Luvanaven bought peace with forty districts, the towns of Binge and Khungumnu and a tribute for thirty years. One clause mattered more than the rest. It married Kalidath's son Pavbukhmanen to Luvanaven's daughter [[per:6049|Nauduth]].

The peace did not hold. The [[evt:5964|Second Tallin–Yida War]] (1027–1033) cost Yida thirty-two more districts in [[reg:16|Yidhe]] and its neighbours, with the towns of Rile, Virathkhanu and Piyikhzhidhe. The Yida account (PAL·CH·01018), written in 1043, is a monument of courtly denial. It has Kalidath "swollen with pride" and claims that at Vuvu in 1029 Tallin lost 2,838 men to Yida's 89. Yida did win at Vuvu, but the record gives Tallin 1,217 dead and Yida 359. The account closes on the Treaty of Binge with a sentence that might be carved over every archive door: "Nothing of worth was lost."

Within four years there was nothing left to lose. Luvanaven died in 1032; Nauduth held her father's throne until a usurper, Nathmanen, took it in 1035; and in 1037 came the [[evt:6052|Collapse of the Yida State]]. Most of the realm became the empire of [[pol:262|Mankha]], a small corner of it the city-state of [[pol:263|Yide]]. The record names the lost battles and the two treaties as causes. Nathmanen, writing from exile (PAL·LT·01690, "Letter of Nathmanen from the House of Strangers"), adds what he saw from the throne: "Duminu the storm-lord [...] rain. The barley was thin. The poor man paid in Nulith of silver what he had paid before in copper." A deposed king has every reason to blame the weather, but the prices bear him out. A measure of grain at [[set:10|Navnge]], Yida's capital, cost about 7.6 grams of silver in the 990s and 18.7 grams in the 1030s.

Tallin absorbed its enemy's blood along with its land. Kalidath died in 1036, her son reigned until 1049, and the next king, [[per:6423|Badhashen]], was Nauduth's son, grandson both of the Great Queen and of the Yida usurper. In the [[evt:6147|First Holy War of Tallin against Talenonoy]] (1049–1054), fought in [[reg:8|Diebeguvebenga]] against a kingdom whose armies never much exceeded three thousand men, Tallin won every battle, losing 865 men against 2,075. Badhashen's rock relief at Turamtu (PAL·IN·00426) claims 400 enemy dead in 1053; the record gives 93. The treaty brought nine districts and 321 talents of silver.

The century's other opening war ran on the same pattern. In the [[evt:5735|Ana–Waga War]] (1002–1015) [[pol:229|Ana]] took the land of [[reg:13|Iiyyi]] from [[pol:153|Waga]] after thirteen battles. A veteran's memoir of 1024 (PAL·CH·08433, "The Words of a Spearman of Ana, Set Down in His Old Age") remembers [[per:6002|I'pil Lieze I]] of Waga as "the fox" who stood against Ana "year after year", and does not hide the cost: "We lost more men than Waga lost. The scribes say eleven thousand of ours fell." The record says 10,931 against 10,799: more, if barely, and close to the scribes' figure. By the Treaty of Bakh Waga gave up fourteen districts, the towns of Toiyoe and Tlomesh, and tribute for thirty-nine years. Later ages called I'pil Lieze the Conqueror, which says more about court historians than about his first war.

## Drought, famine and a people on the move

The climate of these centuries was mild on average, about 0.18 °C below the long-run mean, but the average hides a dry century. The [[evt:5818|Onset of the Great Drought in Nivi (AY 1012)]] began a drought that lasted until 1125 and touched 530 districts. In the 1020s more of them lay in Yida than in any other state, which may explain Nathmanen's thin barley, although the record does not count drought among the causes of Yida's fall. A first famine struck Khishkhih at once: some 274,000 people died or fled in 1012.

The second had larger consequences. The [[evt:6231|Famine of AY 1066 in Mana]] struck the heartland of Mankha; about 264,018 people, 18 per cent of the population, died or fled. Here the record draws the causal line itself. Between 1066 and 1090 some 236,500 Ryethti, the people of Mankha, left Mana for Mikhukhizhi, a province of the [[pol:204|Khie'a]] empire, and the [[evt:6358|Collapse of the Khie'a State]] in 1077 is laid to that migration and to the usurpation of Esiesh Ada in 1054. The "Lament for Khie'a of the High Gate" (PAL·PO·00026) names both:

> Then the strangers came down. The Ryethti came down from the far country,  
> they came with their herds, they came with their tents ...  
> Their fires were on our hills. Their sheep ate our barley.

Khie'a survived as the smaller empire of [[pol:274|Khyeka]]; Mikhukhizhi became [[pol:275|Mikhuthitsu]], a Ryethti state seated at Unushti. Sixty years after the famine it fought Mankha, Ryethti against Ryethti, and the war of 1126–1133 left rival chronicles. The Mikhuthitsu account (PAL·CH·01185), written 49 years later, has the enemy lose 6,640 men at Unushti in 1133 against 333 of its own. The Mankha account (PAL·CH·01186) skips three of the five battles and claims that at Longa in 1127 it lost 409 men against 2,435; in fact it lost 741 and Mikhuthitsu 653. The victor himself comes closer. In a letter of the year of the peace (PAL·LT·01677) King Nuvbolen tells the Mankha king that at Unushti "more than two thousand of yours fell before my spearmen. Of mine, not a thousand." The record gives 2,517 and 902: near the truth on both counts. The peace cost Mankha fifteen districts and 488 talents of silver, and the record lists it among the causes of Mankha's own collapse in 1147.

Other droughts followed, in Ng'jinaguh from 1079 and Daadüdhih from 1081. At Mbilo a temple record (PAL·RE·00026) puts grain at "2.25 Ngüzhin the measure", about 18 grams of silver, about twice the usual price.

## Three falls in thirty years

In AY 1081, in the city-state of Tsinondonu, a man named Kathkhuknakhen is credited with the first war chariot in the record. Tsinondonu, founded the year before, held a single district, and Tallin attacked it at once. At the first battle the city put 247 men into the field against 15,047; at the second, 196 against 32,256. Tsinondonu lost 97 and then 58 men; Tallin lost 13 and then 6. In 1082 the city was annexed, and the invention vanished with it. No other state took up the war chariot until 1480. When later chroniclers give their enemies "chariots beyond counting", they are writing literature; the fighting chariot of this age lived and died in one small city.

Then, within thirty years, three empires fell, among them the two largest in the world.

The first was [[pol:169|Bäni]], which broke into four in 1111. The record lists two old provincial revolts, of Duegowinga in 1059 and Lona in 1082, and three defeats in the field. A civil war had split it once before, in the [[evt:6014|Secession of Tungarvikhrim]] of 1033, and Tungarvikhrim reappears among the successors of 1111. A scribe of Kvile who lived through the [[evt:6685|Collapse of the Bäni State]] (PAL·CH·08442, "Chronicle of the Breaking of Bani") put it more sharply than any modern historian: "it was not the spear of the enemy that broke Bani. It was the shut gate. It was the empty seat."

The second was [[pol:155|I'ith]], in 1120 the largest state on the map, with 11 per cent of the land and over seven million people. In 1121 it split into six, among them [[pol:291|Tantethi]] and a kingdom that kept the name of [[pol:292|I'ith]]. For the [[evt:6753|Collapse of the I'ith State]] the record lists no cause at all, no lost war, plague or drought; it shows only order draining away, its measure of stability falling from 0.49 in AY 1000 to 0.04 in 1120. Its last king, Ish'o Lu, wrote from exile that year (PAL·LT·01691): "My army was not broken. My spear was not broken. But while I was in the field, my servants turned against me." And then: "One kingdom has become six thrones, and none of them is mine."

The third was Tallin itself. Under [[per:6818|Tafbafkolvan]] it fought the [[evt:6680|Tallin–Mankha War]] (1110–1122) for [[reg:2|Nivi]], the heart of the long drought. At Khungumnu in 1111 a Tallin host of 35,313 was beaten by 6,257 men of Mankha and lost 6,436 against 1,320. Tallin won in the end, as it always did, and took 38 districts, but over the whole war it lost 10,282 men to Mankha's 5,563. By 1130 it stood at its height, some 10.1 million people and 340 districts, and was very nearly ungovernable: its stability, 0.53 in AY 1000, had sunk to 0.17, and by 1140 it was 0.06. In 1141 came the [[evt:6958|Collapse of the Tallin State]], and the causes the record names are the defeats at Khungumnu in 1111 and 1116, in a war Tallin had won. Six states rose from it, among them [[pol:306|Yeda]], [[pol:310|Pome]] and a new kingdom of [[pol:307|Navnga]].

An old soldier of that war, writing in 1144 (PAL·CH·08431, "Tablet of an Old Spearman of Tallen"), refuses to count the dead ("I count my brother") and asks the question of the whole age: "The land we bled for, who holds it? The barley we threw, who gathers it?" The Harbour Flux, a plague that killed about 499,000 people between 1139 and 1142, struck Ane, Bakh and Pivithie, not [[set:0|Taulin]]. Tallin was not killed by plague. Its own victories wore it out.

A translator's note on the chief heir. The scribe of 1001 calls the land of Yidhe "Yeda", the form Tallin's Bibbu-speaking court used, and the empire that rose at Taulin in 1141 took that regional name. Under [[per:7268|Pavtovan]] and his son [[per:7516|Nathpofnon]] it grew past five million people, and by 1240 it had seven million. Meanwhile, as the giants fell, the middle powers doubled between 1000 and 1240: [[pol:178|Puvu]] from 2.5 million people to 6.1 million, Waga from 2.5 to 5.4, [[pol:237|Tagath]] from 2.8 to 5.8 and [[pol:175|Yinnga]] from 3.3 to 6.5.

## Ash, silver and the wheel

In 1146 the [[evt:6938|Eruption of Mount Punenio (AY 1146)]], of the force the record classes as VEI 7, cooled the world by about 1.05 °C for several years. The volcano stood in the Nivi districts Tallin had taken from Mankha in 1122, by then mostly in the new Navnga. Drought had returned in Baan in 1144, across 306 districts; a famine in Luthe that year took about 216,000 people from Tagath, and grain at [[set:9|Dagafu]] rose from about 10 grams of silver in the 1130s to 16.5 in the 1140s. Then came the [[evt:6940|Famine of AY 1149 in Nuekhmokh]], which killed or drove off 82,051 people, almost a third of the population. The record does not name the eruption as its cause. Our knowledge is thin in a telling way: the "Record of the dry years from AY 1144" (PAL·RE·00031) survives as one line, "Grain stood at 1.69 Niva the measure", only because a Bani chronicler quoted it 223 years later.

Money held steady. Each great state weighed silver to its own standard: Tallin's Lul at 7.8 grams, the Ikhal of I'ith at 10.7, Ana's I'ash at 8.7, Puvu's Ngüzhin at 10.1. None of them was debased in these 250 years, and at Taulin a measure of grain cost between 7.6 and 12.7 grams in every decade from 1000 to 1250. The merchant's danger was short weight, not bad money; Nuvbolen demanded payment "by the weight of the dina of Mikhuthitsu, and not by the light stones of your merchants." Fifty-eight merchant houses were founded in the period.

Faith shifted without great upheaval. By 1120 La'im held 15 per cent of the land, ahead of Ya'cuam and Jinru at 8 per cent each and Kieve at 7. In 1005 [[pol:231|Yiutl]] adopted [[mov:14|Uem]] and [[pol:238|Kavu]] adopted [[mov:4|Khupande]]; in 1008 [[pol:246|Kesivi]] adopted [[mov:16|Nenje]]. New cults appeared, among them [[mov:34|Yuivi]], founded by [[per:6060|Yima]] at Faamni in 1009, and [[mov:35|Njimbu]], founded by [[per:6434|Nashizle]] at Yizih in 1044. But the best evidence for what people believed is one sentence. The old spearman of Tallin had it from the priests of Jinru, Ish'o Lu from the priests of Ya'cuam, Nathmanen from the teaching of Ryethki, the singer of Khie'a from La'im, and the mourners of "The Weeping of Nelo by the Sea" (PAL·PO·00025) from the priests of Khupande: the wheel turns; what ends will begin again. Five faiths offered one consolation to an age that needed it.

Languages went on dividing. In 1099 Lela emerged from Bibbu, the speech of Tallin, and Kumbi from Kukuge, the speech of Puvu, along with Kuze, Lunulte and Beni. The record dates 60 sound changes to the period: Tasemem lost its long vowels about 1141, for instance, and Telnu simplified its double consonants about 1085. In the sources Tallin is "Tallen", Mankha "Manke", Waga "Vaga" and Tagath "Tagavu", depending on who held the stylus.

## Roads, usurpers and the last ash

Elsewhere the late wars were long and inconclusive. In 1190 two usurpers took two thrones, [[per:7822|Navavun]] in Tagath and [[per:7826|Lomeramadhen I]] in Yeda, and each reigned for nearly forty years. The [[evt:7435|Waga–Khyeka War]] (1189–1198) ended in stalemate; the stele of the Khyeka king Pimkunufmalin II (PAL·IN·00510) claims 10,100 Waga dead at Tunavükhini in 1193, where the record gives 2,298. Khyeka itself fell in 1218, into four states, and two of them, [[pol:336|Tomözel]] and [[pol:337|Val'a]], were gone within twenty years.

Puvu fought twice for the roads of [[reg:24|Pyivi]]. The [[evt:7662|Third Pyivi Trade War]] (1211–1220) ended in stalemate, with 13,632 Puvu dead and 11,062 of Tagath. An old soldier of Kabaf, dictating his memories in 1239 (PAL·CH·08432), says of the last battle at Aguga that "for each man of Puvu who went down, near three men of Tagavu went down, as if Tuve had weighed them in a single scale." The record bears him out: 430 against 1,189. He blames the war on Navavun's tolls on salt and copper. Under [[per:8112|Natumbambu]] Puvu won the [[evt:7895|First Puvu–Pievi War]] (1236–1248) and 26 districts, but at Shimbayi in 1238 it lost 6,668 men to 1,114 of [[pol:300|Pievi]]. The Puvu court chronicle (PAL·CH·01387) admits 2,167. The old soldier will not discuss it: "I will not speak of Shimbayi. Let the mothers speak of it." Of the Pievi account a single sentence survives in later annals (PAL·CH·01388), calling Natumbambu "swollen with pride", the stock phrase the Yida chronicle had used of Kalidath. Losers' archives perish more often than winners', and our picture of the age is accordingly a victor's picture.

Ana, meanwhile, was ruled by children. [[per:6360|I'pil Luesh'a II]] came to the throne at six in 1041, [[per:6827|I'pil Luesh'a III]] at five in 1094 and [[per:7462|Mi'shöyueva]] at two in 1156, each under a regent. The [[evt:6734|Holy War of Ana against Mwehosh]] (1119–1126) won the land of [[reg:3|Mii]], and its treaty (PAL·TR·00427) also asked that La'im be permitted in [[pol:234|Mwehosh]] and its shrines protected. The water-stained Annals of Ana (PAL·AN·00125) record the victory at Biyitl but not the defeat at Muvush in 1122, where Ana lost 2,054 men against 471. Ana reached seven million people and 353 districts in 1230; by 1240 the record's measure of its stability stood at zero.

Then came the [[evt:7940|Eruption of Mount Onas (AY 1243)]], again cooling the world by about a degree. Its ash fell mostly on Ana, including the districts it had taken from Waga at Bakh in 1015. In the 1240s grain at Dagafu nearly tripled, from 8.7 to 23.6 grams of silver, and at Ane it rose by a third; at Taulin it did not rise at all. In 1250, with the ash winter not yet over, Ana broke into six states. The record blames two old provincial revolts, of Tiy in 1202 and Unhatlmum in 1204, rather than the volcano: a fitting last verdict on an age whose great states fell to shut gates more often than to enemies. What rose from Ana's ruins, and how Waga, Tagath and Yeda came to dominate the map, is the story of the next chapter.
