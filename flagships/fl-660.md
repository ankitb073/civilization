The surviving documents of the [[pol:946|Miuhitl]] Empire name [[per:24568|I Yuf]] exactly once. A chronicle of the war against Tsiy, composed at Lininadhe in AY 2833 by the historian Hishu Lihtla, survives in a medieval copy, complete with minor losses. Its entry for AY 2798 reads: "I Yuf held the ford against them, and by the grace of the One whom the Miyituyishiu teach, the day was ours." That is all. The same chronicle describes four more of his battles without naming any commander.

The record of battles weighs him differently. He fought thirteen battles between AY 2798 and AY 2832; in the empire's 290 years no other commander led more than six. He was a man of the Mahim, born in AY 2764, and his name means *Two Fire*. The record is silent on his parents, his childhood, any wife or children, and how he rose. He enters it already a general, in AY 2798. That was the year the emperor Sha Mukhe died and his eleven-year-old son Khimi took the throne under the regency of his mother, Kiki. A child at war on two fronts needed someone to fight for him. The record does not say who chose I Yuf.

## Two Fire at the ford

His first campaigns were the easy kind. At Nuvun in AY 2798 he led 18,221 men against 1,275 of [[pol:1049|Tinim]]. At Ninshinuesnun the next year he led 9,065 against 998. They ended the [[evt:24746|Twelfth Ayshi Trade War]]. The treaty survives on a bronze tablet, excavated there in AY 4948. It gave Miuhitl six districts, a yearly tribute and toll-free markets, and it ends: "Let it be read aloud in the markets of both lands." Peace was granted in the name of Khimi, aged twelve.

The battle that earned him his one line was different. At the [[evt:24822|Battle of Zhaalakhazhash (AY 2798)]] the Tsiy general [[per:24462|Yiwemaf]] brought 20,152 men against his 19,953. The odds were even, and I Yuf won outright. Tsiy lost 8,106 men, two in five of its army, and Miuhitl lost 2,166. No battle of the [[evt:24747|First Holy War of Tsiy against Miuhitl]] cost Tsiy more. The chronicler still improved on it. He nearly doubled the enemy army, to 39,472, put their dead at 19,886 and cut Miuhitl's to 1,144. He also credited the victory to "the One", a curious phrase for a cult that taught that many gods share the world. The truth needed no such help.

## The river that rose twice

Between AY 2800 and AY 2804 he lost four battles in a row to [[pol:949|Tsiy]]: at Lishimti, at Zhaalakhazhash again, at the [[evt:24904|Battle of Kadhdanul (AY 2802)]] and at the [[evt:24931|Battle of Unushti (AY 2804)]]. Outnumbered each time, he lost far more men than the enemy: 3,253 against 2,157, then 4,332 against 2,208, 4,294 against 682, and 2,241 against 146. At Unushti the armies were nearly equal, 10,595 against 11,368, yet he lost fifteen men for every one of theirs. His five battles in that war cost Miuhitl 16,286 men, about two-thirds of its losses in the whole war.

The chronicle turns these defeats into misfortune. At Lishimti, it says, "by treachery, our army withdrew in good order". At Zhaalakhazhash and again at Kadhdanul it gives the same words: "because the river rose and cut the army in two, the host fell back to the walls". At Unushti the defeat came "when the allies failed to come". The figures are more revealing than the excuses. In every one of these entries, the victory included, Miuhitl's dead are cut to about half and the enemy's raised about two and a half times. So steady a proportion looks like a rule, not a run of mistakes. The chronicler wrote in AY 2833, while the general was alive, sixty-nine and a year past his last defeat. Whether omitting his name from the defeats was tact or blame, we cannot tell.

In AY 2800 Khimi died at thirteen, of natural causes, the record says. [[per:24605|Mihihhimin]] seized the throne that year, and in AY 2801 Ini broke away under Nitllu Himin, a Mahim like I Yuf. In AY 2804, the year of Unushti, he beat the rebels at Lunge, 16,011 men against 2,434. At the [[evt:24942|Battle of Lunge (AY 2805)]] he beat Pihkemamshil, 11,936 against 4,841, killing 1,581 for the loss of 273. The revolt collapsed and thirty-one districts came back. Mihihhimin's diorite stele at Lunge tells it this way: "defeated Ini in Lune. He killed 9,500 soldiers." That is six times the record's figure, and the general's name does not appear. When Nambili dug at Lunge in AY 4911, he found no destruction layer: "the city seems never to have been sacked."

In AY 2805, too, Mihihhimin made peace with Tsiy at Unushti and gave up thirty-eight districts, Unushti and Kadhdanul among them. The chronicle says the king "accepted the pleas of Tiy". The survey of the 2800s counted 120 districts, against 158 a decade earlier, and about a million fewer people.

## Against the giant

The [[evt:24977|Yiotl–Miuhitl War]] was never an even contest. By the end of the 2800s [[pol:924|Yiotl]] counted some 14.6 million people and 145,124 men under arms. Miuhitl counted 2.6 million and 34,260. At Anavun in AY 2812, [[per:24635|Mashe Yueve II]] came in person with 74,340 men against his 11,106, nearly seven to one. He lost 1,334 men, a small price at such odds. At the [[evt:25036|Battle of Nuvun (AY 2814)]] the Yiotl general [[per:24628|Yoni Lieze]] brought 70,071 against 12,725. This time I Yuf lost 3,887 men to Yiotl's 1,498. These were two of the three largest armies Miuhitl ever faced in the field. Nuvun was where his first easy victory had come sixteen years before. Now the peace signed there cost nine districts and a tribute for thirty-seven years. The queen's basalt stele at Nuvun claims: "She killed 30,600 soldiers." That is more than twice the army he had brought, and she had not commanded that day. Royal steles left out the generals on both sides of the line.

He appears again at sixty-three, in the war Miuhitl waged on [[pol:987|Mane]] in the name of religion. At the [[evt:25191|Battle of Ene (AY 2827)]] he led 15,369 men against the 4,387 of [[per:24916|Kidzililianwu]] and killed 1,548 for 356. The rock relief there belongs to the empress Payatlnim, daughter of the usurper: "She killed 9,400 soldiers." The peace that followed obliged Mane to protect the shrines of the cult of [[mov:25|Mie'to'ieshio]].

His last battle came at sixty-eight, at Lishimti in AY 2832, against [[per:24954|I Himim]], a Tsiy general born the year after Kadhdanul. I Himim had 37,375 men to his 14,776; I Yuf lost 3,380 to 1,072. From then until the war ended in AY 2840, the empress led her armies herself.

## Outliving Miuhitl

I Yuf lived thirteen years after his last battle, and the record keeps nothing of them. In AY 2840 Tsiy besieged [[set:47|Lininadhe]] and took forty districts by treaty, Lishimti among them. In AY 2843 the empire collapsed. He died in AY 2845, of natural causes, at eighty-one, two years after the state he had served.

His totals are not flattering. In thirteen battles his armies lost 25,693 men and killed 20,596. Five of his six victories came with the larger army, three of them against forces a sixth the size of his own or smaller. All seven of his defeats came with the smaller one. The record names five causes of Miuhitl's collapse, and three of them are battles he lost: Kadhdanul, Unushti and Nuvun.

A fair reading cannot stop at the totals. Twice he met Tsiy on almost even terms. At the ford in AY 2798 he won the victory that cost Tsiy more than any other in that war. At Unushti in AY 2804 he suffered a rout. Both days belong to him. Most of his other battles were settled by numbers Miuhitl did not have. His battles span thirty-four years and three reigns: a boy emperor, a usurper and an empress. That he was still in command at sixty-eight suggests that those who knew his failures best found no one better. As an archaeologist I would like to report a stone from one of his thirteen fields that bears his name. There is none; the inscriptions name kings and queens. What remains is one line on parchment and a column of numbers. Read honestly, the numbers are kinder to him than the silence.
