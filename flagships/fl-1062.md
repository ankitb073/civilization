title: The Half That Sits Still

The rider had to say it twice. The first time, the words went into the long hall at Azhi and nobody would take them from him: that [[per:25629|Navande]], daughter of Navivige, had sat down in [[set:227|Nzhinduvi]] and called herself queen of [[pol:914|Kaazhe]], and that seventeen districts had sat down with her.

The second time the hall understood, and every face in it turned to the wrong woman.

Navande of Khishkhis was on her stool at the king's left hand, a fan of plaited grass going slowly in her lap, and she felt the looks arrive one after another, the way flies find a graze. She had been looked at in this hall before. She had been looked at as the price of a lost war; as the foreigner who said the gods' names short; as a woman nine years older than her husband who had nonetheless given him seven children and showed no sign of dying of it. But nobody had ever looked at her as though the rider had spoken her name.

He had, of course. That was the trouble.

When she came to Azhi there was already a Navande at court, the old king's girl, sixteen, all knees and elbows and horses, and a princess of Kaazhe outranks a clause in a treaty. So the name stayed with the girl. The bride from Khishkhis became *the prince's wife*, and then *the queen*, in the hall; *the Khishkhis woman* in the market; and in the kitchens, as she had once overheard through a wall, *the Treaty*. Nobody in Azhi had said her name to her face in years, except the old nurse she had brought from home. Now a man with dust to the knees had shouted it under the roof-beams, and the court of Kaazhe was looking at her as if it had caught her with her hand in the grain jar.

Two people did not look at her. One was her husband. [[per:25628|Nazhimi]] was watching the rider as though the man might yet take it back. The other was the king's mother, Najime, who was looking at the bench by the door where her daughter used to sit with her knees drawn up, cracking melon seeds between her teeth through every lawsuit about water.

"Is she well?" the king said.

It was the wrong question, and the hall felt it: a small shifting of feet on the mats. The rider blinked.

"She was on a horse, lord," he said, as if that answered it; and perhaps it did.

* * *

The first time she met the other Navande was at Yilish, in the summer of the treaty, on the bank of the river her mother had held.

Kaazhe had lost at [[evt:25942|Yizih]], and at [[evt:25976|Yilish]], and at [[evt:25980|Yilish]] again, and the old king had come down to the ford himself to swear the peace across from the woman who had beaten him. The [[evt:25981|Treaty of Yilish]] was a short thing, as such things go: silver, a truce of fifteen years, and a marriage. The bride was the marriage. She sat through the swearing in her mother's tent, sweating in the good wool her mother had insisted on, and listened to herself being given away in a language that was nearly her own. The people of Kaazhe said everything a syllable longer. *Azhe*, her mother's scribes wrote; *Kaazhe*, said its king. *Shilim*, said the bride, when she prayed to the lady of wisdom; *Shilimu*, said the priests across the water, as though the goddess were hard of hearing.

The girl came over in the dark, with one groom and no permission. She stood inside the tent flap with river water running out of her boots and looked at the bride the way you look at a horse someone has told you you've been given.

"Is it true," she said, "that your mother held the ford herself?"

"Who told you that?"

"Everyone. In our camp they say it the way you'd say somebody has a fever."

"It's true."

"Was she afraid?"

The bride thought about lying, and found she did not want to, not to this face. "She was sick into a basin before," she said. "And after. In between, no. In between she sat her horse in the middle of the water where everybody could see her, and they did what she did."

The girl took that in like a mouthful of something too hot to swallow. Then she said, "They tell me you're to have my name."

"I've had it fifteen years longer than you."

The girl considered this. Then she laughed, a big unroyal laugh that must have carried to both banks. "Then we'll share it," she said. "You can have the half that sits still."

She was gone before the bride could answer. The bride had been composing an answer, on and off, ever since.

* * *

Nambave brought the curds herself that evening, which meant she had something to say and wanted no kitchen girl to hear it. She had nursed the bride in Khishkhis and followed her to Azhi, and she was the only person in Azhi who said *Navande* to her, and she said it the Khishkhis way, clipped, from the front of the mouth.

"In the kitchens," Nambave said, setting the bowl down, "they're saying Nzhinduvi is full of people who pray to the One."

"It is."

"And that so is this room."

The queen looked around the room: the two of them, the bowl, the lamp, a wasp worrying at the lamp. "This room," she said, "has two old women in it who pray to whatever happens to be listening. Sit down."

Nambave sat. The dry sky of Azhi had cured her broad, sensible face like a strip of meat. "They're saying the luck went out of Kaazhe when you came into it. The priests teach that prosperity is the gods' favour made plain, and since you came—"

"Since I came," the queen said, "Kaazhe has been exactly as lucky as it was the day before I came, which is to say it had already lost the war. Nobody thought to give me to it until afterwards."

"I'm only telling you what they say."

"I know."

Nambave lowered her voice, though there was nobody to lower it for. "Your mother would have known what to do."

"My mother held a ford and an empire," the queen said, "and now she holds neither." She heard how hard it came out, and was sorry, and did not take it back. Three years ago the empire of Khishkhis had come apart like a loaf left out in the rain, into six realms, none of them ruled by her mother. Somewhere in that wreckage a pious old woman was still alive and still saying her prayers. And here in Azhi the old woman's daughter was still a term of a treaty whose other party had ceased to exist: one half of a truce of fifteen years, with nobody left on the far bank to keep the other.

Outside in the courtyard the children were shrieking. She went to the doorway.

Her eldest girl, Nakhizhi, had climbed the mounting block by the stable and was standing on it with a reed in her fist. "This is [[pol:1134|Nzhinduvi]]," she was shouting, "and I am the queen of it, and you can come and take it if you dare." Her brothers came at her with reed spears, Nalaavu roaring, Nanzhizhim silent and quick, and it was Nanzhizhim who caught her ankle and brought her down into the dust. She got up with a skinned knee and a face like weather.

"It isn't finished," she told him. "I'll have it back next time."

What ends will begin again, the priests of the [[mov:38|Zhibinjizu]] taught; the whole world turned on their great wheel, and nothing went under it that did not come up again on the other side. The queen had always thought this a doctrine for people who had never lost anything. She watched her daughter limp across the yard towards her and did not call out to stop the game.

"Mother. Is Aunt Navande a king now?"

"She says so."

"Can a girl be king?"

The queen knelt and looked at the knee, which was nothing, and at the face, which was not nothing. "In my mother's country," she said, "a woman ruled for thirty years. In your father's, we are about to find out."

* * *

The king came late, smelling of the lamp-oil of the council room, and sat on the edge of her bed with his boots still on, as if he might be called away again at any moment.

"Everyone looked at you," he said.

"I noticed."

"They're fools."

"They looked at me because it's my name."

"It was hers first."

"It was mine first," she said. "I am fifteen years older than your sister."

He laughed, which was what she had wanted: a short, surprised breath through the nose, the first of his day. Then he put his face in his hands for a while. He was thirty years old. His father had lost a war to a woman and had given him that woman's daughter to show for it, and now his sister had taken seventeen districts and the name of his throne and gone to sit in a city full of the faith his father had gone to war against.

"She came back from your tent," he said into his hands. "At Yilish. She'd been across the river; everyone knew, nobody said. She didn't speak to anyone for two days. What did you tell her?"

"That my mother was sick into a basin before the battle."

He looked at her between his fingers. "That's all?"

"She asked if it was true that my mother held the ford. I said yes."

"Then it's your mother's fault."

"In this country," she said, "everything is."

He did not laugh that time. He took off his boots, slowly, one and then the other, and set them side by side on the floor. "What does a woman do," he said, "at a ford?"

She thought of the brown water, and her mother's horse in it up to the chest, and the small upright figure that every man on that bank could see. "She goes where her men can see her," the queen said. "That's all. Everyone thinks there's a trick to it."

"Navande will do that."

"Yes."

"I'll go myself," he said. "When the grass is up." And then, after a while, so quietly that the crickets nearly had it: "If her men can see her, so can mine."

He lay down without undressing further and was asleep the way soldiers and fathers learn to be, all at once, like a stone dropped into a well. She lay beside him and did not move. This was her half, she thought: the half that sits still. She had kept it faithfully, through seven children and the dry seasons of Azhi and the long courtesy of a house where nobody used her name. She had never once wanted the other half, and she did not want it now. She only wanted, very badly, to have said something better than *it's true* to a wet girl in a tent.

Towards morning she prayed. Not to the One; that had always been her mother's door, not hers. She prayed to Shilim, the lady of wisdom, Shilimu as they called her here, with their extra syllable, because a goddess who was a woman might understand what she meant. She asked her to keep Navande safe.

She did not say which. It was, she thought, the first prayer she had made in Kaazhe that she could be certain would be granted, at least by half.

**Author's note.** The record gives the house of Kaazhe two women named Navande in AY 2909: the king's sister, and the wife the Treaty of Yilish gave him. What passed between them is mine. The ford and the shorter Khishkhis words (*Azhe*, *Shilim*) come from PAL·CH·04752 and Nadazum's own stele, PAL·IN·01914. The story leaves out that the sister was killed the next year commanding at the [[evt:26087|Battle of Bandidadhu]], and that a pupil's king list from Azhi omits her (PAL·SX·00385). In AY 2951 the girl on the mounting block, Nakhizhi, claimed Nzhinduvi herself; she was executed (PAL·IN·05454).
