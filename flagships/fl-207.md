title: Kings Who Vanish Between the Years: Regnal Dating and the Tupchinga–Chinontonu War

The [[evt:1928|Tupchinga–Chinontonu War]] (AY 529–544) was long, nearly even and fatal to rulers. The record counts thirteen battles, six won by [[pol:64|Tupchinga]] and seven by [[pol:65|Chinontonu]]. Four commanders died in them, three of whom were reigning sovereigns. Each chronicle reports eight battles, and each reports the death of exactly two enemy commanders and none of its own. The omissions can be recovered from inside the texts. The scribes cut the battles, but they could not cut the calendar.

## The tablets

The Tupchinga account (PAL·CH·00253, Veruldu, excavated at Dininge in AY 4913) is Yichyetkut's, dated "in the 5th year of Ununchifin", AY 563, nineteen years after the peace. Yichyetkut was a girl when the war began. The Chinontonu account (PAL·CH·00254, Balman, excavated at Zhinontunu in AY 4899) is Chengnun's, dated "in the 103rd year of Chinontonu", AY 583. That was eighteen years after the kingdom had ended, and Chengnun was born during the war. Both were writing from court memory, not experience.

## Where the calendar betrays the text

Chengnun opens "in the 3rd year of Nupdon", AY 529. His next entry is dated "in the 1st year of Nupdon", AY 531. The regnal count has reset, so these are two kings of one name, and the text never says how the first one stopped reigning. The record does. Nupdon I, who had seized the throne in the [[evt:1911|Usurpation of Nupdon I]] in AY 527, fell at the [[evt:1947|Battle of Zhinontunu (AY 530)]], a Tupchinga victory that Chengnun omits. He also omits the [[evt:1948|Sack of Zhinontunu (AY 530)]] that followed it. Yichyetkut, by contrast, keeps that battle: "Nupdon himself was killed, and his head was brought to the king."

Yichyetkut's calendar has a larger hole. She dates by "the 35th year of Chiftapbapin" in AY 530 and then jumps to "the 1st year of Bukin" in AY 535. In between, the record has Chiftapbapin killed at the [[evt:1966|Battle of Zhinontunu (AY 532)]] and his successor, Queen Chidjimchet, killed at the [[evt:1986|Battle of Zhinontunu (AY 534)]]. Both were Chinontonu victories, and both are missing. Chidjimchet's whole reign has fallen out of the dating scheme. Chengnun preserves both deaths, the second in full: "Chidjimchet herself was killed, and her head was brought to the king." Yichyetkut wrote under Ununchifin, and the reign of Bukin before him had begun in a usurpation. Such a court had little use for the memory of a fallen king and queen.

## Field by field

*Who began it.* In the record, Tupchinga attacked to seize territory. Chengnun agrees ("there came word that Chiftapbapin of Tupchinga had gathered an army against Chinontonu"). Yichyetkut reverses it and adds that "the gods commanded that the wrong be avenged."

*The battles.* Each tablet keeps every victory of its own side. Yichyetkut keeps all six Tupchinga victories and admits two defeats, at Linnshal in AY 537 ("in a great storm") and at Zhinontunu in AY 543 ("by treachery"). Chengnun keeps all seven Chinontonu victories, beginning with the [[evt:1959|Battle of Zhinontunu (AY 531)]], and admits one defeat, at Dininge in AY 538. He drops the [[evt:1993|Battle of Zhinontunu (AY 535)]], where his general Tunatbin fell.

*The dead.* The record gives Tupchinga 5,631 dead and Chinontonu 4,347. Yichyetkut claims 1,089 and 7,291. Chengnun claims 14,182 and 903. Battle by battle, the rules are steady. Yichyetkut multiplies enemy dead by about 2.2 and admits about 38 per cent of her own side's, while Chengnun multiplies by about 3.6 and admits about 55 per cent.

*Outcome and land.* Tupchinga won. The treaty sworn at Zhinontunu in AY 544 ceded two districts of [[reg:9|Tuvizhinge]] and imposed a ten-year truce. Yichyetkut claims three districts, "returned" to Tupchinga. Chengnun calls it a stalemate, which on the battle count is less absurd than usual. He admits nothing that deserves the name of territory: "Some villages of no account were given up, which had always been poor."

## Method

Fifteen years of war for two districts was a poor bargain for both sides, and neither court could say so. They could, however, leave out a king. What they could not easily do was renumber the years, because regnal years were also the dates on contracts and tax rolls. Where a count resets or skips without explanation, the reader should look in the record for the battle that has gone missing.
