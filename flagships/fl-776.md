title: Lament of a vine-dressers' village outside Adhezhile after the fifth siege

This is the lament of the vine-dressers of the village by the east road, outside the walls of [[set:65|Adhezhile]], set down by one of them who learned his letters from the priest's boy, in the year that the city fell.

Sieges are a kind of weather here. The old men speak of them as they speak of hail: the year of the first, the year of the third. When we were children the old men boasted of the sieges the city had stood, and we believed that a city could be like a mountain, that armies would break on it as wind breaks on rock. It is a comfortable belief. It costs nothing until the day it costs everything.

Last year the armies of [[pol:1125|Khishkhis]] came. They beat our lord's men before the walls, and then they sat down for the [[evt:27339|Fourth Siege of Adhizhili]]. They did not take it. We told ourselves that the old rule held. In the spring they came again, more of them, so many that the road was a single column of dust from dawn to dusk for three days. There was another battle before the walls, and then the [[evt:27345|Fifth Siege of Adhizhili]], and this time the rule did not hold.

What did the siege mean for us, who live outside the walls and have no walls of our own? It meant that their horses ate our vines down to the stock. It meant that their foragers took the seed barley from our jars and paid us in promises, which is a coin that does not ring. It meant that our daughters slept in the reed beds. It meant that we watched the city from our roofs, as one watches a sick man through a window, and could do nothing.

Then the gates opened. Our lord [[per:26861|Nakhizhi]], who had commanded before the walls the year before, went out to the emperor [[per:26788|Navavun]], and set his seal to the peace, and the land of [[pol:1019|Adhezhile]] became a part of their empire. That was the [[evt:27346|Conquest of Kaadhezhile]]. And after he had set his seal, they put him to death. I set this down plainly, because I have heard it said already in the market that he died of a fever, and it is not true, and someone should write that it is not true.

Now we are subjects of a city we have never seen. The tax-men count our vines, or what is left of them, and value the harvest in [[cur:630|Mbumbin]], their silver. A measure of grain is about a Mbumbin and three-quarters. That is not a famine price. But a vine eaten to the stock does not bear for three years, and three years is a long time to be grateful for a fair price on grain we cannot buy.

The teachers of the [[mov:24|Udhungala]], whom our new masters honour, say that time does not turn on a wheel but runs in a line, from the making of the world to a final judgement, and that each of us may find the truth by inner light and by reason. I have tried the inner light. It shows me my vines. I have tried reason. It tells me that a city is not a mountain, and that a peace is a thing the strong give to the weak, and the weak sign.

If there is a judgement at the end of the line, I hope this year is read aloud at it. I hope the one Power hears how the vine-dressers of the east road were treated, and how our lord was treated after he signed. And I hope that, when it hears, it is just, as its teachers say, and moderate, as its teachers also say; for if it is only moderate, we shall not have much.

Written in the year 2938 of the Udhungala.
