*From the memoir published in parts by a workers' press of the capital. The author signs only as "one of the Anli crowd."*

**CHAPTER THREE: THE LISTS ON THE POST-OFFICE WALL**

My generation did not learn politics from books. We learned it from the casualty lists.

When the news of the [[evt:46172|Battle of Anavun]] came over the wire, I was a boy apprenticed to a printer. We set the type for the special edition ourselves, and I remember the figures because I set them with my own fingers: twenty-five thousand of ours sent against an army of more than four hundred thousand. Nobody at the case said a word. The foreman read the proof twice and then went out into the yard and was sick.

Two years later came the [[evt:46210|Battle of Xath]]. Seventeen thousand against more than half a million. I have heard it called a battle. It was an arithmetic lesson, and the whole of [[pol:1935|Nuidaduth]] sat it. Then the [[evt:46211|Treaty of Xath]]: our country made a vassal of Tallin, its merchants given privileges in our ports that our own merchants did not enjoy. I watched the officers of the foreign customs move into the harbour office, and I understood, at fifteen, what the word "sovereign" means by watching it leave.

**CHAPTER FOUR: THE YOUNG KING**

[[per:45338|Miilu]] came to the throne the following year, a man of two-and-thirty, and for a season the cafés were full of hope. He would reform the ministries; he would renegotiate the treaty; he would at least lower the grain duty. He did none of these things. I do not say he was wicked. I say he was a king, and a king is a man who can afford to wait. We could not.

We met in reading circles, as many did, above shops and behind bakeries. Our faith, the [[mov:69|Kievi]], teaches that each seeker may find the truth by inner light and reason, and asks for justice in dealings and moderation in all things. We took the first half of that very seriously. I confess we were less faithful to the moderation.

**CHAPTER FIVE: ANLI**

In the winter of the revolution the price of grain had doubled while wages stood still. That year [[per:45592|Imamfaya]]'s pamphlet went through the city. I helped to fold it. I can still recite the opening: *They tell you to be patient. You have been patient for a thousand years.* We demanded the eight-hour day. We demanded bread at a price a family could pay. And we told each other, as the pamphlet told us, not to fear the police, because there were more of us than of them.

There were. On the first morning of Anli the squares of [[set:247|Nuidaduth]] filled before dawn. There was frost on the tramlines; I remember the crowd's breath standing over it like steam from an engine. The police looked at us, and counted, and went home. Within four days the old government had fallen, and the papers were already calling it the [[evt:46302|Nuidaduth Revolution]]. Miilu was deposed. No one I knew laid a hand on him.

Three weeks later the proclamation was posted: no one would again rule by birth alone; the republic was established; Imamfaya would lead the provisional government. The bells were rung in every town. I was on the roof of the printing works, and I counted eleven steeples.

**CHAPTER SIX: AFTERWARDS**

Imamfaya was president for four years and died in office, worn out, they said, and I believe it. Others have followed him. Today the president is [[per:45695|Yeüsh]], a man of my own generation.

I will be honest with my readers, because honesty is what we were promised and what we promised in our turn. On the morning of the revolution, according to the *Clarion*, grain sold at 3.23 [[cur:1084|mieniu]] the measure. This morning I paid 3.81. The eight-hour day is law in the capital and a rumour in the provinces. The republic is not paradise. All things turn on the great wheel, and what ends begins again; I have lived long enough now to fear that this is true of kings as well.

But I have lived also to see presidents chosen rather than born. That is not nothing. That is not nothing at all.

*4 Imin 2426 of the Kievi*
