title: The Twenty-Fourth Year of Veni

By the middle of the morning the smoke had found its way into the house of tablets and lay under the roof beams like a second ceiling, and Tyinedath had stopped going to the door to listen. Nothing in the noise was news. Hooves on brick, where there should be no hooves. Women calling names. Now and then the long tearing sigh of a reed roof letting go, and after it a heat on the air like a hand passed too close to the face. Below the palace terrace a man had been shouting one word in the speech of [[pol:890|Tomdal]] since first light, and she had heard it so often that she had almost decided it meant *here*.

She sat on the bench under the high window with the register in her lap. It was not heavy. A year of her life had come down to one tablet, its columns pressed in her own small hand: two provinces, their households, their souls, the sum. She had walked most of it. In the eleventh year of [[per:20547|Odenin]] the king had wanted to know what he had, and she had gone out into Yimola with two donkeys and a boy to keep the clay damp, from village to village under the hills, saying the same words at every threshold: the king's count; how many under this roof; how many men; how old. She wrote what she was told, and, more often than she liked, what she could see.

She knew the number at the bottom the way other women knew the weight of a child. She had pressed it herself.

Magontungath came down the roof stair with soot on her forehead where she had wiped it and her braid half undone. She was twenty-seven and moved as if every room had been built a little too small for her.

"They're in the lower court," she said. "The storehouses on the east side are burning. There's a horse tied in the court of [[dei:7|Yov]]." She looked at the tablet. "Give me that."

"Why?"

"Because it's everyone. Every household between here and the hills. Every man who can hold a spear." She held out her hand. "You made it so the king would know what he had. If it stays on that shelf, they'll know what they have. The well is in the second court. Clay doesn't come back out of water."

Tyinedath did not give it to her. "It doesn't say who anyone is. It says how many."

"To them that's the same thing."

"They know how many," Tyinedath said. "They counted at Xath."

Earlier that year, on the cold high ground at Xath, the general Kuthuvvinanen had stood against more than eleven thousand of the Tomdal with four hundred and thirty men, and a hundred and fifty of them had not come down, and he was among them. Tyinedath had heard the figure in the king's hall the night the news came. On the tablet in her lap, under the sum, in her own hand, it said: *Men fit to bear arms: 5,354.*

Since that night she had been trying to make the two numbers lie down together, and they would not. She had not counted wrong. She had stood in those doorways. She had watched a shepherd above Xath hold up three fingers for his sons and fold one down when his wife looked at him, and she had written three. A count is of who is there. It cannot say who will go.

"Then what was it for?" Magontungath said, more quietly.

Tyinedath considered it honestly, because the girl deserved that. "For being true," she said. "Someone had to set down what was there."

* * *

They heard the Tomdal before they saw them: spear butts on the doorposts of the outer rooms, a laugh, a jar going over and the wet slap of oil on the floor. Then the doorway was full. Four soldiers in leather with iron at their belts; an officer carrying a torch of bound reeds that dripped as it burned; and behind them a thin man with a bag over his shoulder that Tyinedath knew, from the way it hung, held clay.

The officer looked along the shelves, at the baskets of tablets, the lidded jars, the reed mats rolled between them, and said something short. Two of his men began pulling the mats down into a heap in the middle of the floor. Tyinedath understood that well enough. Dry clay will not burn, but a room of reed and old timber burns hot enough to crack every tablet in it and bring the roof down on the pieces.

She stood. She spoke to the thin man, because he was the only one looking at her rather than at the room. "You're a scribe."

He blinked. "I write," he said, in her own tongue, with the vowels set down a little wrong, the way a man sets down a load he has not carried before.

"Where did you learn our speech?"

"From merchants of yours." He had the grace to look away. "They stayed with us some time."

Four years before, she had stood at the side of the king's hall while Odenin walked up and down, dictating to the king of Tomdal, *my brother*, a demand for his merchants and their goods. He had liked the last line. He had had it read back to him twice: "If you will not answer, let the sword answer." It had seemed a fine sentence at the time. It had been answered.

"Tell your captain," Tyinedath said, "that he will want the count before he burns anything."

The thin man spoke to the officer. His name, he told her later, as if it were something she might need, was Baetluee. The officer looked at her for the first time, held out his hand for the tablet, turned it over, and passed it to Baetluee, who read the heading slowly, his lips moving: *made by order of the king.* Then he held it out to her again.

"You read," he said. "I write."

Her hands had already gone to her own clay jar. She pinched off a lump, kneaded it smooth against the bench, and gave it to him, and only when he had knelt and wetted his stylus did she understand what she had done. Then she read her register aloud to the men who had taken [[set:105|Pviye]], standing in the smoke: the households and the souls of Yimola; the households and the souls of Veni; then the sum, thirty-nine thousand one hundred and eighty-four households and one hundred and ninety-seven thousand one hundred and forty souls. Baetluee pressed it all into her clay in letters she could not read. Then she read the last two lines.

"Men fit to bear arms: 5,354. Priests and their households are exempt."

Baetluee put it into Tomdal speech. The officer laughed, a short sound through his nose, not unkind, and said something, and Baetluee hesitated before he gave it back.

"He says five thousand is a good number. He says at Xath we saw four hundred and thirty."

"Tell him it is a true number."

Baetluee looked at her a moment and did not tell him.

The officer asked one more thing, and Baetluee turned it into: "What does grain cost here?"

"Before your king came to the walls," Tyinedath said, "a measure cost one [[cur:460|Ilshezhu]] of silver and two parts in five."

Baetluee wrote that down too. The officer kicked the heap of mats apart with the side of his foot, said a word to the youngest soldier, and went out with his torch. The boy stayed, leaning on his spear in the doorway, facing out.

"No one burns here," Baetluee said, unnecessarily. He wrapped his tablet in a cloth with more care than she had expected, and followed his captain.

Magontungath had not moved from the foot of the roof stair. "You sold them," she said. "For this." She meant the shelves.

Tyinedath sat down. Her knees were not good. "I told him a number he would have had by next harvest with spears and a tax-gatherer, and had it wrong, and taken more for it." She nodded at the end shelf, where the annals of the old kings lay in their baskets. "Do you know what is on that shelf? In the fourth year of Rvivenen: *Pviye was sacked by the enemy.* Six words. Someone set them down after the smoke had gone, and the city went on, and the tablet has kept them ever since." She folded her hands in her lap, where the register had been. "That is what I sold them for. Six words, and a room to write the next ones in."

* * *

Late in the afternoon the boy in the doorway let them climb to the roof, because Magontungath asked him with her hands and he was bored.

The great court below was full of men and horses. Under an awning at its far end an old man sat in a chair as if his back pained him: Ipil Movi, king of Tomdal, who had beaten Kuthuvvinanen under these walls himself the year before. He was fifty-eight. Tyinedath had imagined him, for four years, as the man the letter was written to, young and insolent. He looked like somebody's grandfather waiting for his supper.

They brought [[per:20547|Odenin]] out between two soldiers. He was not bound. He was forty-three; Tyinedath had known him since he was thirty-two and took the name of king in the first year of Veni, and she thought, absurdly, that he had put on the wrong cloak. A tablet was held out to him, and he set his name to the peace that gave [[pol:901|Veni]] and all fifteen of its districts to Tomdal. Then the two soldiers walked him across the court and out through the gate passage, and he did not come back.

Tyinedath kept her eyes on the gate for a long time. Beside her, as the light went, Magontungath said the evening words to [[dei:10|Mazhude]] under her breath, as on any evening.

At dusk Baetluee came with a lamp and stood in the doorway beside the boy. "It is done," he said. "Your king."

Tyinedath did not ask how. Magontungath did, and Baetluee shook his head, and that was the whole of what either of them ever learned.

When he had gone, Tyinedath got up and put the register back on its shelf, in its place, in the order of things, because a tablet out of its place is a tablet lost.

* * *

Twelve years later, in the same room, Magontungath read her the chronicle before the clay was dry. Tyinedath's eyes had gone; she could no longer see the small hand, only feel it. The palace belonged to the Tomdal now. The boy who had guarded the door had married a potter's daughter from the lower town, and their children ran in the court calling to one another in both tongues.

"In the 8th year of Odenin … Ipil Movi of Tomdal broke the peace and sent raiders across the border of Veni," Magontungath read, and then, "In the 12th year of Odenin … the royal city was taken."

She stopped.

"That's all?" said Tyinedath.

"That's what was taken."

"The king lost under the walls in his ninth year. Kuthuvvinanen lost there in the eleventh, and at Xath in the twelfth, and died. That happened."

"If I write Xath I have to write four hundred and thirty," Magontungath said. "And then one day somebody will take your register down from that shelf and read five thousand three hundred and fifty-four, and lay the two side by side, and say that either the scribe lied or the men of Yimola were cowards."

"Neither is true."

"No. So I won't give them the chance."

Tyinedath sat with that. For twelve years she had been unable to make the two numbers lie down together, and the girl, who was not a girl, had simply not let them into the same room.

"Read me the end."

"*Let this record keep what the sword has taken.*" Magontungath's voice did not change. "*Set down for the instruction of later days by Magontungath, scribe of the palace of Veni, in the 24th year of Veni.*"

"Veni had twelve years."

"Veni has had a great many years. It keeps ending. You've read the shelves." Magontungath set down the stylus. "What ends will begin again. Isn't that what [[mov:1|Yakhkamu]] teaches? Isn't that what the priests say?"

"The priests," said Tyinedath, "are exempt."

Magontungath laughed, the first time in that room for a long while, and then waited.

Tyinedath had counted every door between the palace and the hills. She had never in her life set down a number she had not seen. She held out her hand, and Magontungath put the damp tablet into it, and the old scribe found the last line with her thumb, the way she had once found the sum at the bottom of the register.

"Twenty-four," she said, in the voice she had used at every threshold in Yimola, as though she had stood at its door and been told.
