In AY 65, the first year of a new king of [[pol:1|Tallin]], an old soldier had a scribe press his memories into clay. "I was in the war. I carried the spear. I came home. I set it down." He had spent two summers walking the border with Poyonga, burning threshing floors and having his own burned. "I count my sandals, which I wore through three times." He was there at the end, when the army broke into the city of Poynge in AY 63. "The tablet-house of Poyonga burned; its tablets cracked in the fire like the voices of old men. Their words are gone. Ours remain." (PAL·CH·08457, *What My Eyes Saw at Nin and Poynge*.)

The [[evt:159|First Sack of Poynge]] is the first recorded burning of an archive in the history of Yüvinzhi, and the soldier's boast is the first thing a reader of this chapter needs to understand. The institutions of the age have left us only 77 documents, 66 of them inscriptions, beside some sixty poems, letters and memoirs. Nearly all the songs and soldiers' tablets of Tallin's wars came out of a single excavation at Taulin in AY 4948. We see these centuries largely through the eyes of the kingdom that burned other people's tablets. Its boasts can be checked; its victims' silence cannot.

## A world mostly without states

At AY 0 the record sees eight states, with about 2.6 million people under their rule. By AY 120 there were nineteen states and 14.5 million subjects, and by AY 240 there were twenty-four states and 26.4 million. Even at AY 120, 87 per cent of the land lay outside any state. The four largest kingdoms, Tallin, [[pol:6|Ana]], [[pol:3|Tagath]] and [[pol:0|Shue'ethie]], each held about one hundredth of it. These were small kingdoms on rivers, surrounded by herders, hill peoples and villages that paid tax to nobody.

Most of the great names of the age were already there at AY 0: Tallin, seated at [[set:0|Taulin]]; Ana at [[set:7|Ane]]; Tagath at [[set:9|Dagafu]]; Shue'ethie at [[set:3|Ishwyithie]]; the [[pol:5|Nilu]] kingdom at the old city of [[set:5|Nilu]]; and the [[pol:7|Buneynga]] chiefdom at [[set:11|Biniynge]]. [[pol:8|Navnga]] was founded in AY 10 under [[per:9|Banathbin]], with its capital at [[set:10|Navnge]], and [[pol:13|Tivintusa]] in AY 40, with its capital at [[set:13|Tivintukhe]]. In AY 110 the [[pol:18|Kadhdazhile]] Empire appeared. It would outlast every other state born in this chapter and survive until AY 1008.

Long reigns gave these little states their continuity. [[per:1|Baliun]] ruled Tallin for 39 years, [[per:24|Nangatsu II]] Tagath for 49 and [[per:28|Rudan]] Buneynga for 53. Women ruled often: [[per:56|Todifoyatl]] held Shue'ethie for 46 years (38–84), [[per:143|Pimttumunuerat]] and later [[per:294|Duebunen II]] held Buneynga, and [[per:434|Tatakhshalath]] gave Navnga its longest reign (169–210). In Ana, [[per:153|Kwi'etlue'e I]] (75–106) and [[per:277|Ma'she Movi]] (119–174) reigned long, but the throne there was contested too; two tablets of AY 32 and 73 are apologies by rulers "who took the seat of Ana".

These kingdoms rested on bronze and water. Shue'ethie worked bronze and dug irrigation canals from the start of the record. Bronze needs tin, the dearest everyday commodity of the age: a mina cost 80 to 90 grams of silver at Taulin, close to twenty times the price of copper. Nobody struck coins; silver was weighed by local standards, such as Tallin's niga of about 9.7 grams. The climate was kind on average, +0.08 °C over the period, but the average hides some very hard decades.

## The clay begins to speak

The first great event of the age happened in a palace. In AY 3 the scribes of Ana, a [[cul:0|La'im]] people, set their signs in order in the [[evt:63|Invention of the Shatl'i'ueza Script]], a logosyllabic system in which "a sign for an ox is also a sound". Three years later, in the [[evt:54|Invention of writing in Ana]], the royal servant [[per:8|Kha'eshi]] showed that the signs could be joined into sentences. The idea spread fast. In AY 50 came an [[evt:137|Invention of writing in Poyonga]], credited to a woman, [[per:83|Shiyodüt]], and counted by the record as independent of Ana's. Yet in AY 58 came the [[evt:147|Adoption of the Pikmuftürit Script in Poyonga]], a syllabary that the record derives from [[scr:0|Shatl'i'ueza]]. Perhaps the borrowed system simply won. From Pikmuftürit came the [[evt:207|Adoption of the Bali Script in Navnga]] (AY 86), from Bali the [[evt:218|Adoption of the Tlashna Script in Tivintusa]] (AY 93), and from Pikmuftürit again the Yirenturit script in which Tallin's kings cut their steles from AY 106. Within a century of Kha'eshi, the clay was speaking in half a dozen river kingdoms.

The wheel travelled far more slowly. In AY 186, in the [[evt:386|Invention of the wheel in Shue'ethie]], a carpenter named [[per:476|Ma'she Yueve]] told the scribes of Ishwyithie how he had found it: a log rolling down a riverbank, then a round cut from three planks "pegged together with pegs of hard wood" on a pole of oak. "What forty men pulled, now four oxen pull. What went in ten days, now goes in two." He asked one thing of the gods: that the god of war "not take the wheel for the host before the farmer has it for his barley" (PAL·SP·00006, *Words of Ma'she Yueve in the House of Tablets*). The court annals give his triumph exactly one line: "In the 38th year of O'e Ada. The wheel was devised" (PAL·AN·00002, *Annals of Shue'ethie, AY 100–199*). Twenty-one years later [[per:552|I'cuil Mish]] of Tivintusa invented it again, independently, in the [[evt:430|Invention of the wheel in Tivintusa]]. Scripts crossed frontiers in decades; a carpenter's trick did not.

Faith was divided among many pantheons. At AY 120 the largest, [[mov:9|Menibe]] and [[mov:1|Ya'cuam]], each held about 9 per cent of the land, [[mov:0|La'im]] 8 per cent and [[mov:15|Imom]] 7. Almost every source of the age repeats one teaching: "all things turn on a great wheel; what ends will begin again." Ma'she Yueve heard it from the priests of La'im, and the kings of Tallin heard it from the priests of [[mov:2|Jinru]]. In AY 85, two decades after its city was sacked, Poyonga made the [[evt:206|Conversion of Poyonga to Jinru]], giving up its old Ligay gods for its conqueror's. In AY 234 came the [[evt:568|Conversion of Virnado to Lelmi]].

Against the wheel stood one dissenting voice. In AY 79, at Nilu, a teacher named [[per:160|Nakavzi]] made the [[evt:186|Founding of the Uthungala Cult]]. His sayings survive only as a damaged tablet compiled by a disciple (PAL·SC·00001, *Sayings of Nakavzi*). They teach that time runs in a line from creation to a final judgement, that "there is one above all the gods, and the gods are its servants", and that a man should not believe a thing because a priest says so: "Test it as you test silver." For centuries [[mov:24|Uthungala]] stayed a cult of a few towns; later chapters will follow it to more than a hundred million faithful. Meanwhile the spoken languages drifted. The record dates 33 sound changes to the period, among them the loss of emphatic consonants in Telnu around AY 14.

## Dry years

Two volcanic shocks, Mount Ashsi (AY 55–59) and Mount Yyeshmongra (78–82), each at severity 6 on the record's scale, made the 50s and 70s the coldest decades of the age. Droughts did more damage than cold. The [[evt:132|Onset of the Great Drought in Khishkhih (AY 53)]] dried 191 districts and lasted half a century. The [[evt:148|Onset of the Great Drought in Daadüdhih (AY 63)]] struck 328 districts, and the [[evt:149|Onset of the Great Drought in Ng'jinaguh (AY 64)]] another 138. Navnge lay inside two of these zones. In the 50s and 60s a measure of grain there cost 35 to 38 grams of silver; at Ane and Dagafu, outside the drought, it cost about 7.

Hunger had come even earlier. The Famine of AY 30 in Mana killed 145,875 people, about one in eight in the part of Navnga it struck. A farmer's wife recorded those years on a tablet found at Navnge (PAL·DI·00023, *A farmer's wife's tablet of the hungry years in Mankha*). The barley came up thin, then not at all. "We gave the ox for two baskets of barley. We gave the copper pot for one basket." Her mother-in-law died, then her small son. "The children ate grass." The king's men brought one basket of barley to each house. She watched the hill people leave with their goats: "They said: in Mikhukhizhi there is grass. They went. They did not come back." The record counts some 126,850 people in that [[evt:105|Migration of the Unsatlmum into Mikhukhizhi]] (AY 30–46) and names the famine as its cause.

The pattern recurred. The [[evt:267|Onset of the Great Drought in Yizamashti (AY 122)]] across 275 districts led, the record says, to famine in Yimla in AY 138, which killed 37,507 people in the kingdom of Wal'a. Baan dried out twice, in the [[evt:266|Onset of the Great Drought in Baan (AY 123)]] and again in 164. Worst was the [[evt:305|Onset of the Great Drought in Daadüdhih (AY 144)]], which lasted 93 years. Grain at Linnshal, inside it, stayed at 24 to 28 grams a measure for half a century. A famine in Daadüdhih in AY 168 killed 222,899 people; the record names no cause, but it struck the drought's own region in its twenty-fifth year. The age also ended dry. The [[evt:518|Onset of the Great Drought in Daadüdhih (AY 235)]] covered 513 districts and the [[evt:519|Onset of the Great Drought in Khishkhih (AY 236)]] covered 326. Famine followed at once: 121,632 dead in Mikhukhizhi, in the lands of Shue'ethie, and 93,623 more in Ng'jinaguh.

## The spears of Tallin

Against this background one kingdom outgrew the rest. Tallin began with about 560,000 people. In AY 60–63 it took a strip of [[reg:9|Tuvizhinge]] from the city-state of [[pol:11|Poyonga]] and burned Poynge. Then it overreached. In AY 83 its king [[per:115|Ngamultun]] went to war with [[pol:10|Lenunushal]], whose usurper-king had "shut the road of the donkeys" and stopped the tin. In his letter home from the [[evt:193|First Tuvizhinge Trade War]], Ngamultun admits a defeat at Taulin in AY 84: "the field of Taulin was not mine that day." But he insists that the enemy "lay down in heaps" (PAL·LT·01710, *The King Writes from the Camp after Taulin*). The record counts 1,629 Tallin dead out of 3,885, and 80 for Lenunushal, whose king's stele claims 4,300 (PAL·IN·00001). Both kings lied, in opposite directions. The treaty does not. By the Treaty of Taulin in AY 87, Tallin paid 446 talents of silver, about 13,370 kilograms, and opened its roads to Lenunushal's merchants (PAL·TR·00002).

Ngamultun reigned for 48 years and got his revenge. In the [[evt:236|First Tallin–Lenunushal War]] (AY 107–111) his army lost again at Taulin. Then it won at Tathriubu, besieged Linnshal until "the people of Linnshal ate their seed", and took the city in the [[evt:250|First Sack of Linnshal]]. His stele claims 900 enemy dead at the last battle; the record gives 396. Lenunushal ceded four districts and paid tribute for 29 years. In the decade the war ended, a mina of tin at Taulin briefly cost 106 grams of silver.

The next generation took the good barley land of [[reg:16|Yidhe]]. In the [[evt:259|Second Tallin–Poyonga War]] (AY 118–128), the queen Kulufpimtuth and then King Panshedominin fought for ten years. Tallin lost 3,699 men to Poyonga's 1,656, more than two thousand of them in one defeat at Nin in AY 123, which the victory hymn passes over: "Of Noni we do not sing." It ended in the [[evt:288|Conquest of Poyonga]] and the annexation of 24 districts. A village lament written 35 years later adds what the hymn left out. The Poyonga queen "gave the land, and they killed her. The old women do not say it loud" (PAL·PO·00104). The record confirms that she was executed.

[[per:327|Tethtun]], king at seven in AY 135, reigned fifty years and carried Tallin into [[reg:8|Diebeguvebenga]]. The [[evt:323|First Diebeguvebenga Trade War]] (146–150) won five districts, the river Nodhnavbu as a border and fifteen talents a year for fourteen years (PAL·TR·00004). Under his successor, the queen Makhshikoth, the [[evt:397|First Tallin–Pumi War]] (187–189) finished the job with the [[evt:406|Conquest of Pumi]] and 28 more districts. A lament sung in AY 199 counts the cost: "One thousand, eight hundred, eighty and four: so many of ours." The record gives exactly 1,884. "The mothers counted wrong," the poet adds, "for each mother counted only one" (PAL·PO·00050, *Lament for the Reapers Who Did Not Come Home from Duvekopuvinga*).

By AY 180 Tallin ruled 4.3 million people in 111 districts, with about 215,000 kilograms of silver in its treasury and nearly 20,000 men under arms. But the record's measure of internal order had fallen from 0.74 in the 60s to 0.53. The village poem suggests why: "Now we are the people of the tax." The tax-men "take the tenth and the tenth of the tenth".

## The usurper's war

In AY 190 the throne passed to Jingunin, aged two, under a regent. He died in AY 201 at thirteen, and in the same year a man named Mirmakhkulin took the throne in what the record calls a usurpation. "The men of the old house call it the Usurpation of Mirmakhkulin," an old spearman wrote. "I do not call it so. The gods gave the seat" (PAL·CH·08458, *The Tablet of the Six Years of the Spear*). Mirmakhkulin needed a victory, and chose [[pol:12|Chinontonu]].

The [[evt:444|First Tallin–Chinontonu War]] (AY 201–206) was the greatest war of the age. At Puthnimdu in AY 202, Tallin's 7,475 men were beaten by 2,231 and lost 2,664 of their own. The Tallin palace chronicle admits the defeat, blames "the cowardice of hired men" and halves the losses to 1,351 (PAL·CH·00013). The Chinontonu king's stele claims 19,900 Tallin dead (PAL·IN·00007). The next year Mirmakhkulin returned with 13,467 men against about two thousand and won, at the cost of 2,054 dead. His own stele claims 2,100 enemy killed, and the record gives 604 (PAL·IN·00008). In AY 206 the city of Zhinontunu fell and its house of tablets burned, and Chinontonu became Tallin's vassal. Over the whole war Tallin lost 7,364 men and Chinontonu 1,294. The spearman's tablet, written for no court, gives army sizes the record accepts and declines to count his own side's dead: "Let the earth count them."

A scribe inside the burned city adds a price: "A measure of barley in the market costs a Yong and a quarter, and the widows cannot pay it" (PAL·CH·08467). That is about 9.5 grams of silver; the record gives 9.4 for the decade. His last line is the best verdict on the war: "it was not the gods who were weak. It was the men of Tallin who were many."

Tallin reached its peak in AY 230, with 4.7 million people and 116 districts, while rotting from within: the measure of order fell to 0.38 under the child-king, 0.21 in the 200s and 0.09 by the 230s. Mirmakhkulin died in AY 231, and his successor, the queen remembered as Danidath the Last, reigned three years. In AY 234 came the [[evt:538|Collapse of the Tallin State]], which split the kingdom into three successors: [[pol:25|Yida]], which kept the old capital at Taulin, [[pol:26|Duvekopuvinga]], seated at Himi, and a short-lived kingdom at Vuvu. The causes the record lists are the defeat at Puthnimdu in AY 202 and the usurpation of 201. There is no drought among them; the great drought of AY 235 began a year after the fall. Tallin was not starved into collapse. It won its greatest war under a king the old house called a usurper, and never recovered the order it had spent.

## Heirs (AY 229–250)

The last years belonged to the next generation of kingdoms. [[pol:15|Kavu]] ended the [[evt:502|Kavu–Unjinlathu War]] with the [[evt:517|Conquest of Unjinlathu]] in AY 229, swallowing the [[pol:24|Unjinlathu]] chiefdom. Tettkaelit, queen of [[pol:22|Virnado]], set up a boundary stone in [[reg:7|Tunnge]] in AY 236 after a small war of her own: "May Baevil curse the enemy" (PAL·IN·06426). That same year she was killed fighting [[pol:14|Egu]] in the [[evt:549|First Egu–Virnado War]] (235–243), which Egu won, taking five districts and 26 years of tribute. Its king's stele claims 3,300 Virnado dead at Zhinlampive in AY 240; the record gives 533 (PAL·IN·00012). Ana grew more quietly, peaking at 2.7 million people in AY 240 under [[per:700|Silla]]; [[per:660|Mononna]] ruled Shue'ethie from 224 and [[per:738|Nathbikafbafen II]] Navnga from 237.

The heir in Taulin began as its parent had. [[per:713|Dokutnakhin I]] reigned from AY 234 to 245, and his son [[per:776|Dokutnakhin II]] took up Mirmakhkulin's unfinished business in the [[evt:589|First Yida–Chinontonu War]] (245–253). In AY 250, after the king's attack on Jinontonu was thrown back into the ditches, a scribe in the camp wrote to the court: "One thousand nine hundred and forty-six of our men do not answer when their names are called. Of Jinontonu, I am told, two hundred and thirty-eight lay down, no more. I write what I have counted. I do not write a smaller word to please you." The record agrees with him to the man. He begged for grain: the measure "that costs a Niga and a quarter in Tallin", about 12 grams of silver (the record gives 12.3), "costs more here, and there is none to buy". He did not know how the war would end: "The gods know its end." (PAL·LT·01712, *Letter from the Camp before Jinontonu to the House of the King*.)

The priests in his camp said what all the priests of the age said: the wheel turns, and what goes down comes up again. They were not wrong. Tallin had fallen and Yida had risen in its seat, in the same city, with the same gods and the same enemies. The next chapter follows that heir through two and a half centuries of tribute wars, cold decades and collapses, until it became the largest state Yüvinzhi had yet seen.
