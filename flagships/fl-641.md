Of the man to whom the record credits the invention of writing in [[pol:6|Ana]], we know almost nothing that he did not tell us himself. That is the first irony of [[per:8|Kha'eshi]]'s life. He saw early that whoever holds the clay holds the story, and he made sure the clay held his.

## A man of the house of tallies

The record gives him a birth forty years before AY 0, a people, the La'im, and a natural death in AY 10, at fifty. It names no father, no mother, no wife and no children. It does not say what he looked like or how he earned his bread before AY 6. The rest must be read from his world and from a single tablet.

When [[per:6|Shuesh'ayueve I]] made [[set:7|Ane]] the seat of a kingdom in AY 0, Kha'eshi was already forty, ten years older than his king. Ane was then nearly three centuries old, a Bronze Age port governed as a city-state. By the end of AY 9, the year before Kha'eshi died, the kingdom counted 427,577 people in ten districts, fewer than 25,000 of them in towns, with 1,718 men under arms. Its faith, [[mov:0|La'im]], held that truth was kept by an ordained priesthood and handed down in order.

The tablet is his own notice of AY 6, The Clay That Speaks (PAL·NP·01077). In it he calls himself "servant of the king, servant of Uelti the wise, maker of marks, son of the house of tallies." Whether that means a family of storehouse clerks or a fellowship of counters, the record does not say. It fits what he describes: a city that used clay to count sheep, oil and barley, but never to speak.

## The signs and the sentence

Kha'eshi did not invent the signs. The record dates the Shatl'i'ueza script, a logosyllabic system, to AY 3 and names no maker. A court poem of that year, Praise of the Signs (PAL·PO·00030), excavated at Ane by Nambili in AY 4920, credits the scribes "in the house of tablets by the river gate," the god Uelti, and the king who fed them: "Uelti gave the thought; the king gave the bread." Kha'eshi concedes as much: "In the 4th year of the king the signs were set in order," he writes. But the signs could only list: "the signs did not say: the king has spoken." His claim is the step from list to sentence: "Now I join sign to sign as a man joins word to word."

The record honours the distinction: it dates the [[evt:54|invention of writing in Ana]] to AY 6, gives it to him, and knows no earlier one anywhere; the next, at Poyonga, came in AY 50, forty years after his death.

The documents are less tidy. The poem of AY 3 is itself a run of sentences, and its heading says it was "set down on clay in the new signs, in the 4th year of Shuesh'ayueve." It already knows the decisive trick, that "a sign for an ox is also a sound." And a brick from the palace of Shue'ethie, at the site now called Ishwyithie, excavated by Nambili in AY 4882 (PAL·IN·03168), records in the same script that Siensayo, queen of Shue'ethie, restored her palace, in her seventh year: AY 6, the year of Kha'eshi's notice. Either both were set down later than they claim, or the sentence was already abroad among the La'im when Kha'eshi announced it. The evidence does not let us choose.

## Who gets the credit

The notice is the work of a man who expected to be robbed: "I made it. I, Kha'eshi, made it. Let no one say another made it." He took a lamb and a jar of oil to the house of Uelti and reports a liver "without spot"; he poured water to the river god and watched the moon for three nights. In a faith of ordained priests, a maker of marks had reason to put the gods' approval on record before the priests could object. To the scribes who complained that "the hand is slow," he answered like a farmer: "the plough was hard before the ox learned it."

His fear was well founded. In AY 12, two years after his death, the dying king wrote to his young son, "In my days writing was found in Ana," and named no finder (PAL·LT·01754). The coronation hymn of AY 27 gives the deed to everyone: "Then the scribes of Ana learned to set words in the clay" (PAL·PO·00079). Of all the surviving documents I know, his name appears in one: his own. Even that is not his tablet but a medieval copy from the temple library at Ane, complete with minor losses. He had asked, "Let the scribes copy it." Centuries later, someone did.

## What the clay kept

Kha'eshi lived four years after his notice, long enough to see a silver weight standard, the Sovosh of 7.6 grams, fixed in Ana in AY 8. The record does not connect the two, and it does not say where he died or who mourned him.

What he promised the people at the gate is the best measure of him. He did not promise the king glory; he promised that the headmen of the river towns would hear the king's word, that each priest would say the rites as the one before him had said them, that at the harbour "the clay keeps the bargain," and that when a father dies "the clay keeps the name of his field, and the son is not cheated." These are a counting-house man's hopes for people who would never be sung.

"The clay does not lie," he wrote. Of course it does: it says what its writer wants, and his tablet claims more than the poem and the brick allow him. But it kept his name when the king's letter and the coronation hymn let it fall, and that, I think, is what he made it for.
