*Spoken before the magistrate of the district and the priest of [[dei:14|Wulimi]], in the year 64 of Kizhyitli, by a man of the hill villages of [[reg:19|Mane]], who came of his own will to the door of the court; taken down by the clerk word for word, as the law requires.*

I came here of my own will. Let that be written first, for it is the only thing in this account to my credit.

I was taken in the levy in the first year of the [[evt:22492|Kizhyitli–Yufkoy War]]. I was a vine-dresser. I knew how to prune and how to tread grapes; the drill-master taught me how to hold a shield, and the rest I learned by watching who died.

I was at [[set:55|Yuftuy]] in the second year, under [[per:22018|Yi Lihtle]]. We were three to their one, and still they held the field till evening; and when it was over the dead of the two sides were nearly equal in number, which the sergeants said was a victory and the burial parties said was not. I dug. I believe the burial parties.

I was at the [[evt:22526|Battle of Akhtse]] in the fifth year, where the king [[per:22143|Maeshi Yuf]] himself commanded. I saw him once, from far off, on a grey horse. That day went well for us. The men of Yufkoy broke, and we followed them, and I killed a man in a vineyard. He was pruned like my own vines, in the same manner. I have thought about that more than about any speech of the king's.

Then in the sixth year we came again to Yuftuy. The battle was bloody, bloodier for us than for them; I lost the whole of my file but one. And after the battle came the [[evt:22538|Seventh Sack of Yuftuy]].

Hear me, magistrate. I do not excuse myself. But I will tell you what I saw, so that you may judge what I did by what I saw. I saw men I had shared bread with drag an old woman out of her door by her hair because she would not say where the silver was, and she had no silver. I saw a child sitting on a step, quite still, looking at nothing. I saw the granary fired with the grain inside, at a time when grain in our own market was four [[cur:521|Tlam]] the measure and our own children went short. I asked myself what civic virtue was served by it, and I could find none. The philosophers say a citizen owes his city his body in war. They do not say he owes it his soul.

That night I put down my shield in the ruins of a house and walked away. I walked eleven nights and hid by day. I came home to my village. My wife hid me in the press-house through the winter.

Why do I come now? When the war ended and Yufkoy was ours, I hoped the matter would be forgotten. It was not. Now the [[evt:22548|Kizhyitli Civil War]] has begun, and the recruiters are in the hills again, and each side says it is the true kingdom, and each side hangs deserters from the other. I would rather be judged by the law of the court than by the rope of a faction.

I ask the priest of Wulimi, lady of war, whether a man who has run from her may still be received at her altar. I ask the magistrate to set what penalty the law sets. I will pay a fine if I am given time; I have the vines. I will not take up the shield again. If that is the penalty, then let it be the other penalty.

That is my confession. I have spoken it whole.
