### VII. The Year We Took the Republic Down

I was nine years old when the news of the [[evt:46350|Battle of Jyijinjivji]] came up the railway. I remember that the stationmaster read the telegram aloud on the platform and then sat down on a bench and did not get up. Eighty-three thousand of ours had gone into the line at Tlitlintlivtli against an army of more than a million. Thirty-five thousand did not come back. The next year it was the [[evt:46360|Battle of Ndulamlol]], and the same story with smaller numbers, for there were fewer of us left to lose.

Then came the [[evt:46362|Treaty of Khankhush]], signed in our own capital, under our own flags, by men in our own uniforms. Every schoolchild of my generation could recite its articles as other children recite prayers. Tlitlintlivtli ceded. Ndulamlol ceded. An indemnity of 19,605 to the treasury of Khishkhis. Twenty years of peace on the conqueror's terms. And, worst of all, the word that our fathers could not say without lowering their voices: *vassal*.

The Republic signed that treaty. The Republic paid that indemnity. The Republic, for twenty-seven years afterwards, held elections, printed newspapers, debated tariffs, and did not once raise its eyes from the ledger to ask what a nation is for.

I joined the Party at nineteen, in a cellar behind the tannery in the old quarter of [[set:97|Kankush]], where the meetings were held because the smell kept the police away. We were a few hundred then. We read the teachers of [[mov:162|Duthladul]] by lamplight: that all things turn on a great wheel, and what ends will begin again; that the good life is the life of duty, order and right sacrifice; that the wise withdraw from wealth and appetite. We asked ourselves what the Republic had ever sacrificed, and to whom it owed its duty, and we could not find the answer in any speech of any minister.

When [[per:45807|Nejigi]] was elected President in 178, the old parties thought they had chosen a safe man. He was not yet fifty, a careful speaker, a man of no faction. They did not know that he had been coming to the cellar for three years.

I will not describe every hour of 182. Others have done it, and some of them were even present. I will say only this. The Assembly was not stormed; it was dissolved, by decree, in the President's own hand, in the morning. By noon the Party's guards held the telegraph office, the railway yards and the mint. By evening the old parties had been told that their services were no longer required by the nation, and most of them had the decency to agree. Very few shots were fired. I fired none. I carried a typewriter.

That was the [[evt:46597|Kankush Revolution]]. The Republic ended. The nation began again, as the wheel teaches. The President became Chairman, and [[pol:1915|Kankush]] became, for the first time since my childhood, a country that could look a foreigner in the eye.

Thirteen years have passed. Bread costs two [[cur:1082|Yivi]] and a little more. The Party is in every street and every factory. And now, for the first time since the stationmaster sat down on his bench, our armies are winning.

I write these lines while the [[evt:46689|Kankush–Biniynge War]] is still being fought. I do not know how it will end; no honest memoirist pretends to. But I know the names that have come down the wire: the [[evt:46712|Battle of Turamtu]], last year; the [[evt:46724|Third Battle of Khüdivi]], this spring. Two victories. Two telegrams read on two platforms, and this time nobody sat down.

The Chairman is sixty-five. He still rises at five. I have seen him do it.
