**THE BIGE NEWS-LETTER.** *6 Vande 4415 of the Uthungala.*

**ON THE NEW ART OF FINDING THE WAY UPON THE OPEN SEA, BY ITS AUTHOR**

The publisher of this sheet has asked me to set down a plain account of the art which the city has lately been pleased to call the [[evt:43354|Invention of ocean navigation in Bige]]. I do so willingly, the more so because the account given in the taverns is, I find, more marvellous than accurate, and because certain gentlemen have been pleased to say that the thing was not mine.

First, what the art is not. It is not a charm, nor a lucky stone hung from the mast. It is a method, and a method may be taught to any pilot of ordinary sense.

Our fathers sailed along the coast, from headland to headland, and when they lost sight of land they prayed. A ship two days out of sight of the shore knew neither where she was nor how far she had come; she knew only that the sea was large and her water small. I have asked myself, since boyhood in [[set:271|Bige]], listening to the seamen who came up from the coast, why this must be so; and the answer I came to, by the inner light and reason which the Teaching of [[mov:24|Uthungala]] commends to every seeker, is that it need not be so at all.

The method has three parts. The first is an instrument, a quadrant of brass with a plumb-line and two sights, by which the height of the sun at noon, or of the fixed star in the north at night, may be taken from the deck. The second is a set of tables, which cost me the better part of three winters, by which that height may be turned into a distance north or south. The third is the log and line: a weighted board thrown astern upon a knotted cord, the knots counted against a sand-glass, from which the ship's speed, and so her way, may be reckoned. Put the three together on a chart ruled in squares, and the pilot may prick his position upon it each noon, and know, within a morning's sail, where he is.

I tried the method two years since aboard a coasting vessel, out of sight of land for nine days. On the ninth day I told the master that we should raise the southern cape before evening. He laughed. We raised it at the fourth hour.

Now to the gentlemen. It is said in certain counting-houses that the quadrant was known before, and so it was, to astrologers on rooftops; and that tables of the sun were known before, and so they were, in the libraries. I never claimed to have invented the sun. I claim to have put the instrument, the tables and the log together in the hands of a seaman, upon a moving deck, and to have made them answer. That had not been done. Let him who says otherwise bring his tables to the coast and his ship to the cape.

Our city is young; it is not yet fifteen years since the [[evt:43257|Founding of Bige]]. Within that time we have struck our own silver, in the [[evt:43263|First Coinage of Bige]]; and this very season my neighbour in the arsenal has astonished the city with the [[evt:43353|Invention of gunpowder weapons in Bige]]. I observe only that a city where men are free to reason is a city where such things happen. The Teaching holds that prosperity is a sign of the divine favour. Let the merchants of Bige consider what favour a ship may earn that can sail straight across a gulf instead of creeping round it.

I have laid a fair copy of my tables before our Lady [[per:42782|Nazhige]], with my humble prayer that they be kept in the city's chancery, for the use of every licensed pilot, at a charge of a few [[cur:1009|Ngzhizhin]] for the copying. The sea belongs to no one. The method should not either.

— **[[per:42870|Nanizhi]]**, of the Street of the Instrument-Makers, in [[pol:1836|Bige]].
