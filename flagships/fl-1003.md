title: Pour Water for the Tablets
---
From the roof of the house of tablets Padiyyiviyath could see the river gate, and she watched it until the light went.

The king had gone out by that gate in the morning. She had counted the men through it herself, as the house always did, on a stool in the shade of the gate-tower with a wet tablet on her knee and a reed in her hand, district by district, name by name: one thousand six hundred and twenty-five. Her son Yurbinin had gone through with the men of the canal district, near the back. He had not looked at her. A man does not look at his mother when he is carrying a spear. [[per:4520|Vanavmanen]] had gone through first, bronze on his breast, the king who had won the [[evt:4339|Battle of Virathkhanu]] three years before and lost the [[evt:4351|Battle of Linidhagshal]] last year, and the street had wondered, quietly, which of those two kings had come out of the palace that morning.

In the evening the gate opened again, and what came through it was not the army. Runners first. Then men holding one arm with the other. Then men carried on doors. The runners said the field belonged to the men of [[pol:165|Yida]]. They said the king lived. They did not say how many did not, because nobody had counted, and Padiyyiviyath, who counted for her bread, found that she could not make her mouth ask.

Behind her, her grandson set down another jar.

‘That is nine,’ Shevilin said. He was twelve, and Yurbinin’s, and he had been carrying water up from the canal since noon. ‘Is nine enough?’

‘It was not enough last time.’

He knew what she meant. The first thing an apprentice learned to read in the old signs was the tablet of the burning. It had been written in this city more than five hundred years before, by a scribe of this house, on clay so badly smoothed you could see where the heel of a hand had pressed it, and its signs were the grandmothers of the signs Padiyyiviyath used, so that reading it was like listening to a very old woman. It said that the men of Yida had come and sat down in a ring around the walls, and that "the ring did not break us." It said the ring went away, and the city told itself it was over, and it was not over. It said the king went out himself, and did not run, and the field was theirs. Then they came into the city. Then they came to the house of tablets. "I saw a man with a torch," the scribe had written, and the shelves fell, and the tablets of the grain-count and the fields and the kings' years cracked in the fire. "[[dei:18|Eva]] of the fire, why did you eat the words of our fathers?" And: "I did not save one tablet. Not one."

Last year the men of Yida had come and sat in a ring around [[set:17|Pumi]], and in the end they had gone away. The [[evt:4352|Siege of Himi]], they called it; Himi was their name for the city, as it had been the name the other men of Yida used, five hundred years ago. The ring had not broken them. People had said it was over. This morning the king had gone out himself.

‘The priests say the wheel turns,’ Shevilin said.

‘The priests say a great many things.’ She had never minded the wheel of [[mov:2|Jinru]], what ends will begin again; at funerals it was a comfort. She had not understood until tonight that it could turn so exactly, line by line, like a pupil copying a model.

Out on the plain the fires of the Yida camp came up one by one, more of them than she could count from a roof, and she was a woman who could count anything.

* * *

She could not sleep, so she went down to the storeroom with a lamp.

The storeroom had no window. It held what the house was for: the count of the grain in the king's granaries and the temple granaries, year by year; the tablets of the fields, canal by canal; the price of barley in the market, which this year stood at a [[cur:92|Taltu]] of silver and more than half again for a single measure, where before the war it had been less than one. Hundreds of tablets in their baskets, and none of them baked. Nobody bakes a count. A count changes.

She looked at them a long while, and then went back up for one of Shevilin's jars.

The scribe of the burning had been thinking about fire. Fire cracks a tablet. Water does something quieter. Pour a jar into a basket of sun-dried clay and leave it until morning, and in the morning there is no count, only mud with the marks of reeds in it, softening, going. The men of Yida would come down to this room and find nothing to tell them what [[dei:17|Nidhikhti]] had put in the granaries of Pumi, or whose fields lay along which canal, or how much silver a city like this could be made to weigh out next year and the year after.

She stood with the jar against her hip for a long time.

Two things stopped her, and she was honest enough, afterwards, to remember that the first was not the nobler. Men who cannot count must guess, and men who have just won a battle guess high. And the second: the count was not the king's. When the men of Yida had gone, and they would go, somebody would have to say how much barley was left and how many mouths it must last. On the back of that bad clay the scribe of the burning had written, "I write the count of the grain again on new clay. I write the names of the fields again, as far as I remember them." She thought of that scribe with burned hands, remembering fields. She would not do that to some woman of the house five hundred years from now, or to herself.

She set the jar down by the door, against fire, which was what it was for. Then she said the night-prayer to [[dei:19|Yenan]], who counts the nights, and asked him to count this one short.

* * *

They came in at the river gate in the morning, and at the gate of the barley-merchants. She heard it go the way the old tablet said it went: the street of the potters, then the street of the weavers. Smoke stood up over the barley-merchants' quarter and leaned toward the river.

Padiyyiviyath and Shevilin stood on the roof among the jars.

Toward midmorning a man came down the street with a torch.

She had read the line so many times that for a moment she did not see him at all, only the signs. Then she saw that it was full daylight and the torch was lit anyway, and that behind the man who carried it walked another man, younger, with a basket of fresh clay on his hip and a reed behind his ear, and behind him four spearmen. They stopped at the door of the house of tablets.

‘Stay here,’ she told Shevilin, and went down, and he came after her, as she had known he would.

The young man with the reed spoke her language the way the border merchants did, with the endings knocked off. ‘Keeper?’

‘One of them.’

‘Granary,’ he said. ‘Fields. Show.’

So she showed him. The torch went ahead of them into the storeroom, and its bearer held it high and steady, away from the reed mats, and she understood that it had been brought for the dark: the scribe needed to see. She had not expected fire to come into the house of tablets so that someone could read.

He could read. That was the next thing. He took a tablet of the temple granary from its basket and turned it to the light and said the signs aloud, the right sounds in the right order, and understood not one word of it, like a man reciting a prayer in somebody else's tongue. So the men of Yida wrote with the signs of Pumi now, and had fitted their own words to them. Nobody had told the house.

‘Say,’ he said, and gave her the tablet.

She read it to him in her language, and he wrote it in his, on his own clay, in her signs. Her words went into him and came out of his hands changed, and only one thing came through untouched: the numbers. The sign for a hundred is a hundred in any mouth. Once, at the king's granary, she let her eyes rest on the hundreds and thought about saying a smaller word, and before she had decided anything he laid his finger beside hers on the clay, on that very sign, and said the number in his own language, and waited. She read it true. The words had been hers. The numbers, it turned out, belonged to anyone who could count.

He asked why the tablets stood as they did, and she told him: by canal, then district, then household, then year, so that a man could find any field in the kingdom between two trimmings of a lamp. He made her say it twice. He drew the shelves on a corner of his clay. He was not taking the tablets, she saw then. He was taking the house.

‘In Yida,’ he said, hunting for the words, ‘scribes write the king. What the king did.’ He tapped her basket. ‘Here you write the barley.’

‘The barley does more.’

He laughed, surprised, and for a moment he was only a young man who was good at his work, and she hated him less than she wanted to.

Then he took from his own basket an older tablet and read from it in his language, and put it into hers. ‘Your king,’ he said. ‘Two thousand six hundred and forty-four men. In the field, at Himi.’ Yesterday already had a name in Yida: the [[evt:4360|Battle of Himi]].

‘One thousand six hundred and twenty-five,’ said Padiyyiviyath. ‘I counted them through the gate. I have the tablet.’

He looked at her a long while. Two thousand six hundred and forty-four was close to every man under arms in the kingdom, the men away at the other war included, as if Yida had beaten the whole of [[pol:159|Pumi]] in one morning. He knew it was wrong. She watched him know it.

‘My king,’ he said at last, gently, as if explaining something to a child, ‘likes mine.’

He did not change it. He was not lying, she thought, not exactly; he was writing the kind of tablet his house wrote. At the top of each new tablet he set the year, and she read it upside down: the thirty-seventh year of Shyevuanaven. At the top of the muster, yesterday, she had written the twenty-first year of [[per:4520|Vanavmanen]]. The same year had two numbers now.

In the doorway one of the spearmen had taken Shevilin by the wrist and was looping a cord round it. Out in the street other boys and young men were already walking tied, two and two. The young scribe turned and said a word in his own language, sharply, and the spearman stopped.

‘He reads?’

Her throat would not work. She nodded.

‘Then he stays.’ He swept his hand at the shelves, the baskets, the whole dim room. ‘Somebody must read all this. After.’

The spearman shrugged and let the cord fall. Shevilin rubbed his wrist and came and stood against her side, and she put her hand on his head and understood exactly what she had been given and what it would cost. Next year, and the year after, and for as many years as the men of Yida pleased, somebody in this house would stand in this room and read the barley of Pumi aloud to strangers. It would be him.

* * *

When the men of Yida went out by the river gate they took the goats and the oil, the cloth from the looms, the silver from the merchants' boxes, weighed on Pumi's own scales, and young men with cords on their hands, as the old tablet said they would. The young scribe took nothing from the house of tablets but his own clay. He had copied what he needed. No beam of the house was scorched, and no tablet cracked. The men of Yida had their name for those days, the [[evt:4361|Sack of Himi]]; the old scribe's people had called theirs the burning. Padiyyiviyath could not think what to call a sack in which nothing of hers had burned.

Then the others began to come in.

They came for days, by every gate, in ones and twos: men who had run from the field into the canals and lain in the reeds, men who had been taken and had slipped their cords on the road. Padiyyiviyath sat on her stool in the shade of the river gate with the muster tablets on her knee, as she had sat on the morning they went out, and as each man came in, or was named to her by those who had seen him home, she pressed a small mark beside his name. Women stood behind her and read the marks over her shoulder. Some of them could read only that much, whether there was a mark or not, and it was enough.

When the men stopped coming she counted the names without marks. She counted them twice, because that was the rule of the house, and the second count agreed with the first: six hundred and fifty-nine. The men of Yida, she supposed, had a number of their own for it, and theirs would be larger. Hers was true. One of its strokes was Yurbinin of the canal district, who had gone out through this gate near the back and had not looked at her.

That evening she climbed to the roof. The jars were still there, nine of them, full, a skin of dust on the water. Shevilin had carried them up against a fire that never came. She lifted one, and the boy took the other side of it without being asked, and together they carried it down to the threshold.

‘Read me the end,’ she said.

He did not need the tablet. ‘Pour water for the tablets.’

All her life she had taken it for a prayer over broken clay. She tipped the jar, and he tipped it with her, and the water ran over the threshold and out into the street, for the tablets that had burned and the tablets that had not, and for the names on them.

**Author's note.** The old tablet is real: *The Burning of the House of Tablets at Pumi* (PAL·CH·08471), written after the sack of AY 332, and every line the story quotes is its own. The Yida account of the war (PAL·CH·00693) gives Vanavmanen 2,644 men and 1,452 slain, and omits the sack; the record gives 1,625 men and 659 lost. The record says Pumi's archives were not burned, and dates Yida's scribal bureaucracy to 855; it does not say how. By the treaty of 857 (PAL·TR·00260) Pumi bound itself to tribute for thirty-six years. Padiyyiviyath's family is invented.
