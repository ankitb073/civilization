Three documents of his own century name [[per:9345|I'cuil Shim'am]], and all three concern the last war he fought, the one his country lost. Two are chronicles, one from each side, and they share a habit. The Pviyo scribe names him only in the battles he lost. The Yimla scribe names him only in the battles he won. Each spent the general's name where it bought the most. This chapter tries to give him back the battles each scribe left unsigned.

## A kingdom with two enemies

He was born in AY 1308, a man of the Ya'cuam people, and served the Kingdom of [[pol:372|Yimla]]. His name means *Seven Eagle*. Of his parents, his household and his rise to command, the record says nothing. The country of his youth was large and settled: the survey of the 1310s counts 722,722 people in 68 districts and an army of 4,336 men. It kept the [[mov:2|Jinru]] gods, whose priests taught that all things turn on a great wheel and that prosperity is a sign of divine favour. In such a faith a defeat needed explaining.

When the record first notices him, Yimla was fighting two wars. The [[evt:8988|Second Waga–Yimla War]] began in 1342. A letter of that year from the seventeen-year-old queen [[per:9210|Sha'o]] to the king of [[pol:153|Waga]] survives (PAL·LT·00996), and its threat over seized merchants is out of all proportion to her army: "Return what was taken, or I will come and take it." Waga came instead. In 1345 [[pol:325|Virnatho]] opened the [[evt:9012|Holy War of Virnatho against Yimla]]. The queen commanded in person and lost before her capital in 1344 and 1346. In the kingdom's seventy-two years the record names only two generals: Shosh'atlü, dead since 1339, and Shim'am, from 1347.

His first battle was the [[evt:9038|Battle of Nuvun (AY 1347)]]. He was thirty-nine. Against him came Virnatho's own king, [[per:9126|Ulokutnakhen]], known as the Great, with 4,753 men to his 1,267. Virnatho lost 1,024 men, Yimla 317. The next year the queen herself commanded on the same ground and was killed. Ulokutnakhen's stele (PAL·IN·00623) boasts "He killed 3,400 soldiers"; the record gives 985. Her son Ilshazh'igw'a came to the throne in 1349, aged four, under the regent Shimin'ash.

In 1349 the general fought on both fronts. Before the capital, [[set:207|Niuzafammas]], he met Waga's general [[per:9357|Ieninieme]] with 1,599 men against 15,829, nearly ten to one, and lost 633 men to Waga's 145. Waga's king Simenmovi, who had not commanded there, raised a stele claiming the victory and 1,500 dead (PAL·IN·00624). A Waga satirist of the same year saw the joke (PAL·PO·00031): "The captain struck. The captain won. The snake sent a letter." The peace cost Yimla 24 districts and was signed for Yimla by a child of four. That same year, at Nuvun again, Shim'am met Ulokutnakhen with 1,111 men against 4,239 and won: Virnatho lost 1,615, Yimla 153.

## Before the walls

The next five years are the core of his career, and no Yimla account of them survives. In 1350 [[pol:401|Shati]] broke away with nine districts under a rival claimant, and Ulokutnakhen beat Shim'am before Niuzafammas (787 men against 3,088; Yimla lost 277, Virnatho 60). The city was sacked. Then he held it. He beat Ulokutnakhen there in 1351 (Virnatho lost 1,163, Yimla 135), the general [[per:9388|Tahpuhnan]] in 1352 (1,036 to 177), and Ulokutnakhen again in 1353 (497 to 98), each time outnumbered between two and four to one.

Between those defences he fought the [[evt:9068|Yimla Civil War]], at Hiviyu in 1352 and Xath in 1353, against [[per:9389|Cui'dilada]], a Ya'cuam like himself and three years his junior. These were the only two of his fourteen battles in which he had the larger army, and the least costly: 96 Yimla dead in both together. Shati was reabsorbed in 1353 under an amnesty.

In 1354 Tahpuhnan beat him at Nuvun, 288 Yimla dead to 46. Even so, the war ended in Yimla's favour. By the Treaty of Nuvun, Virnatho, the side that had begun a holy war, paid Yimla an indemnity and accepted a ten-year truce. Over the war Virnatho lost 5,541 men and Yimla 2,430. Shim'am commanded seven of its eight recorded battles and won five, four of them against Ulokutnakhen in person.

## The tenth sheaf

In 1358 [[pol:402|Pviyo]] rose under [[per:9455|Vyinufmalin]], and 29 of Yimla's 45 districts went with it. The Pviyo declaration of 1363, The Tablet of the Unbowed Neck (PAL·DE·00950), says what those districts had paid: "They took the tenth sheaf and the fifth sheaf. They took the fat ox. They took the young men for their walls." By the end of the 1350s Yimla's whole army numbered 1,327.

Shim'am won the first battle, at Niuzafammas in 1359: 1,013 men against 2,111, with 197 Yimla dead to 632. The Yimla chronicle (PAL·CH·01616) gives the enemy "4,001 men and spears beyond counting" and says "The scribes counted 2,242 enemy dead; we lost 112." The Pviyo chronicle (PAL·CH·01615) passes the battle over. The Tablet comes nearest the record, two thousand spears against more than a thousand, six hundred and more of Pviyo's dead against near two hundred of Yimla's, and makes a creed of the defeat: "The field was Yimola's, but the name was ours."

At Xath in 1360 he had 584 men against 3,903 and lost 180 to Pviyo's 32. At Niuzafammas in 1361 he beat [[per:9484|Vutalen]] with 389 men against 2,920, killing 285 for 97. At Xath in 1362 he had 257 men against 3,705, fourteen to one, and lost 110 of them; Pviyo lost 65.

Vuvanan, writing for Pviyo in 1372, names Kikukil Shimkamu, as his tablets spell him, at Xath in 1360 and 1362, and inflates his army both times. In 1362 he "had gathered 392 men there", and "412 of them lay dead on the field, and of ours only 16": more dead than men. In 1361 the general goes unnamed, the casualties are turned about (71 of Pviyo's, 363 of the enemy's), and the defeat is laid on absent friends: "when the allies failed to come, our men could not hold the field." Shimin'agw, writing for Yimla, names Shim'am for 1359 and 1361 and leaves him out of 1360, which he blames on ritual: "because the omens had been neglected, our army withdrew in good order." He omits 1362 altogether. Where prosperity meant favour, a lost field could be laid on the omens rather than the man.

Shim'am did not fight again. In 1363 the king, now eighteen, commanded at Niuzafammas himself with 302 men against 3,309, and lost. The city was sacked a second time, and the treaty that followed recognised Pviyo as free. The record does not say why the general, then fifty-five, was absent: illness, dismissal, or a young king's wish to lead.

## A dead kingdom's calendar

Waga opened the [[evt:9203|Holy War of Waga against Yimla]] in 1366. It ended without a settlement, and in 1367 the kingdom collapsed; its last sixteen districts passed to no state at all. Shim'am died in 1368, of natural causes, aged sixty. He had outlived his country by a year. Where he died, and who mourned him, the record does not say.

The arithmetic of his career is plain. He commanded in fourteen battles, more than any other commander of his century in the record, and won nine. He was outnumbered in twelve. His armies lost 2,758 men and their enemies 7,001. He won the holy war waged against his kingdom and stood on the losing side of the two wars that took its land. Yimla shrank under him from 69 districts to 16; its army, 4,378 men in the survey of the 1330s, numbered 888 in the last. Generals do not make peace, and three of the four settlements his wars produced were signed by a child.

Shimin'agw, a boy of eight at the time of the 1359 battle, finished his chronicle in AY 1399, thirty-two years after Yimla ceased to exist. He still styled himself "scribe of the palace of Yimla" and dated his work "in the 105th year of Yimla". His account ends with the peace: "The honour of the realm was preserved." It is the voice of men who kept a dead kingdom's calendar, and it shows that some of them remembered the general, for his victories. The 257 men who stood at Xath in 1362 against a field of spears deserve the other half of the remembering.
