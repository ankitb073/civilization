title: One Sound, Many Outcomes: The Labiovelar in the Tasemem Languages

The [[lan:0|Tasemem]] family is among the best documented in the archive. The standard comparative list treats twelve of its languages, eight of them still spoken, descended from a common ancestor whose forms we reconstruct. My aim here is not a full survey but an argument. The family's history, I suggest, is best read through a single unstable sound, the labiovelar *kʷ, which its daughters inherited intact and then resolved, each in its own way. Agreement between daughters is not always evidence of shared history; in Tasemem it is often evidence of shared temptation.

## The family and its oldest laws

The two oldest separations are those of [[lan:6|Simem]] (c. AY -2020) and [[lan:7|Tlatlam]] (c. AY -1967), and the record treats them as the two primary branches. Within that list, from Simem descend [[lan:26|Xaxkhax]] (from c. AY -1450) and [[lan:12|Ie'im]] (from c. AY -741), and through them [[lan:43|Tyitltusum]], [[lan:48|Sevim]] and [[lan:41|Fietlte'ma'im]]. From Tlatlam descend [[lan:27|A'man]], [[lan:25|Shimim]], [[lan:29|Mamalim]] and [[lan:13|Saba]], and through Shimim, [[lan:45|I'tlim]].

Two dated laws precede every separation and so belong to the whole family: deaffrication (tʃ > ʃ), c. AY -2934, and debuccalization (k > ʔ), c. AY -2478. The second is visible in our cognate sets. For 'water', *kikʷeka, Tlatlam has 'igi, the apostrophe writing, as in our usual transcription, the glottal stop that the law produced from initial *k; Simem yiv, Ie'im ibia, Saba iva and Shimim ege have since lost it. For 'king', *kʷakake, Tlatlam 'i'a keeps a glottal stop where the medial *k stood, while Simem paa, Saba paa, Ie'im poae and Shimim kae show the vowels left in hiatus when it was lost. The doubled vowel of paa is the scar of a vanished consonant.

The remaining laws in the dated series (spirantization, p > f, c. AY -1976; breaking, e o > je we after an initial consonant, c. -1814; loss of final m, c. -1610; open-syllable lengthening, c. -1159; syncope of i between consonants, c. -1013; loss of vowel length, c. -727) all postdate the first separation and belong to the Simem line. One curiosity deserves mention: lengthening and its undoing fall less than five centuries apart, so that in the Simem line long vowels were a passing episode.

## The labiovelar

Now the argument. Set out the reflexes of *kʷ:

- 'king' (initial *kʷ): Simem paa, Ie'im poae, Saba paa; Shimim kae; Tlatlam 'i'a.
- 'water' (medial *kʷ): Simem yiv, Ie'im ibia, Saba iva; Shimim ege; Tlatlam 'igi.

The daughters divide neatly into a labial group (p, b, v) and a velar group (k, g, and in Tlatlam the glottal stop that a velar can become). The obvious reading is that the division is genealogical. It cannot be. Saba descends from Tlatlam, yet it sides with Simem and Ie'im. Since the parent is velar and the daughter labial, the labiovelar must still have been intact when Saba separated (c. AY -486), and it must have been resolved separately in each line afterwards. The record's fuller series of dated changes shows the same for Ie'im, which separated from Simem long before Simem resolved its own labiovelar. What looks like two branches is, in fact, at least four independent decisions about one awkward sound.

The lesson is general. A shared outcome is evidence of common descent only when the outcome is improbable. The choice between labial and velar for a labiovelar is not improbable; it is the commonest fate of such sounds, and parallel development should be our first hypothesis.

## Stability and replacement

Against this instability stands a remarkably stable sound, the lateral affricate *ƛ, written tl. 'God', *ƛate, gives Simem tlath, Tlatlam tleth, Saba tlath and Shimim tlath; 'silver', *mitinaƛma, gives Simem myithnatlma, Tlatlam midznatlma, Ie'im mithnatlma, Saba midhnatlma and Shimim miednatlme. Every daughter cited keeps tl in 'silver', and the family's own names (Tlatlam, Tyitltusum, I'tlim, Fietlte'ma'im) announce it. 'Silver' also shows the loss of the second vowel in every daughter; but the record dates separate syncopes in Simem (whose loss Ie'im inherited), Tlatlam, Saba and Shimim, so this agreement, too, is parallel rather than inherited.

One form refuses all correspondence: Ie'im khobam, 'god', which no law I know can derive from *ƛate. It must be a replacement. Its source I cannot identify, and I prefer to say so rather than invent one. Reconstructions are hypotheses about regularity; khobam is a reminder that words also have histories of their own.
