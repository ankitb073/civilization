title: Fourteen Districts and None: The Navnga and Vuvo Accounts of the War of AY 235–237

The [[evt:548|First Navnga–Vuvo War]] (AY 235–237) survives in two palace chronicles that agree on little beyond the fact that it ended. Colleagues usually rank the Vuvo tablet as the weaker witness, because it leaves out the fighting. I want to argue the reverse. Tathdon's silence is easy to see and easy to correct. Bunufmalen's adjustments sit beside true statements and borrow their credit, which makes them the more dangerous.

## The tablets

The Navnga account of the First Navnga–Vuvo War (PAL·CH·00021, excavated at Navnge in AY 4910) is a clay-tablet copy in Nebir. Bunufmalen, "scribe of the palace of Navnga", set it down in AY 252. He had been in his mid-twenties during the campaigns, and he wrote under Nathbikafbafen II, who had commanded at Vuvu in person. The Vuvo account (PAL·CH·00022, excavated at Vuvu in AY 4919) is in Bibbu and dated "in the 38th year of Vuvo", that is, AY 271. Tathdon was born in AY 246, nine years after the treaty, so he knew the war only at second hand. His reckoning by the years of Vuvo also outlived Vuvo itself, which Navnga had conquered in AY 264. By then he was serving a palace of memory.

## Field by field

*Who began it.* The record is plain: [[pol:8|Navnga]] attacked, to exact tribute. Tathdon agrees ("Nathbitafbafen of Navnga, forgetting the oaths his fathers swore, set his hand against Vuvo"), and here the defeated side is the honest one. Bunufmalen reverses it: "Nufdafbafin of Vuvo broke the peace and sent raiders across the border of Navnga. So the king went out to punish him."

*The battles.* The record has two engagements in [[reg:16|Yidhe]], the [[evt:558|Battle of Nodhinge (AY 236)]] and the [[evt:562|Battle of Vuvu (AY 237)]], and Navnga won both. Bunufmalen gets both right: the years, the victor and the Navnga commanders. Tathdon has neither. His tablet passes straight from the outbreak to the terms.

*The dead.* The record puts Navnga's losses at 693 (66 at Nodhinge, 627 at Vuvu) and [[pol:27|Vuvo]]'s at 2,967 (1,198 and 1,769). Bunufmalen gives the enemy 2,254 and 3,329 dead, 5,583 in all, and admits only 28 and 270 of Navnga's own: 298 dead, where the record has 693. The proportions are too regular to be accidental. Each Vuvo figure is a little under twice the record's. His enemy musters (6,067 men at Nodhinge and 10,606 at Vuvu, against the record's 2,390 and 4,178) are inflated by a steady factor of about two and a half. This is a rule of thumb applied line by line, not a misread roll. (Navnga's own dead are cut by the same method, to a little over two-fifths of the record's at each battle.) Tathdon records no dead at all, which is an absence, not a count.

*Commanders.* None was killed, and neither tablet claims otherwise.

*Outcome and land.* Under the [[evt:563|Treaty of Vuvu (AY 237)]], the victorious Navnga took eight districts, Nodhinge among them, together with 825 kg of silver a year for 24 years and a ten-year truce. Bunufmalen has Vuvo suing for peace. He claims fourteen districts and presents the gain as restitution: "The land which they had stolen was returned to Navnga, 14 districts in all." Tathdon offers an honourable peace: "wearied of bloodshed, the king agreed to terms ... The border was settled." No district changes hands and no silver is paid.

## What each court needed

Bunufmalen wrote for the son of the king who had begun the war, and that son had led the army at Vuvu. Such a court needed a just cause, so Vuvo strikes first. It also needed a cheap victory, so the dead are discounted and the gains enlarged. Tathdon wrote decades later for a kingdom that no longer existed, and he needed a war in which Vuvo was wronged but not beaten. He could concede the aggressor because it cost him nothing. He could not concede the battles or the cession, because they were the defeat.

Together, the tablets give the record's skeleton. Tathdon supplies the aggressor, and Bunufmalen the battles and the victor. Only the treaty terms and the muster figures give the true cost. Each scribe told the truth exactly where his court could afford it.
