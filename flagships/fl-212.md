title: A Rival Chronicle Missing: The Pumi Account of the Fourth Myinive Rising, Read Against the Record
---
The method of rival chronicles needs two witnesses. For the [[evt:2750|Fourth Myinive War of Independence]] (AY 642–645) we have one. The Pumi account (PAL·CH·00398, Bibbu, excavated at Himi in AY 4925) has no recovered Myinive counterpart. When the record must stand in for the missing rival, the method can still do a good deal. The Pumi tablet's distortions follow patterns familiar from the paired cases closely enough to be measured, and the record identifies exactly what a Myinive voice would have had to explain away.

## The tablet and its author

Kulvanufmalin set the account (PAL·CH·00398) down "in the 4th year of Nufmaldon", AY 657, twelve years after the suppression. He was in his fifties during the war, which makes him an unusually old witness. The nearest Myinive voice is a tablet from the previous war: Kuthudyiyshan's account of the Third Myinive War of Independence (PAL·CH·00393), dated AY 645, the very year this rising was crushed. It counts the years of Myinive unbroken from AY 637, but it says nothing of the new rising.

## Field by field

*Who began it.* The war grew from the [[evt:2749|Revolt of Myinive]] in AY 642. This rising had its seat at Zhiyiyzhikhti rather than Nivanga, the record names [[reg:2|Nivi]] as the contested region, and the overlord was now [[pol:63|Pumi]]. Kulvanufmalin agrees that the rebels began it: "Duvilomin of Muyenibe, swollen with pride, coveted the lands of Pumi." In wars of independence the suppressor's scribe has no need to reverse the blame, since the truth already suits him.

*The battles.* The record has three, all won by Pumi under Luldominin: the [[evt:2755|Battle of Khungumnu (AY 643)]], the [[evt:2762|Battle of Zhiyiyzhikhti (AY 644)]] and the [[evt:2769|Battle of Khungumnu (AY 645)]]. Kulvanufmalin has all three, correctly placed and dated, with the right victor and the right Pumi commander.

*The dead.* The record gives [[pol:115|Myinive]] 25 dead (6, 6 and 13) from forces of 23, 37 and 37 men. It gives Pumi 988 dead (314, 407 and 267) from armies of roughly three thousand. Kulvanufmalin reports 63 rebel dead (15, 15 and 33), about two and a half times the record in each battle. He reports rebel musters of 62, 99 and 99, about 2.7 times the record. For Pumi he admits 4, 5 and 7 dead, sixteen in all, where the record has 988. The enemy is enlarged less than in many paired cases, but the method is the same: the enemy grows by a steady factor and one's own losses are cut almost to nothing.

*Commanders.* The record names one killed in battle: Dvilomin, the rebel king, at Khungumnu in AY 643. Kulvanufmalin reports it: "Duvilomin himself was killed, and his head was brought to the king." The record also shows two things he leaves out. Leadership of the rising passed by usurpation to Panpavukhin, whom the tablet names only as a commander at Zhiyiyzhikhti. And Panpavukhin was executed at the suppression.

*Outcome and land.* The [[evt:2770|Suppression of the Myinive Revolt]] annexed all twenty-six of the rebel kingdom's districts, with an amnesty that did not reach its king. Kulvanufmalin is unequivocal: "Muyenibe is no more, and its people serve Pumi. A stele was raised to Dumamnul in the capital." But he counts forty-six districts won, three-quarters again the kingdom's extent.

## What the missing rival would have had to say

From the paired cases we know what a defeated rebel's scribe typically does. He blames the overlord for the war, drops or excuses the defeats, multiplies the enemy's dead and turns annexation into an honourable peace with a few villages lost. A Myinive account of this war would have faced unusual difficulties. Its king died in the first battle, a usurper replaced him within the year, and the usurper was executed at the end. The record cannot tell us how Myinive remembered any of this, and I will not invent a memory for it.

What the record does allow is an assessment of the Pumi tablet without a rival. It is reliable on structure: the cause, the battles, the victor and the king's death are all correct. It is unreliable on every quantity. That is the pattern of the paired cases, and it suggests that a single partisan account can be used with confidence for the things its audience witnessed and with none for the things they could not count.
