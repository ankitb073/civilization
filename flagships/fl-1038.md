title: No Man Shall Sell

Numdhith plaited her daughter's hair in the dark, by feel, the way she had done it when the girl was small enough to sit between her knees. Four plaits, bound at the ends with red wool. Then she put her into the good coat, the felt one with the red edging that Numdhith had been married in, and tied it at the throat.

— It's hot down there, Avithnith said. Everybody says it's hot.

— Town people look at your coat before they look at your face.

The girl thought about this and let it stand. She was twelve, and that year she had begun to let things stand, which her mother had noticed and had not liked.

They took the two ewes that could not have gone up with the flock. Tathtun had driven the rest into the high ground ten days before, with the boy, when the last grass on their own hill had gone the colour of ash and then gone altogether; it was the third year without proper rain. The ewes left behind had stood all week at the dry spring with their heads down, as if they were listening for water under the stones. Now they went down the goat-path ahead of the women, stopping when they liked. Nobody hurried them. There was no sense in bringing an animal to market that had fallen on the way.

They came out above the river at sunrise. It was still a river; you could say that for it. But it had drawn back from both its banks like an old man's gums, and the mud it had left was cracked into plates that curled up at the edges, and along the canals the barley stood white and upright and empty. It had come into the ear and the ear had withered, and nobody had troubled to cut it. Numdhith had been told this in the hills and had not believed it. She had thought a farmer would cut anything. Now she saw there was nothing in it to cut.

On the road they met people going the other way. A man pushing a hand-cart, with an old woman sitting in it on the bedding. Two families together, the children carrying the pots. A boy by himself with a cockerel under his arm. Nobody asked where she was going and she did not ask them. That year everybody in the hills was going down to the town, and everybody in the town, it seemed, was leaving it.

* * *

The animal market lay under the wall by the lower gate, and she had never seen it so full. Every herder in [[reg:15|Bani]] had had her thought. Sheep and goats stood in their hundreds on the beaten ground, and a few horses, and they were all the same shape, which was the shape of the frame inside an animal.

The butcher walked down the rows with his hands behind his back. He looked at her ewes for a long time, and then he named a price she would once have been ashamed to ask for a lamb.

— They're dry, he said. Anyone can see they're dry. And look about you. Everybody's selling. Nobody's buying but me.

She took it. She knotted the coins in with the others and the cloth grew heavier by very little. Four [[cur:846|Il]], now, in all.

The grain-sellers kept their row behind the cloth market, under awnings, and that morning only three of them had their shutters up. Numdhith went to the one she had been told to go to, the big one at the end with the carved post, and stood in the line, holding Avithnith by the wrist.

He sold from a wooden measure banded with iron, a stamp burnt into its side. He was a broad slow man with flour in the creases of his knuckles. Behind him, through the door, she could see sacks stacked nearly to the rafters, and a woman moving among them, counting.

— Six, he said to the man in front. Six and a part. Don't look at me like that. I didn't stop the rain.

Six and a part. Numdhith did the sum the way she counted sheep, on her fingers inside her sleeve, and came out where she had known she would come out: four Il would buy her two-thirds of a measure. Two-thirds of a measure would last until — she put the thought down. The woman inside the door had stopped counting. She was looking at Avithnith. Then she looked at Numdhith and nodded, very slightly, the way you nod to someone you have been told about.

Across the street, on the steps of a house with a wheel cut over its lintel, a [[mov:69|Kievi]] teacher in grey was telling a ring of people that the wise withdraw from appetite. Nobody in the ring was arguing with him. Most of them were sitting down. It was easier to listen sitting down.

* * *

The drum came down the street from the upper town, and the whole row turned to it at once, the way a flock turns.

There were four soldiers, two of them with long guns on their shoulders and the smell of slow-match coming off them, and between them a clerk in a dark coat with a roll of paper. He climbed onto the mounting-block by the gate post, unrolled the paper and read it out in the speech of [[set:27|Tushi]], which nobody in the grain row spoke at home:

"By order of the government of Ayshi: Because of the Famine in Baan, no man in Ayshi shall sell grain above 3.59 Il the measure. Whoever hoards grain shall forfeit it."

And then, as if the day mattered as much as the rest: "Done at Tushi, 21 Ash 1986 of the Kievi."

He read it again in the town's speech, slower, and said the number twice. Three and fifty-nine. A man beside Numdhith said it over to himself and then said, three and a half and a bit; and then the whole row was saying it, three and a half and a bit, to each other and to nobody, the way people repeat the name of a child just born.

Numdhith did the sum again inside her sleeve. Four Il. A whole measure, and something over.

She found that she was holding the girl's wrist harder. A whole measure. You could go home on a whole measure. You could go home, the two of you, up the goat-path in the dark, and nobody would have to —

She turned back to the carved post. The grain-seller was not at his measure. He was inside the door with his wife, and the two of them were lifting the shutter-boards into their slots, one, two, three, without hurry, the way you shut up a shop at night. Up and down the row, while the clerk was still climbing down off the block, the other two shutters came down as well.

— Tomorrow, the grain-seller said through the last gap. Come tomorrow.

The clerk had a boy with him carrying a pot of paste. He pasted the paper to the gate post, high up, smoothing it flat with the palm of his hand, stood back to look at it, and went away up the street with his soldiers. The paper stayed. People who could not read came and stood in front of it. Numdhith stood there too. The cheapest barley anyone in the hills had heard of in three years was pasted to a post, and there was not a grain of it to be had.

* * *

The soldiers came back in the afternoon.

Numdhith had not gone anywhere. There was nowhere to go before dark, and the woman had nodded. She sat with Avithnith in the strip of shade under the wall and they shared the last of the dried curd. The girl ate slowly, the way she had been taught, and asked nothing.

This time there were six soldiers. The clerk knocked on the grain-seller's shutter with one knuckle, politely. Then the soldiers took the shutter down.

It was quickly done. The grain-seller stood in his own doorway and said that he was not hoarding, he was going to sell tomorrow, he had said tomorrow, everybody had heard him say tomorrow. The clerk said, quite reasonably, that grain which was not for sale today was grain being kept, and grain being kept was hoarded, and he pointed up at the paper on the post as though the grain-seller might care to read it for himself. The soldiers carried the sacks out on their shoulders and laid them in a cart. The row watched. Nobody pushed forward; the guns were there. It was a strange thing, Numdhith thought, to watch so much barley go by at arm's length and not put out a hand. But the hand stayed where it was.

— Where's it going?

— Tushi, someone said.

— The army, someone else said. They beat them at [[evt:41895|Tiaiosh]] last year, and now at [[evt:41906|Xath]], they say. An army eats whether it wins or not.

— It eats more when it wins.

The cart went off up the high road with a soldier sitting on the sacks. The grain-seller went back into his empty shop and did not put the shutter up again. There was nothing left for it to keep out.

* * *

At dusk the woman came out of the alley behind the shop.

She did not come to them straight. She went to the well-head first with a jar, as if for water, and only on her way back did she stop in front of them and look down at the girl and say, quietly, in the town's speech:

— This is the one?

— This is the one.

— Tiwgalath told you how it is with us.

— She told me.

— Then come.

The house behind the shop was dark and smelled of flour, the way a house goes on smelling for years. In the corner of the kitchen the woman lifted a mat, and a board beneath the mat, and under the board was a jar as tall as Avithnith, sunk to its shoulders in the floor. The soldiers had walked over it at noon.

— If they find it, it's forfeit, the woman said. You heard him. In a jar it's hoarded. In a girl it's eaten, and nobody can forfeit that.

Numdhith held out the knotted cloth with the four Il in it. The woman pushed her hand back without looking at it.

— Don't. Silver makes it a sale.

She filled a sack from the jar. She did not use the iron-banded measure; she used a bowl, and she did not count the bowls aloud, and Numdhith, who counted everything, found she could not count them either.

— Understand me, the woman said, tying the neck of the sack. I sold nothing. You sold nothing. There's no price on it, so nobody can say it was above the queen's price. She'll work in the kitchen and the yard, and carry water, and sleep by the oven where it's warm. When it rains you can come for her.

Numdhith asked the one thing she had come down the hill meaning to ask.

— Does she eat first or last?

The woman looked at her for a moment as though she were a foreigner, which she was.

— With us, she said. What do you take us for?

Avithnith had been standing by the oven the whole while with her hands inside her sleeves, looking at the jar. Now she undid the tie at her throat and took off the good coat and folded it the way she folded it at home, sleeves in, and held it out.

— For Ngiven, she said. He'll want it in the winter. It's warm here.

Numdhith took it, because the girl was right, and because she found she could not make her hands do anything else.

* * *

She went up the goat-path in the dark with the sack on her back and the coat over her arm. The sack was heavy. It was the heaviest thing she had carried since she had carried the girl. She had four Il in a knot of cloth and nothing in all [[pol:1521|Ayshi]] she could spend them on.

Somewhere above the river she found she was saying the clerk's number under her breath. Three and fifty-nine. She did not know what it meant any more than she knew what the paper said; it was a word in the speech of Tushi, and she said it on each step, the way you say the words over a sick animal, until it was nothing but the sound her breath made going in and out. Over the hills the stars had come out, all of them, the way they come out only when there is no water anywhere in the air.

* * *

The paper was still on the gate post the next year when she came down, though the sun had faded it to the colour of the wall. Another had been pasted up beside it. She did not stop to hear it read; she had heard in the lower market what it said. The queen who had set the price was dead, and her sister [[per:41222|Milnim]] was queen in Tushi now.

She went round by the alley. She had brought nothing. She had come to look.

The back door stood open for the air. The girl came out of it with a water jar on her hip, taller, her arms filled out, and saw her mother at the end of the alley, and stopped. Then she did a thing Numdhith had never seen her do. She bent and set the jar down on the step, carefully, with both hands, and turned it a little so that it stood true; for in that house, in that year, nobody spilled water. Then she ran.

She called out first in the town's speech. Halfway down the alley, as if she had remembered where she had put it, she called out in theirs.

**Author's note.** The clerk reads from the edict itself (PAL·DE·00677); its ceiling stood about 30% below the price in the capital. A priest in the neighbouring kingdom wrote that year what the town in this story keeps behind its back door: "Families sold their children for bread" (PAL·RE·00183). The record counts the [[evt:41827|Famine in Baan]], 4367–4369, at about 895,274 dead or fled, 16% of the people. It names one cause, the [[evt:41737|Battle of Nivanga]], fought sixteen years earlier between two other kingdoms, and not the drought. The drought over Numdhith's hills lasted until 4387; [[pol:1521|Ayshi]] fell in 4376.
