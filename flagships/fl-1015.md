title: Seven Grains in the Shoulder
---
The men of [[pol:14|Egu]] were singing again.

Keshelat lay on the roof of her house in the street of the potters, with the child asleep against her side under the one blanket, and listened to them across the dark fields. They sang the way the men of [[set:61|Tsinlampiba]] sang at the sowing, with the long falling note at the end of each line, and the words came over the wall so clearly on the night wind that she could have sung along. She had sung them every autumn of her life.

*Open your hand, lady of the barley. Open your hand over the furrow.*

They were singing to [[dei:36|Nidziktti]], who was her goddess as much as theirs. It was the sowing moon. [[dei:34|Tselni]] stood over the plain nearly full and laid his white road across it, and along the north side of the city the fires of Egu burned in their rows like the lamps of a feast.

She had stopped counting them. In the first siege, when she was a girl, her mother had made her count the enemy's fires every night, as if knowing the number were a kind of wall. This time she knew the only numbers that mattered without counting. Two hundred and forty-eight men of the city had gone out before the gate in the spring, when the barley stood green to the knee, and the men of Egu had been nearly ten for every one of them. Sixty-three had not come back. Numanlan, who mended nets and sang worse than anyone in the street, had been one of the sixty-three. He was out there still, somewhere in the field the men of Egu had reaped, and she had not been able to set a cup of water beside him or say his name to [[dei:38|Shedartsi]] of the house below.

The lord had led the spears out himself, and he had led back the ones who came back, and since that day the gate had been shut.

In the summer they had eaten the oxen: the lord's first, then the plough-oxen of the shrines, then the old dun ox that had trodden the clay for the whole street since before Keshelat was married. When the oxen were gone there was no dung for the cooking fires either, and the kiln at the end of the street had been cold since the barley was cut. Under the floor, wrapped in a rag, she had a lump of silver about a [[cur:13|Bilu]]'s weight that Numanlan had got for a season's fish. In the spring it would have bought a measure of barley at the market and a handful over. Now it bought nothing, because there was nothing to buy.

The lord's mother had gone out to the hill villages before the ring closed. Lultsidzipat next door said that was how you knew the lord had seen it coming. Keshelat thought only that an old woman who had lived through the first sack had the right not to wait for a second.

The child said something in her sleep and turned over. Across the fields the men of Egu came to the end of the song and began it again.

* * *

A boy from the house of Nidziktti came to the street of the potters at first light, asking for the woman who made the seed jars.

The storehouse of the goddess was a long low room behind the shrine, cool even in the summer, with a smell she had known since childhood: dust, and mice, and the sweetness of grain that has been kept dry. The seed jars stood in two rows down its length, sunk in the floor to their shoulders, each one stopped with a cap of clay and the cap pressed with the goddess's seal. Under every rim, where no one looked, was a thumbprint. The jars in the first row had her mother's. The rest had hers.

She and her mother had made them after the first sack, when the old jars had been broken by men looking for silver in them. Keshelat had been sixteen. She had rolled the coils on the floor of her mother's yard and built the jars up round by round, walking round them on her knees, smoothing the joins with a shell and her wet palm, and burnishing the shoulders with a river pebble until they shone like the skin of a fish. Every harvest after that, while there were harvests, the priests had filled them with the fattest ears of the year, the goddess's share, set aside before anyone ate, and every harvest she had come with her bag of fine clay to make the caps fit.

Two priests were waiting by the jars. With them, in a plain wool cloak with a bronze pin at the shoulder, was [[per:782|Tsibulun]], lord of the city.

She had never stood so near him. He was a tall man going grey, with a hollow under each cheekbone that had not been there at the spring feast. People in the street said he was as old as the gate his grandfather had built, and took comfort in it, because the gate was still standing. He had no wife and no son. His mother had gone to the hills. He looked, she thought, like a man who has been awake for a month and has decided not to mention it.

"You made these," he said.

"My mother made the first row, lord. I made the rest."

"Can you open them so that they can be closed again?"

She looked at the priests. The older one had his hands folded in his sleeves and was looking at the floor. The younger one was looking at the lord.

"If I have water," she said, "and a thin blade. The caps will come away whole if they are softened first. The shoulders will not crack."

"Then open them like that."

The younger priest moved his hand, as if he would put it on the nearest seal, and then let it fall. The older one did not look up. Keshelat waited for one of them to say the word that would stop it, the word that belonged to them and not to the lord, the word about the goddess's share and the order of things and what was owed to whom. Nobody said it. The priests did not forbid it.

They brought her a bowl of water and she saw the younger priest hesitate before he set it down, because water too was counted now. She wet her fingers and worked them round the edge of the first cap until the clay went soft and grey, and then she slid the bone blade in under it and walked it round, slowly, the way you would ease a splinter out of a child's foot. The cap came up whole in her hands. The seal of the goddess was on it, a woman holding two ears of barley, crisp as the day it had been pressed.

The smell came up out of the jar like breath.

The seed in it was two harvests old, from the last harvest the city had reaped for itself. Twice since then the city had sown, and twice the men of Egu had reaped. What was left lay a forearm's depth below the shoulder, pale and dry and perfect, the best barley of a good year, and when the older priest finally knelt and put his hand into it, she saw his lips move, though she could not hear what he said.

She opened every jar. She set every cap face down on a cloth so the seals would not be rubbed. By the time she had finished, the women of the city were standing in the yard outside in a line that went out of the gate of the shrine and down the street, and the priests were measuring the seed into bowls, a bowl for each house, and not speaking.

When it was done the lord stood a while looking down into the last of the jars, as if there were something at the bottom of it he had been told to look for.

"What goes down comes up again; the priests have always said so."

He said it to the priests. Neither of them answered him. After a moment he turned and went out by the side door, and Keshelat heard his steps on the stair that went up to the wall.

She gathered the caps on their cloth and gave them to the younger priest. "Keep them dry," she said. "Keep them in order. They'll fit again."

He looked at her as though she had said something in a foreign tongue. Then he took them.

* * *

The child had never had bread like it.

They ground it on the quern in the yard at dusk, Keshelat kneeling at the high end and working the rubbing-stone, the child sweeping the grain back under it with the side of her hand, and the flour that came off was white and fine and smelled of summer. Keshelat baked it on a sherd over a fire of the last of the reed mats. It was the best bread they had eaten since the spring. That was the part she could not stop thinking about: that the goddess had kept back the best of every year for this, and this was what it was.

"Is it Nidziktti's barley?" the child asked, with her mouth full.

"It was."

"Is she angry?"

"Eat," Keshelat said. "She gave it to you."

The child ate. Then she said, the way children ask the one question you have been walking round all day, "What will they plant, then? When the men of Egu go home?"

Keshelat did not answer. She got up and went into the back room, where under a wet cloth in a pit lined with sherds she had kept a lump of good river clay all summer, wetting it with water she could not spare, which was foolish, which Numanlan would have told her was foolish. She brought out a piece the size of two fists and sat down with it by the fire.

She had held back seven grains from the quern. She had not decided to; she had found them in her palm.

She rolled the clay into coils and built a jar no taller than her hand, the way her mother had taught her, round by round, turning it on her knee, pinching each coil into the one below. She smoothed it with her thumb. She shaped the shoulder and the little mouth. Then she took the grains one at a time and pressed them into the wet shoulder, side by side in a row, deep enough that the clay closed a little over their backs.

The child watched her.

With a thorn Keshelat lifted them out again, one at a time, and where each had been there was a small clean hollow in the clay, pointed at both ends, with a line down the middle of it like the fold of a closed eye. Seven of them, in a row round the shoulder of the jar. She put the seven grains into the child's palm.

"Eat them."

"Why did you put them in?"

"So it knows what it's for," Keshelat said. "When there's wood, we'll fire it. Then it keeps the shape. Even when there isn't any barley, it will know the shape of barley."

The child looked at the grains in her hand for a long moment, and then she ate them, one at a time, chewing each one carefully, the way you eat something you have been told is important.

Later, on the roof, with the child asleep again and the jar drying on the shelf above the door, Keshelat lay and listened to the men of Egu begin their song. She found she was singing it with them, under her breath, the same words, because there were no other words.

*Open your hand, lady of the barley. Open your hand over the furrow.*

There was no wood that autumn, nor through the winter. The little jar stood on its shelf above the door with the shape of seven grains in its shoulder, waiting for the fire she had promised it.

The fire, when it came, was not hers. It was hot enough.

**Author's note.** The seed jars come from one line in Tsibulun's letter to his mother (PAL·LT·01723): "We ate the oxen. We ate the seed-grain." The record gives the [[evt:605|Battle of Zhinlampive]] of AY 252, 248 men against nearly ten times as many, 63 lost, but nothing of the [[evt:606|Second Siege of Zhinlampive]] beyond its year. His grandfather's gate is PAL·IN·03347; his father's siege is PAL·LT·01730. The next year Tsibulun went out again and lost, and the same letter says the men of Egu set the street of the potters burning.
