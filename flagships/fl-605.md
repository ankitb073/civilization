The tomb lintel from the royal tombs of Kabave is the last object made for him, and it says almost nothing. "Nakagum, king of Puvu, son of Nakagum, dwells in this tomb." The lines below are worn past reading. That a king later called the Great should be remembered on his own tomb only as his father's son is an accident of weathering. It is a useful warning all the same. [[per:7086|Nakagum II]] reigned over [[pol:178|Puvu]] for sixty-two years, from AY 1125 to 1187. What survives of him is mostly stone and tablet: boundary stones, treaty texts, a scribe's word list. Read together, they show a reign with a single purpose. His name meant "road", and the documents of his reign are about roads.

## The usurper's grandson

He was born in 1120, a Kumbumbi prince of a young house. His grandfather, Nambumbi, had seized the throne in 1098 and is remembered as the Usurper. His father, [[per:6915|Nakagum I]], reigned from 1105 and died in 1125, not yet forty. His mother was [[per:7084|Namumbe]]. An elder brother had died in childhood before Nakagum was born. The [[evt:6785|Accession of Nakagum II]] made a five-year-old king of an empire of some 3.8 million people, with Namumbe as regent. His only younger brother died in 1131, aged ten, and his mother in 1132. At twelve he was the last of his parents' household.

The first war of his reign was fought in his name before he could have understood it. The [[evt:6793|Second Khishkhih Trade War]] of 1125–1127 was waged against the small kingdom of [[pol:297|Kanjidhu]] and its king [[per:7062|Namandane]], who was killed in battle in 1127. The court account, written the next year, claims that the enemy brought seventy and eighty men to the field and that Puvu lost a handful. The record puts Puvu's dead at nearly seventeen hundred and Kanjidhu's at fifteen. The treaty explains why a regent would pay that price. The [[evt:6828|Treaty of Anjith (AY 1127)]] survives on a silver tablet written by the scribe Nasuve. It makes a river the boundary and then states its real purpose: "The merchants of Puvu shall pass through the land of Kanchidu unhindered; no toll shall be taken of them and no man shall seize their goods."

## Wars for the road

The pattern held for forty years. The [[evt:6889|Puvu–Luyükhizi War]] against the larger [[pol:252|Luyükhizi]] empire ended in 1136 with the [[evt:6916|Treaty of Luyügih (AY 1136)]], which gave Puvu thirteen districts, the town of Divnüzith and a tribute. Luyügih was sacked that year. Two boundary stones from the conquered district of Gishugizi, set up in 1136 and 1147, are among the best preserved objects of the reign. Each names the king and calls on a god to curse the enemy. One is weathered and the other almost pristine, and the second is a reminder that the border had to be fought for twice.

Kanjidhu went to war again in 1145, and the twenty-five-year-old king took the field himself. At the [[evt:7022|Battle of Luyügih (AY 1146)]] he led about eleven thousand men against 252. The court account inflates the enemy to 640. Namandane's son [[per:7146|Navavun]], whom later ages called the Ill-Starred, is named as signatory of the [[evt:7039|Treaty of Anjith (AY 1147)]] and died in battle the same year, aged twenty-five. The Puvu account says his head was brought to the king. The tablet of that treaty is lost except for its opening words, "Kanchidu came to my feet", and Anjith was sacked.

In 1153 Nakagum turned south against [[pol:301|Fil]] in the [[evt:7102|Second Pyivi Trade War]]. He commanded at Ajü in 1154 and 1156 and won both times. The town of Hil was besieged and then sacked. The [[evt:7143|Treaty of Hil (AY 1158)]], again on silver and again in the hand of a palace scribe, made Fil a vassal and its king [[per:7291|Naladhunji]] the friend of Puvu's friends and the enemy of its enemies. Once more it guaranteed toll-free passage for Puvu's merchants. Its curse is the most memorable of the reign: whoever alters it, "may the river refuse him water and the field refuse him grain."

## The household and the empire

The tablets do not mention grief, but the record does. His eldest son died in 1156, aged fourteen, in the middle of the war with Fil. A second son died in 1158 in his first year. His queen died in 1164. Of his children only a daughter, Nachügü, born in 1153, lived to adulthood.

The year his queen died he began the [[evt:7193|First Khanji Trade War]] against the city-state of [[pol:289|Lamlivje]]. It is the war that shows the reign at its least admirable. Twice, in 1165 and 1167, he brought more than ten thousand men against fewer than five hundred, and won both battles. Even so, Puvu lost nearly 4,800 men in the war and Lamlivje 211. Lamlivzhe was sacked in 1166 and again in 1167. The [[evt:7223|Treaty of Lamlivzhe (AY 1167)]] was sworn by the young [[per:7578|Nakange III]], who had just come to the throne. It imposed four talents of silver a year for forty years, and a clause that no man "shall plough beyond the stones". Its merchants' clause is word for word the one in the treaties of Anjith and Hil. By then this was the formula of the house.

What this meant for the empire can be measured. In the course of his reign Puvu grew from about 220 districts to more than 250, and its population from 3.8 to some 4.6 million. Its treasury, by the record's reckoning, rose by about seventy per cent. The urban population hardly grew. Silver from tribute and tolls went to the palace, not to new towns. A word list made in 1170 by the scribe Nakanu, for clerks who dealt with the people across the border, gives the foreign words for friend, bread, silver, salt, horse and road. It is the working document of an empire of traders, and it tells us what such clerks needed to say.

## The defender

The last war of his reign was not his choice. In 1173 Nelo, under the usurper [[per:7570|Nakazno]], invaded Puvu, and the [[evt:7290|Nelo–Puvu War]] lasted five years. Nakagum, past fifty, commanded at all three battles and won them, the last at Shyithe in 1178 against the young general Navande. The [[evt:7331|Treaty of Shyithe (AY 1178)]] made Nelo pay an indemnity. The Nelo account of the war, written in 1180, reverses everything. It has Nakagum breaking his fathers' oaths, its own king taking pity on "the pleas of Povo", and the honour of the realm preserved. Only a fragment of the treaty survives, its truce clause and its list of gods.

He died a natural death in 1187, aged sixty-seven. The [[evt:7418|Accession of Nachügü]] put his daughter on the throne. The law of Puvu favoured sons, and none survived. [[per:7584|Nachügü]], remembered as the Just, reigned only three years and died at thirty-seven, and the crown passed to a child under a regent. The house of Nambumbi held the throne for almost another century, until a usurper took it in 1274, and Puvu itself lasted until 1307.

## A verdict in stone

An archaeologist learns to distrust what is easy to find. The boundary stones and silver tablets of Nakagum II survive because they were made to last, and they speak for the palace. But they are unusually consistent. Across forty years and four treaties the clause that matters is the same one, that the merchants of Puvu shall pass unhindered. He fought small neighbours with very large armies, lost far more men than he killed, and outlived his wife and sons. The empire he left was richer and larger than the one his mother had held for him. The road was kept open, at a cost the tablets do not record.
