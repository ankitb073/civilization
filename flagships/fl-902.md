title: The House That Was Six (AY 250–500)

Some time around AY 498 a singer in Taulin composed a lament in five songs for a state that no longer existed (PAL·PO·00023, *Lament for Yida, the House That Was Six*). The fourth song walks through the map of the singer's own day:

> Six daughters, and each says: "I was never a daughter."  
> Six houses, and each says: "I was never one house."

Then the singer turns from kings to the market. "In the days of Yida a man came to the market with one tunrun, and he went home with nearly two measures of grain on his ass. Now in each market there is a different weight. Now in each gate there is a different toll. The merchant weeps, and the ass weeps with him." The memory is accurate. A tunrun was about 9.6 grams of silver, and in the last years of [[pol:25|Yida]] a measure of grain at [[set:0|Taulin]] cost about 6.

The lament mourns the largest state the world had yet seen. In AY 480 Yida ruled 8.7 million people in 269 districts. Its treasury held some 384,000 kilograms of silver, and its army numbered more than 52,000. A year later it had broken into six. This chapter asks how that happened, and why so many other kingdoms of the age went the same way.

## The heir in Taulin (AY 250–343)

The world of this chapter was fuller than the last. The people living under states rose from 27.4 million in AY 250 to 35.3 million in 370 and 43.5 million in 490. The number of states fell from 24 to 21 and then rose to 31 as the great ones broke apart. Even in AY 370, 59 per cent of the land still lay outside any state. The largest, Yida and [[pol:8|Navnga]], each held about one twentieth of it, [[pol:6|Ana]] a twenty-fifth.

Yida, the heir of Tallin, lived by tribute. It sacked Zhinontunu twice, in the [[evt:663|Sack of Zhinontunu (AY 275)]] and the [[evt:674|Sack of Zhinontunu (AY 278)]], and in the [[evt:699|Yida–Lenunushal War]] (285–293) it forced [[pol:10|Lenunushal]] to pay. Lenunushal's palace chronicle claims that at Linnshal in AY 287 its army killed 10,804 of the enemy and lost 84 (PAL·CH·00042). A Yida victory song admits the same battle as a defeat: "Two thousand eight hundred and thirty of Yida lay down in the water" (PAL·PO·00055). The record gives Yida 2,830 dead and Lenunushal 272. Here the victor's poet told the truth and the loser's chronicler did not. The Lenunushal account then turns five defeats into treachery or orderly withdrawals and ends, "out of mercy for the common folk, the king agreed to terms". Those terms ceded 22 districts and promised 943 kilograms of silver a year for 34 years.

The song names the woman who finished that war, the queen [[per:957|Shenshetkuth]], "the young lioness", who took up the spear of her elder sister in AY 292 and reigned for 51 years. Her great test came from Navnga. In AY 312 its king, urged on by the priests of the Retti gods, marched into [[reg:16|Yidhe]] to warm the altars of his gods in the land of the [[mov:2|Jinru]]. The [[evt:811|Holy War of Navnga against Yida]] began well for him with a victory at Nin in AY 313. Then it went very badly. An old spearman of Navnga remembered the next battle: "The sun went down on that field and we were not standing. My brother did not come back from Virathkanu. I counted fires that night and there were too few fires." (PAL·CH·08463, *The Tablet of an Old Spearman of Navnga*.) The record counts 4,992 Navnga dead there. At Shivange in AY 315 the king himself fell. Shenshetkuth's victory stele claims 9,400 enemy dead; the record gives 3,164 (PAL·IN·00024). The carver's formula was so fixed that it calls the queen "he". By the Treaty of Shivange in 317 Navnga gave up no land, only trading rights and its pride. It had lost 11,074 men to Yida's 4,259.

The holy war had a long tail. The measure of order in Navnga, already low at 0.16, fell to 0.02, and it names the defeats of 314–317 among the causes of the [[evt:929|Secession of Vuvo]] in AY 335, when a claimant named Pufdun took a quarter of the kingdom. [[per:1092|Kamagonen]], the dead king's young son, crushed the rebellion by AY 340. "So the face of the gods has come back to Navnga," the old spearman concluded. It had not, quite.

[[pol:14|Egu]] under [[per:813|Patun I]] (253–295) opened the period with the [[evt:613|Second Sack of Zhinlampive]] and swallowed the city-state of Tsinlampiba. [[pol:30|Yi'otl]] was founded in AY 301 and would last until 760. In Ana, [[per:700|Silla]] reigned until 283 and [[per:1273|Yuekwkasimen]] for 67 years (347–414). In [[pol:0|Shue'ethie]] the queen [[per:660|Mononna]] reigned until 266, and a later queen, [[per:1067|Sho'e Luesh'a]], for 61 years (307–368). The [[evt:616|Onset of the Great Drought in Pyivi (AY 262)]] parched 206 districts. The climate was still mild: the 280s and 290s were the warmest decades of the chapter.

## The cold turn (AY 320–398)

Then the weather turned. The 320s were 0.28 °C colder than the long-run mean, and Mount I'amikhil erupted from AY 328 to 332. The 330s and 340s were the driest decades of the chapter. The [[evt:904|Onset of the Great Drought in Mikhukhizhi (AY 333)]] spread across 282 districts and lasted until 387. Famine followed. In Tsiey in AY 334 it killed 157,788 subjects of Tivintusa, and the record names two causes, the drought and the Navnga invasion that had fought over the same fields in 325. In Khishkhih in AY 344 it killed 240,059 people in the Kadhdazhile Empire. In the 340s a measure of grain cost 36 grams of silver at Luyululi, 34 at Adhgumgi and 32 at Biniynge, against about 7 at Taulin. A second drought, the [[evt:1023|Onset of the Great Drought in Baan (AY 364)]], dried 264 districts for half a century.

Kingdoms cracked too, though not because of the weather. In AY 333 the [[evt:923|Secession of Miniushti]] split Tivintusa; the record blames defeats by Navnga and a treaty of 331 that cost fourteen districts and 27 years of tribute. The crown recovered the province in two years. Navnga itself was the aggressor in these years, and it grew. Under [[per:1313|Kukathren]] (357–394) it reached 4.6 million people and 160 districts by AY 390. Yida, meanwhile, survived a usurper and found another fighting queen in Ayiviyath I. Her [[evt:1006|Second Yida–Duvekopuvinga War]] (356–363) won 18 districts, ten years of tribute and the sack of Himi from [[pol:26|Duvekopuvinga]]. The court chronicle admits about two thousand Yida dead in all (PAL·CH·00095); the record gives 8,248.

In AY 368 Kukathren went to war with Yida. The [[evt:1045|Navnga–Yida War]] was the hardest war of the century. In AY 371 he wrote home from the field at Binge (PAL·LT·01657). Yida's army had come out seventeen thousand strong, and "their queen Ayiviyath fell among her spearmen. I counted the hands of their slain: six thousand and five hundred I counted." The record agrees: 17,152 men, 6,495 dead, and the queen among them. "Of my own men, few fell," he added. The record says 2,637. He asked the treasury for ten weights of silver for every house whose son had died, which suggests he knew the number was not small. Navnga won again at Binge in 372. Then the war turned: Yida won at Himi in 373, at Shivange in 376 and at Binge in 377, and in 378 Kukathren and the new queen [[per:1376|Ayiviyath II]] put their names to a silver tablet: "Each shall keep what he held before the war" (PAL·TR·00040). Navnga had lost 12,660 men and Yida 17,589.

Kukathren died in 394. Four years later came the [[evt:1153|Collapse of the Navnga State]], at the height of its size. Among the causes the record lists are the Secession of Vuvo, sixty years before, and the lost battles at Himi, Shivange and Binge. Navnga broke into four. A new [[pol:40|Navnga]] empire kept the capital at [[set:10|Navnge]], beside [[pol:41|Vuvo]], [[pol:42|Nimiti]] and a short-lived Nothinga.

## Kingdoms that fell in a row (AY 389–453)

Navnga's fall began a run of collapses that would end with Yida's. Most followed a pattern: lost battles, often in wars of the state's own choosing, frequently a usurper or a breakaway province, and a split that came, more than once, just as the state reached its greatest size.

Shue'ethie came first. In the [[evt:1110|Talenonoy–Shue'ethie War]] (389–398) the kingdom of [[pol:19|Talenonoy]], ruled by [[per:1317|Himttumtibun]], attacked it at the fords of [[reg:15|Baan]]. Both courts wrote their chronicles within four years of the peace, and a modern study has shown that each scribe scaled the true figures by his own fixed ratio, battle after battle (PAL·SP·00018, *Scaled by Rule*). The record gives a costly and fairly even war: 5,100 Talenonoy dead and 6,706 Shue'ethie. The Shue'ethie queen fell at Viradhuthpi in 395, and by the Treaty of Khuele her successor [[per:1439|Tlamfen Lieze]] gave up six districts and 28,975 kilograms of silver. Shue'ethie's measure of order fell to zero and stayed there. It reached its greatest size, 3.1 million people in 98 districts, in AY 430. Four years later the [[evt:1294|Collapse of the Shue'ethie State]] left a kingdom of [[pol:46|Tomözel]] and the city-state of [[pol:47|Tlaiya]], and Tlamfen Lieze is remembered as "the Last".

[[pol:17|Wal'a]], seated at [[set:22|Bau]], had grown steadily under long reigns such as [[per:1053|Yalni Shimin]] (301–338) and [[per:1133|Yi Lishi]] (338–375). It won a war of conquest against Wa'mi in 425–434 but lost its two largest battles, and a usurper took the throne in 427. The [[evt:1344|Collapse of the Wal'a State]] came in 443, three years after its peak, and left [[pol:49|I'iti]] and the [[pol:50|Tantöti]] Empire.

Talenonoy's turn came next. After defeats by Egu and Virnado in 412–414, the [[evt:1245|Secession of Weyo]] split the kingdom in 419. Its last long-reigning king, [[per:1568|Nuhuanan]], then picked a fight with Yida over the trade road of [[reg:8|Diebeguvebenga]]. His letter from the camp before Biniynge in AY 442 is a masterpiece of the genre (PAL·LT·01658). At Bimnge in 438, he writes, "six thousand of Yida and more I struck down. Of mine, barely six hundred." For once a king's boast was true, and so, unusually, are his other figures: the record counts 6,266 Yida dead and 601 of his own. But the letter also shows a king in trouble. The enemy now came "with twenty thousand. Twenty thousand! Every man of Yida who can lift a stick." There was whispering in the city, and he needed a thousand measures of grain at "one tyektsi and a little more" each, a little over 9.3 grams of silver, where the record gives 10.5 at [[set:79|Taalinnuy]]. Yida won six of the war's eight battles. In the [[evt:1299|Fourth Diebeguvebenga Trade War]] Yida lost 18,348 men and Talenonoy 8,722, yet it was Talenonoy that paid. The Treaty of Bimnge in 445 cost it 854 talents of silver, about 25,600 kilograms (PAL·TR·00060). Eight years later came the [[evt:1400|Collapse of the Talenonoy State]], which left a [[pol:54|Talenonoy]] Empire, [[pol:55|Yimola]] and the city of Bimunga. In Nilu meanwhile the [[evt:1329|Secession of Shavè]] had divided that old kingdom in 439.

## The silver of Egu (AY 455–481)

Yida came through all this richer and larger. Its king [[per:1406|Nonakhlun]] reigned for 45 years (405–450) and earned the name "the Victorious". But the record shows the same slow decay we saw in Tallin: Yida's measure of order fell from 0.66 in AY 250 to 0.33 in 400 and 0.22 in 450.

The greatest war of the age began in AY 455, when Yida marched on Egu to "bring me the silver of Egu", as a hymn of 464 puts it (PAL·PO·00002, *Hymn to Vulme for the Silver of Egu*). Egu was then at its height, with 3.1 million people in 132 districts under the queen [[per:1623|Lulyibiyat]] "the Fair". The [[evt:1417|Yida–Egu War]] lasted eight years and was fought in eight great battles, with Yida fielding between 13,000 and 18,000 men in each. Yida lost two of them, at Bimnge in 457 and at Ture in 459, where 4,772 of its men died. The hymn turns these into pauses: "Yida drew back, as the ox draws back from the furrow to draw breath." It won the other six, killing the Egu general Vamun at Zhivampive in 461. By the Treaty of Shiniy in 463, Egu gave up 37 districts and three towns and promised 62 talents of silver a year for forty years (PAL·TR·00069). The price was 20,083 Yida dead and 11,771 of Egu's.

Then the weather turned against everyone. The [[evt:1463|Onset of the Great Drought in Daadüdhih (AY 468)]] spread across 487 districts, and the record ties two famines to it: 89,446 dead in Ayshi and 75,468 in Khanji. The 470s and 480s were a quarter of a degree colder than the long-run mean. In AY 475 a plague remembered as the Shaking Sickness broke out at Dambakhu and ran through a dozen towns of Tagath and Kavu, killing 551,972 people, almost a third of those it reached. It was the deadliest single event of the chapter, and it struck far from the great powers' battlefields.

Yida kept fighting. In the [[evt:1530|Yida–Vuvo War]] (472–475) it sent 27,687 men against 2,372 at Poynge, made Vuvo a vassal and imposed 34 years of tribute. Vuvo's court annals mention one of the nine battles it fought in that century. Of this war they say only that "Yida broke the peace" and later that "the land had rest" (PAL·AN·00043, *Annals of Vuvo, AY 400–499*). Yet by the 470s the record's measure of order in Yida stood at zero. Three rulers held the throne in twelve years, and in AY 476 a man named Talon seized it. In AY 480 the [[evt:1584|Collapse of the Egu State]] left a new [[pol:58|Egu]] that would last until 840, beside [[pol:59|Yinnga]] and Duegowinga. Yida broke a year later. The [[evt:1589|Collapse of the Yida State]] produced the six daughters of the lament: a [[pol:61|Tallin]] Empire in the old capital, the kingdom of [[pol:62|Buneynga]], Pumi, Tupchinga, Chinontonu and the city of Punennushal.

The record's list of causes is the most instructive in this chapter. It names Talon's usurpation. It also names four battles: Bimnge in 438 and Biniynge in 441, both lost to Talenonoy, and Bimnge in 457 and Ture in 459, both lost to Egu. Yida won both of those wars. It collapsed from the battles it lost on the way to winning them, a generation later, just as Tallin had. The lament, characteristically, remembers only the dead: "At Ture they fell, and the stones were red."

## Gods, wheels and new tongues

Behind the wars, the slower history of belief went on. No faith held more than a tenth of the land. At AY 370 [[mov:1|Ya'cuam]] held 9 per cent, [[mov:9|Menibe]] and [[mov:0|La'im]] 8 each and [[mov:15|Imom]] 7. Rulers could change gods quickly. [[pol:22|Virnado]] adopted Lelmi in AY 234 and, 41 years later, made the [[evt:678|Conversion of Virnado to Lona]], returning to its old faith. The [[evt:953|Conversion of Mieshlothie to Uem]] came in 335 and the [[evt:954|Conversion of Yeshtakshal to Lelmi]] in 336. Vuvo, newly independent, made the [[evt:1198|Conversion of Vuvo to Jinru]] in 402, taking the faith of Taulin; its annalist records it in six words, "The king turned to the Jinru." The successor states adopted faiths of their own: Nimiti took [[mov:13|Onsatlmom]] in 405, Tlaiya Imom in 446, Tantöti [[mov:17|Nishmam]] in 451 and the kingdom of [[pol:53|Tsuka]] Menibe in 466. Holy war, too, became a habit. Navnga's against Yida was followed by Shue'ethie's against Nimiti (416–418) and Egu's against Tupujinga (425–428).

Ideas still crossed frontiers slowly. The wheel, first made in Shue'ethie in AY 186, had to be invented again in Ana a century later, by a woman named [[per:918|Sho'e To'a]] in the [[evt:682|Invention of the wheel in Ana]] (287), and again at Luyulule in 468. Writing was invented independently at Tahine in 301 and at Tagath in 410. Scripts moved faster. The Khidhamkhulu script, adopted at Tahine in 305, was carved on Yida's own victory steles within a dozen years; Tilcui took up the Ni'samli script in 308 and Luyulule the Khidhamladhle in 366. Two new languages emerged in the record, Injèlu from Khukhande around AY 399 and Veruldu from Balman around 499, and 46 sound changes are dated to the period. Trade grew in spite of the wars: 24 merchant houses were founded. Grain at Taulin cheapened from about 9 grams of silver a measure in the 270s to under 6 in the 480s. A mina of tin fell on average from 65 grams to 49, and bronze grew cheaper with it.

The chapter ends among the heirs. The new Tallin under [[per:1898|Nufmalmagunin]] the Stout and then the queen [[per:1953|Unath I]] held 3.2 million people in AY 490. In 488 it had to cede nineteen districts and pay 29 years of tribute to Vuvo, which had been Yida's vassal thirteen years before. Ana, which peaked at 4.9 million people in AY 450, passed in 479 to the queen [[per:1883|Nuezanieme]]. The new Navnga empire under [[per:1755|Vakun]] had grown to 2.9 million. In AY 493 the people of La'im won their freedom from the second kingdom of Tomözel. Their king's report of the oath is the last word of the age (PAL·LT·01675). The old king of Tomözel "was silent a long time. His counsellors spoke in his ear. Then he said: 'Let it be so. La'im is La'im; Tomözel is Tomözel.'" In AY 498 the singer in Taulin closed the lament with a prayer: "Turn, wheel, turn, and bring back the one house." It never came back. A year later the [[evt:1639|Onset of the Great Drought in Daadüdhih (AY 499)]] began. The next chapter belongs to Navnga, to Yi'otl and to the long-lived Kadhdazhile.
