*[[per:24479|Nangash]], emperor of [[pol:967|Khishkhis]], who went in his own person as envoy to the enemy, to the elders of the council in [[set:85|Nambe]]: this report of the negotiations, in the year 222 of Khishkhis.*

Fathers of the council,

Some of you counselled me, when I set out last year for the camp before Agü, that an emperor ought not to be his own envoy; that it was beneath the dignity of the throne to sit at a table with a defeated king and haggle with him like a wool-merchant over the price of a fleece. I answered then, and I answer now, that there is no dignity so great as a good peace, and no envoy so well able to obtain one as the man who has just won the battle. You will judge from what follows whether I was right.

First, let me remind you how the matter stood. The [[evt:24758|13th Pyivi Trade War]] was not of our making. The men of [[pol:1069|Pyif]] had closed the roads of Pyivi to our merchants, seized their goods at the fords, and laughed at our protests. Twelve wars before this one have been fought over the roads of Pyivi; the men of Pyif might have learned from that history what it costs to close them. They are slow pupils.

At the [[evt:24841|First Battle of Hil]] our generals swept their outposts from the field. In the same year came the [[evt:24842|Sack of Ajü]], and the city learned what it costs to defy us. Yet still [[per:24515|Shel]], their king, would not treat. So I went myself.

At the [[evt:24887|Battle of Ajü]] I commanded in person. I will not boast, Fathers; the field itself will boast for me. We came on with a host that filled the valley from hill to hill; they met us with a rabble that could not fill a market-square. Their line broke at the first charge. Their dead lay so thick before the walls that my horse could not find clean ground to step on, and the vultures of three provinces came to the feast. Of ours, some fell, as men must in war, and I have paid their widows from my own purse. But the gods of the [[mov:16|Ninzhe]] were plainly with us: [[dei:107|Nizhinande]] in the charge, and [[dei:104|Shilim]] in the counsel that followed.

Then we sat down before the gate in the [[evt:24888|Siege of Ajü]], and within days Shel sent out his heralds.

Now to the negotiations. We met in a tent between the lines at [[set:169|Agü]]. Shel came with four counsellors and a face like a man attending his own funeral. He began, as defeated men always begin, by speaking of the ancient friendship between our peoples. I let him speak for an hour. Then I laid before him my terms, and told him that the tent would be struck at sunset whether he had signed or not.

These are the terms of the [[evt:24889|Treaty of Ajü]], which he signed before the sun went down:

That Pyif cede to Khishkhis the lands about Hil, with the town itself, so that the roads of Pyivi may never again be closed against us.

That Pyif pay an indemnity in silver, to be counted at the rate of the [[cur:541|Danzhi]], for the goods seized from our merchants.

That the king of Pyif hold his throne henceforth as the vassal of the emperor of Khishkhis, and render him homage.

That our merchants pass freely through all the lands of Pyif, and trade there on the terms they please.

That there be peace between the two crowns for fifteen years.

Some of you will say I was too hard. I say that I was exactly as hard as was needed, and not one grain harder. A gentle peace with a treacherous enemy is merely a war postponed. A hard peace, fairly kept, is a lesson remembered.

Some of you will say I was too lenient, that I ought to have taken Agü itself and ended the house of Shel. To you I say: a vassal who pays is worth more than a ruin that does not.

Fathers, I am young, and I know that some of you thought me too young. I ask you now to weigh the [[evt:24889|Treaty of Ajü]] and not my years. I commend it, and myself, to the council, and the council to the gods.
