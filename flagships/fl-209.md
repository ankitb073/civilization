title: One God, Two Victories: The Khovave Chronicles of the Third Kadhdazhile–Luyulule War

Rival chronicles usually reach the translator in two languages. Those of the [[evt:2356|Third Kadhdazhile–Luyulule War]] (AY 587–595) are both in Khovave. Both spell the empire "Kadhazhile", and both credit their victories to the same god, Kumbe, in the same words: "Kumbe gave them into our hands". This makes the pair a clean experiment. With idiom held constant, everything that differs between the tablets is a choice. I will argue that the choices are mirror images, each scribe keeping exactly the battles the other drops.

## Context and tablets

This was the third round of a long contest over [[reg:20|Khishkhih]]. In the [[evt:2172|Second Kadhdazhile–Luyulule War]] (AY 563–567), [[pol:69|Luyulule]] had already ceded twenty-one districts there and accepted a tribute due to run until AY 599. The [[evt:2261|Revolt of Khudüle]] in AY 577 had weakened it further. The Kadhdazhile account (PAL·CH·00321, excavated at Adhizhili in AY 4889) is Navavun's, written in AY 598, three years after the peace. The Luyulule account (PAL·CH·00322, excavated at Luyululi in AY 4885) is Najime's, written in AY 607, a year before the kingdom's end. Both men were adults during the war.

A warning for indexers: Navavun the Kadhdazhile chronicler shares his name with the Luyulule general Navavun. The general won the one battle the chronicler leaves out.

## Field by field

*Who began it.* The record says [[pol:18|Kadhdazhile]] attacked, to take territory. Najime agrees: "there came word that Nazhige of Kadhazhile had gathered an army against Luyulule." Navavun reverses it with the familiar formula: "Nanzhizhim of Luyulule, forgetting the oaths his fathers swore, set his hand against Kadhazhile."

*The battles.* The record has four. Luyulule won the [[evt:2389|Battle of Luyululi (AY 592)]]. Kadhdazhile won the [[evt:2395|Battle of Luyululi (AY 593)]], the [[evt:2402|Battle of Yilish (AY 594)]], where King Nazhige commanded in person, and the [[evt:2411|Battle of Luyululi (AY 595)]]. Navavun has the three Kadhdazhile victories and nothing else. Najime has the one Luyulule victory and nothing else. Neither mentions the [[evt:2412|Third Siege of Luyululi]], with which the war ended. Laid side by side, the two tablets reconstruct the full sequence of battles with no overlap at all.

*The dead.* The record gives Kadhdazhile 3,788 dead and Luyulule 1,172. Navavun reports 3,608 enemy dead (1,497, 1,553 and 558), each about three and a half times the record's figure (429, 445 and 160). His enemy musters are inflated by about two and a half. For his own side he discounts the three battles he reports, and he leaves out entirely the costliest engagement of the war, Luyululi in AY 592, where the record has 1,353 Kadhdazhile dead. Omission did as much work as arithmetic. Najime's single battle runs the other way: "word came that Navande had reached Luyulule with 9,682 men ... The scribes counted 3,424 enemy dead; we lost 59." The record gives 6,834 men, and losses of 1,353 against 138.

*Commanders.* None was killed. Neither tablet claims a kill.

*Outcome and land.* Kadhdazhile won. The [[evt:2413|Treaty of Luyululi (AY 595)]] ceded six districts and imposed a ten-year truce. Here the mirror is exact, because each scribe has the other side doing the pleading. Navavun has "Luyulule sent envoys with gifts and pleas", followed by twelve districts "returned to Kadhazhile". Najime has "out of mercy for the common folk, the king accepted the pleas of Kadhazhile", followed by "Some villages of no account were given up, which had always been poor." He calls it an honourable peace.

## What the mirror shows

Najime's colophon dates his tablet "in the 9th year of Nanzhizhim", but this is not the king who fought the war. That Nanzhizhim had died in AY 599, and a successor of the same name was reigning. A court that had now lost land in Khishkhih twice in a generation, under a new queen, wanted one clean victory and a peace it could call generous, and Najime supplied both. Navavun wrote for a king who had won, and he needed the war to look like a punishment, not an appetite.

The lesson for translators is about method. When rival tablets share a language, it is tempting to read their formulas as evidence of a shared tradition and to average them. These two should not be averaged. Each is complete only where the other is silent, and only the record joins them.
