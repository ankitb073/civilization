[[per:25062|Vunevyidun]], king of [[pol:1053|Veni]], priest of the sanctuaries by inheritance and by the anointing of your own hands, to his teacher, the eldest of the servants of [[mov:25|Myigudogüyshuye]], health and the long sleep of an untroubled conscience.

You taught me, when I was a boy and could not sit still at my letters, that a man should write hardest the letter he least wishes to send. I have begun this one four times. Let the fifth stand.

You know already what the council has done and what I have consented to. On the feast of the new moon I went up to the sanctuary, not with the old vessels but with empty hands, and before the people I acknowledged the one Power whom the followers of [[mov:51|Shenlamtanko]] proclaim, and the city did the same with a noise like the sea. I will not pretend it was imposed on me. The greater part of the city had gone over before ever the council sat; I counted the faces at the old sacrifices, and I could count them on my hands. The [[evt:25761|Conversion of Veni to Shenlamtanko]], as the clerks are already calling it in their registers, was the act of the city. I only signed it.

Why did they go? You will say: novelty, or the eloquence of strangers. I think it was the five years we have lived through.

Remember Shish. You blessed the standards yourself before the [[evt:25687|Battle of Shish]], and the field was lost, and by the [[evt:25688|Treaty of Shish]] we gave away Shish itself and the pastures beyond it, and opened our markets to those who had beaten us. The next summer was worse. At the [[evt:25690|Battle of Tungaruikhrimu]] the enemy came to our own walls, and in the [[evt:25691|Sack of Tungaruikhrimu]] they went through [[set:172|Tungarvikhrem]] street by street. You saw the sanctuary doors broken; you and I carried the old images to the cellar of the treasury with our own arms. Then came the [[evt:25692|Treaty of Tungaruikhrimu]], and the tribute that we pay each year, and will pay for many years yet.

Now a measure of grain costs nearly three [[cur:503|Yvenu]] in the market below the palace. The poor have done a sum, my teacher, and it is a simple one. They gave the old gods the first-fruits, and the old gods gave them Shish and the sack.

The teachers of the new way say that prosperity is a sign of the divine favour. I see you smile, for by that measure Veni is out of favour with every power in heaven, the new one included. I put this objection to one of their preachers myself. He answered that favour is not a wage paid in advance; that the one Power asks justice in dealings and moderation in all things, and that a city which has these will be prosperous in time, and a city which lacks them will not, whatever it burns upon its altars. I could not refute him. I am not sure I wished to.

And there is the doctrine which I know you will find hardest: that each seeker may find the truth by the inner light and by reason, without the mediation of an anointed order. You will say it makes every cobbler a priest. Perhaps. It also makes every cobbler answerable, at the final judgement which they teach, for what he has done with his own reason. There is a civic virtue in that which I cannot despise.

What do I ask of you? Nothing that touches your conscience. Keep the old images in the treasury cellar; I will not have them broken. Teach whom you will in your own house. But do not preach against the council in the market, for the city is raw, and I cannot afford a quarrel of altars on top of the quarrel of the tribute.

I remain, whatever the registers say, your pupil.

*Given at Tungarvikhrem, in the 35th year of Vunevyidun.*
