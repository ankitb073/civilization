title: Eleven Battles, One Sentence: Nafunde and the Last Kings of Dagafu

In the 460 years of the Kingdom of [[pol:1464|Dagafu]] (AY 3681–4141), no one led its armies into more battles than [[per:38700|Nafunde]]. The record gives him eleven, between 4111 and 4139; no other commander of the kingdom, king or general, has more than five. He won nine. He served four sovereigns, two of whom took the throne by force. Yet in the whole surviving archive his name occurs in a single sentence, and that sentence survives only because an annalist copied it 267 years later. Two of his kings set up stones for his victories, carved with their own names.

Of the man the record keeps almost nothing. He was born in AY 4085, a Khumbivi, in the reign of Queen Nashinzhè, and died a natural death in 4145, aged sixty. It names no parent, wife or child, and is silent on how he rose. Everything else is battles.

## A road through small states

In 4111, aged twenty-six, he led 62,869 men against the city-state of [[pol:1653|Dadathji]] at Kamvamvil, the field where Nashinzhè herself had beaten Dadavli in 4090, when he was five. Dadathji was barely a year old, and its ruler [[per:38662|Nangulu]] met him with 246 men. Dagafu lost 31, Dadathji 68. The next year King [[per:38553|Nabivi I]], the Conqueror, commanded at Daadadhji in person and took the city. Nangulu put his name to the conquest and was executed.

The defeated city's account (PAL·CH·06960) was written at Daadadhji in 4119 by the scribe Naumbèn, born, like Nafunde, in 4085. Of Kamvamvil it says: "because the omens had been neglected, our army withdrew in good order. 41 of ours were lost, but 107 of the enemy." It trims the city's dead and more than triples Dagafu's, and passes over the second battle. Naumbèn dated his tablet "in the 10th year of Dadathji", seven years after the record has the city annexed. The defeated kept their own calendar.

The record counts Nabivi's conversion of the kingdom to the I'ashakh faith in 4105 (PAL·DE·00605) among the causes of this war and the next, though in 4115 the kingdom returned to the [[mov:24|Uthungala]]. In the [[evt:39319|Third Dagafu–Nsinakhizi War]] Nafunde commanded three of the four battles. At Daadadhji in 4121, the year the Conqueror died, he had 121,383 men against 2,738 under the general [[per:38828|Nahimvi]]; at Davazhufu in 4122, against Nahimvi again, 123,997 against 5,646, more than half the men the kingdom counted under arms at the end of the 4110s. In 4125 he marched on the capital, Khambizhinu, where King [[per:38569|Natili]] waited with 3,054. Nsinakhizi lost 926 to Dagafu's 268, and its 36 districts were annexed by a treaty signed in the city.

Meanwhile the throne moved under him. Nabivi's twelve-year-old son reigned under a regent; in 4122 both the boy and his regent died. The record calls both deaths natural; I report no more. That year Nakhizhi seized the crown, and at Davazhufu he set up a rock relief (PAL·IN·02978): "Nakhizhi, king of Dagafu, defeated Ngzhinakhzi in Davazhufu. He killed 6,100 soldiers." The stone names no general. The record names Nafunde as commander, and gives the enemy 969 dead out of an army of 5,646, fewer men than the stele claims to have killed. Whether he fought that day for the regent or for the usurper, the record does not say.

The one sentence that names Nafunde comes from Nagième's *Dagafu account* of this war, a paper codex of 4132. The book is lost; the fragment survives because the *Annals of Dagafu, AY 4300–4399* (PAL·AN·00755) quote it: "Nafunde marched on Khambizhinu, where Nadili waited with 4,796 men." Even here the court historian made the enemy more than half as large again, and the sentence before it ("1,212 of them lay dead on the field, and of ours only 109") fits no battle of the war in the record. His own side remembered him by enlarging his enemies.

## Brothers at Gyi

In 4123 [[pol:1666|Khavu]] broke away under the claimant Nalame, with its capital at Kavu. The [[evt:39375|Second Dagafu Civil War]] set Khumbivi against Khumbivi: Nafunde and both generals who faced him were of that people. He commanded in all three of its battles, each time with fewer men. At the [[evt:39384|Battle of Gyi]] in 4124 he held 55,645 against 86,767; Khavu lost 7,259, Dagafu 5,237, and [[per:38841|Nadavu]], their general, died on the field. At Gyi again in 4125 he had 53,884 against [[per:38849|Nambave]]'s 69,795, and Khavu lost 21,182 to his 13,471. At Kavu in 4127, 81,889 against 86,173, Khavu lost 20,907 to his 6,049; Nambave was killed and the rebel capital fell. Every death the record counts in this war, 49,348 for Khavu and 24,757 for Dagafu, came in a battle Nafunde commanded.

Khavu's own account (PAL·CH·06981), written at Kavu in 4134 by another scribe named Nagième, tells a war of misfortunes rather than defeats. At Gyi, which he calls Gane, "because the river rose and cut the army in two, our men could not hold the field. We lost 2,325; the enemy, 14,108." At the second Gyi "the allies failed to come"; at Kavu the river rose again. In all three entries he cuts his own dead to about a third and multiplies Dagafu's by about two and two-thirds. He never names Nafunde, but honours his own: "There fell Nadavu, whose name shall be remembered." He ends: "The gods had turned their faces from us."

The peace was signed at Kavu by Nakhizhi the Usurper and by another Nakhizhi, Nalame's son, who had become king of Khavu at five under his mother's regency and was now seven. The terms returned 84 districts to Dagafu and granted an amnesty. The boy lived until 4185. Khavu's scribe, seven years on, still dated his tablet "in the 12th year of Khavu".

## The fields of Khambe

In 4128 Nambulame, aged twenty-nine, seized the throne; in 4133 the kingdom proclaimed itself an empire. In 4135 [[pol:1540|Fièvi]] attacked; a merchant's day-book kept at Dagambi that year (PAL·LE·02668) notes in its margin: "Caravans are held back on account of the Fièvi–Dagafu War." Nafunde, now past fifty, met [[per:38948|Naligizhi]] at the [[evt:39468|Battle of Khambe]] in 4136 with 113,260 men against 102,318 and was routed, losing 23,323 to 2,849. The next year at Khayagu, outnumbered nearly two to one, 56,138 against 105,944, he beat the Fièvi emperor himself, [[per:38632|Naguvu]], who lost 11,924 to Nafunde's 5,738. In 4138 he was back at Khambe with 99,486 against [[per:38953|Nakhumbèn]]'s 75,853, and lost worse: 30,963 dead to 5,281. In 4139 at Khambive, with 100,041 against Naligizhi's 86,198, he dealt the heaviest blow of his life: 37,907 Fièvi dead to 4,274 of his own.

The pattern is plain, if unexplained. Nafunde won all four battles in which he was outnumbered; both his defeats came with the larger army. Of the 89,829 Dagafu dead the record counts in his eleven battles, 54,286, three in every five, fell on the one field of Khambe.

The king claimed Khambive for himself. Nambulame's limestone stele there (PAL·IN·02983) reads: "Nambulame, great king, king of Dagafu, defeated Fièvi in Khambive. He killed 260,200 soldiers." That is three times the whole Fièvi army. In 4140 Nambulame commanded in person at Yibi and was beaten.

## After the kingdom

In 4141 the kingdom broke into six states. The record lists among the causes the secession of Khavu, both battles at Khambe, Yibi, and an old revolt of 4076 whose rebels had ruled from Khambive. The war with Fièvi ended without a settlement: there was no Dagafu left to settle it. The ground Nafunde had fought over became kingdoms: Luthe, ruled from Kavu, where he had ended the rebellion, and Fuvu, ruled from Khambive, where he had won his last battle. The old kingdom's last survey counted 210,810 men under arms, more than any survey of his lifetime; it did not fall for want of soldiers.

Nafunde outlived it by four years. Where he died, and under which crown, the record does not say.

Nearly two hundred thousand men died in the eleven battles he commanded, almost 110,000 of them on the other side. Kings who never named him took his victories, and their stones claim more dead than there were enemies in the field. His two great defeats helped bring the kingdom down. My verdict is mixed: a commander at his best with fewer men, and badly beaten twice when he had more. The record gives him back his eleven battles, losses included.
