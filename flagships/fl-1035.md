title: Only the Honey
---
The rains came in Nuw, as the priests had said they would, and the low fields were planted, and nine days after the first rain the dyke lane began to turn yellow.

Anatdath had been a midwife in [[set:77|Nodhinge]] for forty years, and she knew the lane below the dyke the way she knew the inside of her own mouth: which doors stuck, which dogs bit, which wives would scream and which would set their teeth and make no sound at all. Taanifkuth was a screamer. She screamed from the middle of the night until the first grey, while the rain drummed on the reed roof and ran in at the corner where the thatch was thin, and Anatdath knelt in the wet and talked to her in the low, bored voice she kept for frightened girls and nervous husbands and once, long ago, for a queen.

— Again. Good. Again. There's his head. He's a head on him like a melon.

— Is it a boy?

— It's a head. Wait.

It was a boy. He came out blue and furious and went red at once, and Anatdath laid him on his mother's belly and tied the cord with the waxed thread she kept wound round her left wrist, and cut it with the little bronze knife that had been her mother's. Then she sat back on her heels and listened to the rain and the child and, under them both, to the other sound in the house: the breathing behind the hanging mat, where Taanifkuth's husband had lain since the morning before.

She went in with the lamp when the girl was asleep. Tathtan lay on his side with his knees drawn up, hot as a hearthstone. When the light touched him he opened his eyes, and the whites of them were the colour of millet porridge, of old tallow, the colour three other men on the lane had turned that month before the priests came to sing over them.

— Is it a boy? he said.

— A boy. A big one.

— Good, he said, and shut his eyes again, as if that was what he had been waiting to be told.

Later, in books written a long way off, it would be called the [[evt:32668|Yellow Fever]]. That month in Nodhinge it had no name but its colour. People said it had come up out of the canal with the rains. People said it was in the new millet. The priests of the [[mov:58|Vishithmikh]] said nothing about where it came from and went from house to house singing the prayers of release, which are for the dying and not for the sick, and the lane had learned to shut its doors when it heard them.

Before she left, Anatdath crouched by Taanifkuth and woke her.

— Listen to me. On the third day he may go yellow. Most of them do, a little. In the face first, then down the chest. That's not the other thing. That's only the honey. You put him in the doorway in the morning sun with his clothes off, and you feed him, and it goes.

— How will I know it's the honey?

— He'll be cool, and he'll feed. The other one is hot.

The girl looked past her at the mat.

— And him?

Anatdath had nothing for that. She put her hand on the girl's hair a moment, and took up her bag.

* * *

The palace runner was waiting at her own door when she got home, soaked through, holding a lamp that had gone out.

— The queen, he said. Since midnight.

She did not go in to change. They went up through the city in the grey, the boy running ahead and coming back for her, past the canal steps, past the shut doors and the doors with bedding smoking on the step, past the king's granary with its bronze plaque beside the gate. Anatdath could not read, but she knew what the plaque said, because a priest had read it aloud to the whole quarter the day it was hung, seven years ago, and the children had chanted it in the street for a month after: "Pimtafbafin, king of Nodhinge, restored this granary in Nodhinge." There were more lines below. Nobody had learned those. Grain was a little under two [[cur:748|dufpu]] the measure that year, cheaper than in the dear years before, and the market under the granary wall was half empty all the same.

At the west gate the carters were loading under the arch for the road to [[set:89|Bakhriytil]], wool and oil for the towns of [[pol:1324|Yidhe]], the drivers cursing the rain and the mules. Anatdath did not give them a thought.

The steward met her in the passage of the women's court, a man with a clean beard who held a folded cloth over his mouth and nose.

— You've come from the lower city.

— I have.

— Have you been in a house with the yellow?

She could have lied. He would never have known, and he wanted her to; she could see it in the way he held the cloth.

— I've just come from one. I've been in every house in the dyke lane this month where there was a woman near her time. I'll wash, if you like. I'll burn this dress, if you give me another.

He began to say something, and from beyond the curtain a woman's voice, hoarse and very tired, said:

— Is that Anatdath? Let her in. Let her in, or I'll come out and fetch her.

* * *

They gave her a basin of hot water and a plain dress of the queen's women, and she washed to the elbows, and then to the shoulders, and went in.

Kutaalith was crouched on the birthing stool with a woman holding her under each arm. She was thirty-five years old. Anatdath had first put her hands on her at seventeen, a long thin girl married to the old king's son, and had brought out of her, that year, a boy and a girl, Kuldun and Nurkultuth, who had lived to run about the palace courts and quarrel and climb things they had been told not to climb, and had died, the girl at eight and the boy at nine, within a year of each other. The boy died in the same year as the old king, and the old king's son was made king, and in that year too the queen bore another boy, and they gave him his father's name, and he lived to be three.

Anatdath had been at all those births. She had not been at the burials. Midwives were not asked to the burials. It was nine years since the last child and six since the last burial, and the palace had long ago stopped hoping out loud.

— You took your time, said the queen.

— A girl in the dyke lane had a boy last night. I had to finish him.

— Is he well?

— Very loud.

— Good, said Kutaalith, and then the pain took her and she said nothing for a long while.

It went slowly, and then all at once, the way the late ones do. The rain stopped toward noon. In the outer room someone was walking, up and back, eleven paces each way; Anatdath counted them between pains to keep her own mind still. Once a man's voice asked something through the curtain, and one of the women answered, and the walking went on. A little after noon the queen gave a cry unlike her other cries, and Anatdath had the child in her two hands: a girl, slippery and grey and then pink, with one fist jammed against her mouth and a voice like a door hinge.

— Give her to me.

— Let me look at her first.

— Give her to me.

Anatdath gave her. The queen did not look at the child's face. She counted the fingers, and then the toes, and then she put her mouth down on the wet crown of the child's head and stayed like that, not moving, and the women looked away from her the way you look away from someone at prayer.

When the king came in he stood at the foot of the bed with his hands hanging and did not seem to know what to do with them. [[per:32350|Pimtafbafin]] was the same age as his wife. Anatdath had last seen him this close eighteen years before, a boy of seventeen walking another floor of this palace in the same way, eleven paces and back.

— A girl, he said.

— A girl, lord. A strong one.

He nodded slowly, several times, like a man agreeing to terms.

* * *

On the third morning the child was yellow.

The nurse saw it first, unwrapping her by the lamp before dawn: a tinge in the cheeks and the forehead and the creases of the neck. She made a sound that brought the women running, and one of them ran for the priest, who had slept in the passage since the lying-in began, and he came with his lamp and his book of prayers and had begun on the prayers of release before anyone could stop him.

It was the queen who stopped him. She sat up in the bed, grey to the lips.

— Not those. Not yet. Fetch Anatdath.

Anatdath had slept on a mat across the doorway, because the queen had asked her to stay. She came in and took the child from the nurse and unwrapped her altogether and turned her over in the grey light from the shutter. She put her lips to the forehead, as her own mother had taught her, and the back of her hand to the belly, and then she put her little finger in the child's mouth and felt her pull at it, hard and greedy.

— Is she hot? said the queen.

— No.

— She's yellow.

— She's yellow to here, and not below. Look at her feet. It's the honey, lady. Half of them have it, the third day. Nurkultuth had it.

The queen stared at her.

— Did she?

— Like a millet cake. You don't remember. You were seventeen and frightened of everything.

The king was in the doorway by then, in his undershirt, with his hair standing up on one side.

— How do you know it isn't the other? he said.

— I don't know it, lord. I've seen this one come and go a few hundred times. The other I've only known since the rains.

She took the child up to the roof, because the sun came there first. The king followed her up the stair and nobody stopped him. The city lay below them steaming after the rain, the canals full and brown, smoke going up straight from the lower town in a dozen places where people were burning bedding. Anatdath sat with her back to the warm parapet and laid the child naked across her knees, face to the east, and the king sat on the parapet beside her with his elbows on his knees and watched the yellow child in the yellow light.

— What is it like down there? he said.

She told him. Not all of it. The three men in the dyke lane, and Tathtan, and the priests singing, and the doors shutting when they sang.

— What do they say it is?

— They say it came up out of the canal. They say it's in the new millet. They say the One is punishing somebody for something, and they argue about who.

— And you?

— I'm a midwife, lord. I know where children come from and not much else.

He laughed, once, without much in it. Then he was quiet a long time, looking down at the roof of his own granary, and she did not know what he was thinking and did not ask.

* * *

They put the child in the sun every morning, and the yellow went down her the way water goes off a field: out of her face first, then her chest, then her belly, then off the soles of her feet. On the ninth day it was gone, and that evening the queen held her up in the lamplight and turned her this way and that, like a buyer at a cloth stall, and said her name for the first time.

— Kubilshiwath.

The women said it after her, one by one, to fix it. Anatdath said it too.

The queen paid her herself, out of a cloth bag, more silver than Anatdath earned in a year. In the passage, before she put it away, Anatdath tipped one coin into her palm and looked at it. On the face was the old king, the child's grandfather, with a veil over his head; on the back a man on a horse. She had never seen him either, except like this.

She went down through the city in the evening. The granary gate was shut. The carts had gone from under the west arch. In the dyke lane two more steps had black patches where bedding had been burned, and one of them was Taanifkuth's, and the mat that had hung across the inner room was gone.

The girl was sitting in her doorway with the boy across her knees, naked, his face turned to the west, where the sun was going down red and gold over the dyke and the drowned fields. She had been doing it in the mornings, she said. She had been doing it in the evenings too, in case.

Anatdath crouched and put her lips to the boy's forehead. Cool. She gave him her little finger and he took it like a dog takes a bone.

— He's well, she said. He's fat. Look at him.

— Look at me, said Taanifkuth.

Anatdath took the girl's face in her two hands and turned it to the light. The sun lay along the lane like oil. It was on the girl's cheeks and in the whites of her eyes, and on the boy, and on the walls, and on Anatdath's own hands where they held her; it was on the whole city, from the dyke to the palace roof.

— Well? said the girl.

— In this light, said Anatdath, everybody's yellow. Wait for the morning.

And she sat down on the step beside her to wait.
