title: An Awkward Victory Twice Told: The First Yizamashti Trade War

The [[evt:3485|First Yizamashti Trade War]] (AY 733–738) is a small war with an awkward record. [[pol:40|Navnga]] attacked the city-state of [[pol:144|Shidilnga]] over the trade of [[reg:1|Yizamashti]], won all four battles, and took six districts at the [[evt:3515|Treaty of Ashash (AY 738)]]. Yet the documentary consensus puts Navnga's dead at 89 and Shidilnga's at 218: an empire that fielded some twenty thousand men killed only about two and a half for each it lost, and at the last battle lost more than it killed. Two palace chronicles survive, both fragmentary: Lomagonen's Navnga account, written in AY 745 (PAL·CH·00543), and Shagun's Shidilnga account, written in AY 739 (PAL·CH·00544). My claim is that the awkwardness of the record shaped both. The cost of the victory was its most disputable fact, and the two scribes did opposite things with it: one halved the cost, the other kept it while shrinking his own dead, and each chose something different to hide.

## The Navnga account

Lomagonen keeps the frame of the war intact: four battles, four victories, Navnga triumphant. He gives them in order, the [[evt:3495|First Battle of Yizamashti]], the [[evt:3500|Battle of Ashash (AY 735)]], the [[evt:3503|Battle of Shidyun (AY 736)]] and the [[evt:3514|Battle of Ashash (AY 738)]], and he records no commander's death, in which the record agrees with him. He departs from it in three places.

First, blame. The record says Navnga began the war; Lomagonen says the king of Shidilnga "broke the peace and sent raiders across the border". Second, land: he claims nine districts against the record's six, and calls them land "which they had stolen", so that a trade war becomes the recovery of property. Third, the dead. He gives Navnga 47 and Shidilnga 549: about half the record's figure for his own side, and two and a half times its figure for the enemy.

What is remarkable is how far he stretches the record. In every one of his four entries the enemy's dead outnumber Navnga's several times over ("Of the enemy 156 were slain; of ours, 10"), even at Ashash in AY 738, where the record has Navnga losing 38 men to Shidilnga's 34. That last field can hardly have been forgotten in [[pol:40|Navnga]] only seven years on; Lomagonen did not explain it but reversed it. His own figures for the enemy's strength, which fall from 749 men to 527, leave a trace of the puzzle he buries: hosts the record puts at eighteen to twenty-six thousand needed four battles to subdue forces of a few hundred, and he makes the difficulty vanish rather than explain it. Nor, I should say, does the record, which gives us totals rather than causes.

## The Shidilnga account

Shagun wrote within a year of the treaty, under the king who had made it, and his strategy is the opposite. On Navnga's losses he is close to the record, closer than Navnga's own scribe: 84 against 89. A defeated court lost nothing by counting the enemy's dead carefully when it could shrink its own. Of his own side he admits 19, where the record gives 218, so that in his telling the victor buried more than four men for every one of Shidilnga's.

His more serious distortion is one of omission. Shagun records only two battles, at Shidilnga in AY 736 and at Ashash in AY 738, both lost "by treachery". The first two engagements are simply absent. The text is damaged elsewhere, and its gaps are marked, but nothing suggests a lacuna between the entries for AY 733 and 736, so I read the silence as Shagun's own. He ends the war in a draw, the king accepting "the pleas of Navunga", and the end of his account survives only as "[...] border was settled". Whatever stood in the gap, nothing in the surviving text concedes a district.

## Weighing the two

Set side by side, the chronicles show a pattern common in palace writing of this period: each scribe accepts the fact he cannot dispute and attacks the one he can. Lomagonen accepts the victories and disputes their cost and their cause. Shagun accepts the defeats, or two of them, and disputes the outcome. On who began the war, the defeated chronicle agrees with the record and the victorious one does not, which is the commoner arrangement. A victor needs a just cause more than a loser does.

The stele Engiruth raised at Ashash in AY 738 adds a third voice and a warning. It claims a round two hundred enemy dead in the last battle, where Lomagonen gives 86 and the record 34. The further a text stands from the reckoning of the field, the rounder its numbers grow.
