The [[evt:3485|First Yizamashti Trade War]] (AY 733–738) is a small war with an awkward record. [[pol:40|Navnga]] attacked the city-state of [[pol:144|Shidilnga]] over the trade of [[reg:1|Yizamashti]], won all four battles, and took six districts at the [[evt:3515|Treaty of Ashash (AY 738)]]. Yet the documentary consensus puts Navnga's dead at 7,709 and Shidilnga's at 201: the victor lost some thirty-eight men for every one it killed. Two palace chronicles survive, both fragmentary: Lomagonen's Navnga account, written in AY 745 (PAL·CH·00543), and Shagun's Shidilnga account, written in AY 739 (PAL·CH·00544). My claim is that the awkwardness of the record shaped both. Neither scribe could make the victory cheap, so each chose something different to hide.

## The Navnga account

Lomagonen keeps the frame of the war intact: four battles, four victories, Navnga triumphant. He gives them in order, the [[evt:3495|First Battle of Yizamashti]], the [[evt:3500|Battle of Ashash (AY 735)]], the [[evt:3503|Battle of Shidyun (AY 736)]] and the [[evt:3514|Battle of Ashash (AY 738)]], and he records no commander's death, in which the record agrees with him. He departs from it in three places.

First, blame. The record says Navnga began the war; Lomagonen says the king of Shidilnga "broke the peace and sent raiders across the border". Second, land: he claims nine districts against the record's six, and calls them land "which they had stolen", so that a trade war becomes the recovery of property. Third, the dead. He gives Navnga 4,025 and Shidilnga 508, roughly half the record's figure for his own side and two and a half times its figure for the enemy.

What is remarkable is what he does not change. In every one of his four entries Navnga's dead outnumber the enemy's ("of the enemy 68 were slain; of ours, 1,903"). A court scribe free to invent would not have written that. I take it as evidence that the cost of this war was too widely known in [[pol:40|Navnga]], even seven years on, to be denied; it could only be reduced. His own figures for the enemy's strength, which fall from 634 men to 305, sharpen the puzzle: an army that loses thousands to hosts of a few hundred has something to explain, and he does not try. Nor, I should say, does the record, which gives us totals rather than causes.

## The Shidilnga account

Shagun wrote within a year of the treaty, under the king who had made it, and his strategy is the opposite. On Navnga's losses he is close to the record, closer than Navnga's own scribe: 8,608 against 7,709. A defeated court had every reason to count the enemy's dead carefully when they were many. Of his own side he admits 17, where the record gives 201.

His more serious distortion is one of omission. Shagun records only two battles, at Shidilnga in AY 736 and at Ashash in AY 738, lost "by treachery" and "in a great storm". The first two engagements are simply absent. The text is damaged elsewhere, and its gaps are marked, but nothing suggests a lacuna between the entries for AY 733 and 736, so I read the silence as Shagun's own. He ends the war in a draw, the king "wearied of bloodshed", and his last sentence survives only as "[...] worth was lost". Whatever stood in the gap, nothing in the surviving text concedes a district.

## Weighing the two

Set side by side, the chronicles show a pattern common in palace writing of this period: each scribe accepts the fact he cannot dispute and attacks the one he can. Lomagonen accepts the high cost and disputes its size and its cause. Shagun accepts the defeats, or two of them, and disputes the outcome. On who began the war, the defeated chronicle agrees with the record and the victorious one does not, which is the commoner arrangement. A victor needs a just cause more than a loser does.

The stele Engiruth raised at Ashash in AY 738 adds a third voice and a warning. It claims a round hundred enemy dead in the last battle, where Lomagonen gives 68 and the record 27. The further a text stands from the reckoning of the field, the rounder its numbers grow.
