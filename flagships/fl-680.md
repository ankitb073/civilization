Of [[per:55229|Shirpinin]] the man, the record keeps almost nothing. He was born in AY 4594, a Jego subject of the [[pol:1785|Tallin]] Empire, in the last years of Buyuen the Conqueror. He became a scribe of the empire in AY 4614, at twenty, and served until his natural death in AY 4669, at seventy-five. No father, mother, wife or child is named; where he was born, how he learned his letters and what he believed, the record does not say. What survives is his work: seven documents, all complete, all kept in the State archive of Tallin. No scribe in the record has more (the next have six), and read slowly they show a working life inside an empire's paperwork.

## The roll and the chronicle

His earliest surviving paper is the Muster roll of Tallin for the Battle of Tinlamvukhnir (PAL·MU·01486), drawn up in AY 4620 for the general Nikhmaflen in the [[evt:44695|Second Tallin–Binge War]]. It lists eight towns, their gunners, dragoons and pikemen, to a total of 436,724, and ends: "Each man to bring bread for ten days." That total is exactly the strength the record gives Tallin's army in the battle. Binge brought 32,137. Tallin won, losing 5,336 men to Binge's 8,389.

Set beside it the historian Nathbitun's Tallin account of AY 4622 (PAL·CH·07881). He gives the enemy 54,469 men and 22,023 dead, his own side 2,545 dead, and says the peace returned "127 districts in all"; the treaty text (PAL·TR·02777), signed by [[per:44064|Nivlalin II]], names 66. Nathbitun is silent on the size of his own army, wisely: a host more than thirteen times its enemy's makes poor heroics. The Binge account (PAL·CH·07882) omits Tinlamvukhnir entirely. Of the three hands, only the scribe's counts true.

Yet his roll is not innocent. Its largest contingent, 116,231 gunners, is credited to Nivinga, the colony across the sea founded only in AY 4606, ahead of the 106,452 listed for the capital. The record's first count of Nivinga's people, labelled AY 4700, finds about twenty thousand. How the towns were made to answer for the total, the roll does not explain.

## Courts and harbours

The treaty reached into private lives, and Shirpinin recorded one. In AY 4624, at the court of Tungaruikhrimu, one of the seven towns Binge gave up, Khigen, son of Ununmaanin, asked for his father's 70 acres back. The Crown answered: "No private claim survives the treaty." Khigen kept the fields as a tenant of the crown, at 90.4 [[cur:1028|Nivbe]] a year. Twenty-nine years later at Khüdivi, ceded by Kunnga after the [[evt:44908|24th Tunnge Trade War]], Shirpinin wrote the same form almost word for word: Duweminten, son of Duwebalithin, 170 acres, a rent of 34.8 Nivbe. This time counsel said: "The fields were found empty and were given to settlers." The judgment left the plaintiff on them anyway. The record does not say what became of the settlers, or why the fields were empty.

One cause is near to hand. The Marsh Cough broke out at Igu in AY 4628 and ran until 4638; the record counts 29,651,255 dead across the places it struck, Khüdivi among them. Between the surveys of the 4610s and the 4620s, Tallin's people fell from 54.3 million to 35.3 million, though the Treaty of Binge had added 66 districts. A printed prayer of AY 4640 (PAL·SC·00132) recalls that the sickness first appeared at Igu "when ships came to us from the farther shore". The port at the other end of that first contact was Dyigini, a harbour of my own Khishkhis. The record does not say who carried it.

Shirpinin's papers never mention the cough. His port registers of Luluunul record the Silver Pilgrim from Togannge in AY 4635 (PAL·SM·00254), with gold, iron, salt and spices worth 2,669 Nivbe, and the Golden Maiden from Ushdhnge in AY 4648 (PAL·SM·00253), with gold, cloth, salt, oil and 507 measures of grain worth 5,582, the toll taken "at 6 in the hundred". All three ports were stricken, and the first register falls within the epidemic's years. Each ends: "Passengers: none." A historian of plague wants more; the scribe was there to take the toll.

His Almanac of Tallin for AY 4651 (PAL·AL·00495), a printed sheet from [[set:0|Taulin]], sets the feasts of the [[mov:58|Veshethmekh]] faith beside the work of the fields, and closes with the nearest thing in his work to an observation of his own: "The winters are long these years. Sow late and keep the herds near the stalls." The record bears him out: a cold spell ran from AY 4640 to 4689.

## The words for peace

His last surviving paper, from AY 4654, the year the [[evt:45003|Tallin–Ivun War]] ended, is the strangest: a Word list of Nunmula for scribes of Tallin (PAL·GL·00175), written on a potsherd in an age of print, for reasons the record does not give. It sets fourteen words of his own tongue beside the frontier people's, and is the only document in the archive whose text names him: "Collected by Shirpinin." The tongues are kin: king is lukhne in both, city nirinshal in both, father minnu and minu. But house and horse and wine had drifted apart, and so had enemy and peace. Among its "places across the border" stands Diviminnge, in Tallin's hands since AY 4585; the border he meant may have been one of speech.

He served fifteen more years, under Yivith the Great, and nothing more of his survives. He died in AY 4669, with Tallin at war again in the [[evt:45160|Tallin–Hiyikhzhidhe War]]; the muster roll for that year's Battle of Piyikhzhidhe is in another scribe's hand.

The chronicles of his age tell us how Tallin wished to be remembered. His papers tell us what it did: how many men it marched, what it charged a dispossessed farmer for his father's land, what a ship carried into a small harbour in a plague decade. He did not argue with power. He wrote down its figures, which is why, nearly three centuries on, we can trust him.
