To my mother and my sisters, greeting, from [[set:92|Dambakhu]] in [[reg:22|Dyimbi]], in the year 3099 of the Uthungala.

You will want to know first whether I am fed and whether I am warm. I am both. Grain is cheap here, under one [[cur:593|Ngjizhin]] the measure, and the quartermaster is an honest man by the standard of quartermasters. The barracks are dry. I stand on a wall, I look at fields, I come down from the wall. If this is soldiering, our uncle exaggerated.

But I must tell you about this town, because it has been in my head since we marched in, and there is no one here who wants to hear it.

Dambakhu is small. You could walk round its wall between breakfast and the noon bell and not be tired. It has one gate worth the name. And yet outside that gate, along the road we came in by, there is a long low mound covered in thistles, and the townspeople call it the Hill of the Luthe. Their own dead are under it, the men who held the gate and the folk of the sack. They call it ours because we made it.

The old men tell it this way. Forty years ago, in the [[evt:28665|Third Luthe–Tambakh War]], the emperor's general [[per:28242|Nashishi]] came against this little town with a host greater than the people of three provinces together. Inside the wall there were scarcely more men than make up one of our companies. And at the [[evt:28687|Battle of Dambakhu]] some sixty of theirs died before that wall, against perhaps a dozen of ours. The town was taken and sacked, and somehow not held. The next year it all happened again: [[per:27700|Najithu]], whom they called the Great, came with a host still larger, and at the second [[evt:28699|Battle of Dambakhu]] another thirty of theirs went under the thistles, against ten or so of ours. Then the town's lord set his seal to the [[evt:28701|Conquest of Tambakh]], and it was ours.

Mother, I have tried to understand it and I cannot. Some of the veterans' sons say the emperor wanted all of Dyimbi to watch, so that no other town would shut its gate. Others say the generals brought every man they had against that wall, because they had men and no patience. I do not know which is true. I only know who lies under the mound, and who put them there.

The teachers of the [[mov:24|Uthungala]] say that time runs in a line toward a final judgement, and that each of us will be weighed. I find I hope the generals are weighed first, and slowly. That is not a pious thought. I offer it to you, as I would not to the chaplain.

There is a better thought, too. The orators in Kavu say a city is worth what its citizens are worth. By that reckoning, this little town and its handful of defenders were worth a score or so of us, since that is what was paid. I do not believe it. I believe both sides were worth more than they were spent for.

Our elder brother will tell you that my war is easy compared with his. He is right. He was at Khathekhizi when the Kabave rebels were broken, and the [[evt:29074|Suppression of the Kabave Revolt]] gave us the peace I now stand guard over. Our emperor [[per:28566|Nagunjine]] is young, and they say he means to keep it. I hope so.

Send me warm wrappings for my feet. Send me news of the girls' betrothals. Do not send me cakes; the mice here are veterans too.

Your son, on the wall above the thistles.
