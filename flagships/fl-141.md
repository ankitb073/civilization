### Chapter the Fourth. The Year of the Charter

I was nineteen years old in the year 2525 of the [[mov:58|Vishithmikh]], and a compositor in the printing works on the harbour road in [[set:6|Zhinontunu]], where we set the shipping lists by night and the government gazette by day. I mention the gazette because it was the gazette that taught me politics. A man who sets in type, letter by letter, the decrees of an absolute crown learns very quickly how few of them are about anything but money.

Let me say at once, for the benefit of younger readers who have grown up under the Charter and think it fell from the sky, that it did not. It was got by work, and by fear, and by a great many people standing in the cold.

The trouble had begun long before. Every child of [[pol:1786|Zhinontunu]] knew the story of the [[evt:45990|usurpation of Nathpimtin]] in 2504, when the old king died and the seat was taken in a single night, with the guards bought and the council locked out. My father, who was a docker, used to say that the crown had been stolen once and could be stolen again, and that the only cure for a stolen crown was a written one. When Nathpimtin died and his daughter [[per:45359|Taadheth]] came to the throne in 2512, she inherited his ministers, his debts, and his reputation.

The debts were the thing. Between 2512 and 2525 the [[cur:1057|Lodhu]], which is paper and promises silver, bought a little less every year by the reckoning of the merchants' exchange; slowly, but without ever once turning back. A docker's wage did not rise to meet it. I set those exchange tables with my own hands every week. The figures came off the telegraph in the evening, went into the forme by midnight, and were on the breakfast tables of the shipowners by six. We, who set them, could read them before anyone.

The [[evt:46206|revolution of 2525]], as the schoolbooks now call it, was not one day but forty. It began with the dockers, who struck when the harbour dues were raised for the third time in a year. The railwaymen came out a week later; then the gas workers; then, to everyone's astonishment, the junior clergy, who preached from the steps of the cathedral that a nation whose faith commands compassion for all peoples could not long refuse justice to its own. The printers came out on the eleventh day. I remember pulling the last proof of the gazette, a decree forbidding public meetings, and walking out with it under my coat. We pasted it up on the harbour wall that night beside our own placards, so that people could compare the two.

I will not pretend that it was orderly. There were charges on the harbour road. There were windows broken that I wish had not been broken. But I saw no one killed with my own eyes, and I have never believed half of what the old ministry's newspapers printed about us.

What I wish to record, because the young forget it, is this: we did not ask for the queen's head. We did not ask for a republic. We asked for a Charter, a written crown, with an assembly to vote the taxes and a court that the ministers could not lock out. On the fortieth day the queen received a deputation of six, of whom I was the youngest, in the long gallery of the palace. She was very pale and very civil. She asked us whether we were believers. We said we were. She said: "Then you know that power is a loan, and the One will ask for it back." Within the week the Charter was proclaimed, and the gazette, which I set myself, announced it in a type two sizes larger than any decree before it.

Fourteen years have passed. The queen still reigns, and reigns well, and the assembly still sits. I have set its debates in type every session since. They are much longer than the decrees, and much less dull.

*Zhinontunu, 26 Pivu 2539.*
